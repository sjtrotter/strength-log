# Concurrent (strength + endurance) training, minimum effective doses, and public-health activity guidelines

Scope note for the report writer: evidence grades use four levels. **Strong** means consistent meta-analytic or high-certainty guideline evidence. **Moderate** means one good meta-analysis, or several that mostly agree. **Weak/contested** means meta-analyses conflict, a single subgroup carries the result, or the populations are narrow. **Preference** means no outcome difference, so the user's choice decides. Where I could only reach an abstract or a secondary summary, the finding says so. The full text was read for Schumann 2022 and for WHO 2020 (Bull et al.). The other sources come from abstracts, PubMed records, or search snippets.

## 1. Interference effect: how much does cardio cost strength, hypertrophy and power, and does training status matter?

### Takeaway
At recreational volumes, concurrent training does not measurably reduce whole-muscle hypertrophy or maximal strength (strong-to-moderate evidence). It does reduce explosive strength and power by a small amount (SMD about -0.28; moderate evidence). The power cost is mostly a same-session effect. A small lower-body maximal-strength penalty appears in already-trained people (ES -0.35) and in men, and again mainly when both modes share a session (weak/contested: subgroup findings that Schumann 2022 did not reproduce). "Cardio kills gains" is folklore at the doses an app user is likely to do.

### Cited Findings
- **Schumann et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52:601–612), 43 studies, healthy adults of any age or sex, ≥4 weeks of supervised training, compared with an identical strength-only prescription.** Concurrent vs strength alone: maximal strength SMD -0.06 (95% CI -0.20 to 0.09, p=0.446); explosive strength SMD -0.28 (95% CI -0.48 to -0.08, p=0.007); hypertrophy SMD -0.01 (95% CI -0.16 to 0.18, p=0.919). None of these moderators changed the results: aerobic type (cycling vs running), frequency (>5 vs <5 weekly sessions), training status (untrained vs active), age (<40 vs >40). — [Schumann 2022, doi:10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7)
- Schumann 2022 conclusion: concurrent training "does not compromise muscle hypertrophy and maximal strength development. However, explosive strength gains may be attenuated, especially when aerobic and strength training are performed in the same session." The authors add that "the overall risk of interference effects is rather low. Therefore, most individuals, including recreational athletes, can enjoy complementary benefits." — [Schumann 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7)
- Schumann 2022 limitations: mean PEDro score 4.3 ± 0.9 (medium quality), with seven low-quality studies. Aerobic intensity was not analyzed because studies reported it inconsistently. "Active" participants were not split into moderately vs well-trained, which "may have diluted potential significant effects." The introduction notes that earlier work found maximal-strength interference "when the overall training volume is high," none when volume was cut to two aerobic plus two strength sessions a week, and that even low aerobic volumes reduced rapid force production. — [Schumann 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7)
- **Wilson et al. 2012 (JSCR 26(8):2293–2307), 21 studies, 422 effect sizes.** Concurrent training lowered hypertrophy, strength and power relative to strength alone. Hypertrophy ES: strength-only 1.23, concurrent 0.85, endurance-only 0.27. Endurance frequency and duration correlated negatively with hypertrophy, strength and power. Peak power was attenuated. — [Wilson 2012, PubMed 22002517](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22002517/)
- Schumann 2022 explains why it disagrees with Wilson: Wilson included fiber-level hypertrophy and studies without a strength-only control, and estimated per-group ESs (72 for hypertrophy) instead of using a pre-post-control random-effects model. — [Schumann 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7)
- **Petré et al. 2021 (Sports Med 51:991–1010), 27 studies, lower-body 1RM.** Leg press and squat 1RM were blunted by concurrent training in trained individuals (ES -0.35, p<0.01). The effect was not significant in moderately trained (ES -0.20, p=0.08) or untrained individuals (ES 0.03, p=0.87). In trained individuals the penalty appeared only when both modes were done in the same session. The authors' advice: trained people who prioritize maximal strength "should therefore consider separating endurance from resistance training." — [Petré 2021, doi:10.1007/s40279-021-01426-9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8053170)
- Schumann 2022 on Petré: Petré included multi-arm studies against a single comparator more than once, "potentially inflating power." Schumann's own training-status subgroup "showed a similar direction" without reaching significance. — [Schumann 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7)
- **Lundberg et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52:2391–2403), 15 studies, fiber-level hypertrophy.** Overall concurrent effect SMD -0.23 (95% CI -0.46 to -0.00, p=0.050); type I fibers -0.34 (p=0.078); type II -0.13 (p=0.315). Type I fiber growth was blunted when the aerobic mode was running (SMD -0.81, 95% CI -1.26 to -0.36) but not cycling. The authors call this preliminary. — [Lundberg 2022 (abstract via AMiner)](https://www.aminer.org/pub/63389ee190e50fcafd8a0b77/the-effects-of-concurrent-aerobic-and-strength-training-on-muscle-fiber-hypertrophy); [record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4077385?lng=en)
- **Huiberts, Wüst & van der Zwaard 2024 (Sports Med 54:485–503), 59 studies, 1,346 participants aged 18–50.** Concurrent training blunted lower-body strength gains in males but not females. Upper-body strength, power and VO2max showed no sex difference. VO2max gains were impaired in untrained people but not in trained or highly trained endurance athletes. Other outcomes showed no training-status difference. Hypertrophy data were insufficient. — [Huiberts 2024, doi:10.1007/s40279-023-01943-9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933151/)
- **Sabag et al. 2018 (J Sports Sci), concurrent HIIT plus resistance training.** Lower-body strength gains were smaller than with resistance training alone (ES -0.248, p=0.049). Hypertrophy and upper-body strength were similar. The authors suggest running-based HIIT and longer rest between modes may reduce the penalty. — [Sabag 2018, PubMed 29658408](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29658408/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default (moderate):** for general-fitness, hypertrophy and "hybrid" goals, add cardio freely at public-health doses (2.5–5 h/wk moderate, see Q6) without lowering strength volume or projected strength/size progress. The pooled evidence shows no hypertrophy or maximal-strength cost at these doses.
- **Generator default (weak/contested):** when the user is already strength-trained AND their primary goal is maximal strength (e.g., a powerlifting-style 1RM goal), keep hard lower-body cardio out of the lifting session (see Q2 spacing). Petré found the trained-only penalty only in same-session designs. Males showed a lower-body penalty in Huiberts, so this applies at least to men; the data cannot rule it in or out for trained women.
- **Generator default (moderate):** when the goal is power or explosiveness (jumping, sprinting, sport power), separate cardio from power work by at least 3 h or schedule it on another day. This is the most robust interference signal in the 2022 data.
- Untrained users can be told plainly that cardio won't cost them strength or muscle. In the only training-status split that reached significance (Petré), untrained ES was 0.03. Huiberts suggests the reverse cost for beginners: lifting slightly blunts VO2max gains in the untrained. This is minor for health goals.
- **Ask the user:** primary goal (strength / size / power / endurance / general health); years of consistent lifting (trained vs untrained); weekly cardio they already do or plan to do (sessions, minutes, mode). Sex matters only weakly and can be inferred from an existing profile field if one exists. It is not worth a new question.
- **Folklore check, "cardio kills gains":** not supported for hypertrophy or maximal strength at typical doses (Schumann SMDs -0.06 and -0.01). The claim is partly true only for (a) explosive power, (b) trained lifters doing same-session lower-body cardio, and (c) very high endurance volumes, which Wilson's frequency/duration correlations and the high-volume studies Schumann cites point to. The data contain no defensible "X minutes of cardio and your gains stop" threshold.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis quantifies a dose threshold (minutes/week of cardio) at which hypertrophy or strength starts to suffer. Wilson's frequency/duration correlations are the closest, and Schumann did not reproduce them. A generator should not encode a hard cardio cap.
- Highly strength-trained athletes and trained women are underrepresented (Huiberts and Petré both flag this).
- Aerobic intensity (HIIT vs moderate steady state) as a moderator is unresolved. Schumann could not analyze it. Sabag found a small HIIT penalty for the lower body.
- I did not access full texts for Petré, Lundberg, Huiberts or Sabag, so their subgroup study counts are unverified.

## 2. Ordering within a session, and same-session vs same-day vs separate-day spacing

### Takeaway
If both modes share a session, lifting first gives modestly better lower-body dynamic strength: about +7% (Eddens) or about +4 kg lower-body 1RM (Murlasits). VO2max and hypertrophy are unaffected by order (moderate evidence). For power, distance between the modes matters more than their order. Separating them by at least 3 h removed the explosive-strength penalty in Schumann 2022 (moderate). "Never lift after running" overstates a small effect.

### Cited Findings
- **Eddens et al. 2018 (Sports Med 48(1):177–188), 10 studies.** Resistance-before-endurance improved lower-body dynamic strength: weighted mean difference 6.91% (95% CI 1.96 to 11.87, p=0.006). Static strength showed no order effect (WMD -0.04%, 95% CI -3.19 to 3.11). Hypertrophy, VO2max and body fat % also showed no order effect. — [Eddens 2018, PubMed 28917030](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28917030/)
- **Murlasits, Kneffel & Thalib 2018 (J Sports Sci 36(11):1212–1219).** Lower-body 1RM was higher with strength first (pooled mean change 3.96 kg, 95% CI 0.81 to 7.10). VO2max was unaffected by order (0.39 ml/kg/min, 95% CI -1.03 to 1.81). — [Murlasits 2018, PubMed 28783467](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28783467/)
- **Schumann 2022:** explosive strength was attenuated more within the same session (p=0.043) than when sessions were ≥3 h apart (p>0.05). The pooled analysis "did not provide evidence for an order-specific effect but rather highlights that combining aerobic and strength training in close proximity attenuates adaptations in explosive strength regardless of exercise order." The Key Points report no interference for maximal strength or hypertrophy across "same session vs. same day vs. different day training." — [Schumann 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7)
- **Petré 2021:** the lower-body 1RM penalty in trained individuals occurred only when both modes were done in the same session. — [Petré 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8053170)
- **Sabag 2018:** longer rest between modes was suggested as a way to soften the lower-body strength penalty from HIIT. — [Sabag 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29658408/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default (moderate):** in a combined session, put lifting first and cardio second unless the user's primary goal is endurance. For an endurance-first user, put the key run or ride first and lift after. Murlasits found order doesn't change VO2max, so the cost of either choice falls on the user's secondary goal.
- **Generator default (moderate, for power or trained-strength goals):** prefer different days, or the same day ≥3 h apart. The ≥3 h number comes directly from Schumann's subgroup cut-point. It is a tested threshold, not a demonstrated optimum.
- **Generator default (preference, for general/health users):** same-session is fine. Scheduling convenience beats the small order effect, because adherence matters far more than a few kilograms on a lower-body 1RM.
- If a user insists on cardio first, keep it light/moderate, or upper-body-focused lifting that day. This is a weak inference: Eddens and Murlasits measured lower-body outcomes.
- **Ask the user:** "Do you usually do cardio and lifting in the same visit, on the same day at different times, or on separate days?" and "Which matters more to you: getting stronger or getting fitter for cardio?" (to set within-session order).
- **Folklore check, "never lift after running":** order effects exist but are modest and confined to lower-body dynamic strength. Running first does not "cancel" a lifting session.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis found tested intervals between 3 h and 24 h (e.g., 6 h vs 24 h), so the "best gap" is unknown beyond ">3 h is better than same session for power."
- No source found isolated whether upper-body lifting after running carries any penalty.

## 3. Modality (running vs cycling vs HIIT), and frequency/duration thresholds

### Takeaway
The evidence on modality conflicts. Wilson 2012 found that running, not cycling, hurt hypertrophy and strength. Lundberg 2022 found that running blunted type I fiber growth. Schumann 2022 found no modality effect on whole-muscle outcomes. Sabag 2018 found that cycling HIIT trended worse than running HIIT. Grade: weak/contested. Modality should be a user preference, with a mild tilt toward cycling or other low-impact modes for lifters running high volumes. No validated minutes-per-week threshold exists.

### Cited Findings
- Wilson 2012: resistance training combined with running, but not cycling, gave significant decrements in hypertrophy and strength. Frequency correlated negatively with outcomes (r about -0.26 to -0.35), and so did duration (r about -0.29 to -0.75). — [Wilson 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22002517/) (correlation figures are from a search-result transcription of the abstract and should be checked against the paper)
- A secondary source says Wilson's running-vs-cycling difference vanished when one outlier study was excluded. — search-result summary of [Wilson 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22002517/); I could not verify this against the paper, so treat it as unconfirmed
- Schumann 2022: no significant moderation by aerobic type (cycling vs running) or by frequency (>5 vs <5 weekly concurrent sessions). — [Schumann 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7)
- Lundberg 2022: running, but not cycling, blunted type I fiber hypertrophy (SMD -0.81); called "preliminary." — [Lundberg 2022](https://www.aminer.org/pub/63389ee190e50fcafd8a0b77/the-effects-of-concurrent-aerobic-and-strength-training-on-muscle-fiber-hypertrophy)
- Sabag 2018: concurrent HIIT reduced lower-body strength gains (ES -0.248). Summaries of the sub-analyses report that the cycling-HIIT penalty trended larger (ES -0.377, p=0.074) than the running-HIIT penalty (ES -0.176, p=0.261). The authors suggest running-based HIIT to reduce the penalty. The subgroup figures differ between the conference and journal versions. — [Sabag 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29658408/)
- A 2024 network meta-analysis reportedly found that high-intensity interval running minimized adverse effects on lower-limb strength and muscle cross-sectional area. — search-result summary citing [PMC10767279](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10767279) (I did not verify the full text)

### Inferences
- **Generator default (preference):** let the user pick their cardio mode. Do not steer them away from running on interference grounds. The pooled whole-muscle meta-analysis (Schumann) found no modality effect, and the older and fiber-level signals point in different directions.
- **Generator default (weak):** if a strength- or size-focused user wants a lot of conditioning (e.g., >5 sessions/week), suggest cycling, rowing or other low-impact options for the extra volume, and schedule the runs away from leg days. Cumulative leg fatigue and the impact load of running are plausible practical reasons, but no meta-analysis isolates them.
- **Generator default:** do not encode a hard "max cardio minutes" before lifting progress is downgraded. If the app needs a soft warning, tie it to the regime where interference was observed (high frequency/duration, Wilson) and phrase it as a caution, not a rule.
- **Ask the user:** preferred cardio modes (run / bike / row / swim / walk / other) and any impact limits (joint pain, injury history).

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis gives a dose threshold (sessions or minutes) beyond which concurrent cardio measurably reduces strength or size in recreational lifters.
- I found no meta-analytic data on rowing, swimming or elliptical as concurrent modes.

## 4. Effect of strength training on endurance performance

### Takeaway
For runners and other endurance athletes, adding strength training improves running economy by roughly 2–8%, and time-trial and sprint performance tend to improve as well. VO2max and body mass are typically unchanged (moderate evidence: two systematic reviews of mostly small, mid-quality trials). Heavy loads and plyometrics outperform light-load or isometric work for economy. The fear that lifting will make endurance athletes "bulky and slow" is not supported.

### Cited Findings
- **Blagrove, Howatson & Hayes 2018 (Sports Med 48(5):1117–1149), 24 studies of middle- and long-distance runners with ≥6 months' experience, compared with run-only controls.** PEDro quality scores were 4–6. Running economy (20 studies) "generally showed improvements (2–8%) compared to a control group, although this was not always the case." Time-trial (1.5–10 km) and anaerobic speed qualities tended to improve. VO2max, vVO2max, blood lactate and body composition were typically unaffected. — [Blagrove 2018, doi:10.1007/s40279-017-0835-7](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Effects_of_strength_training_on_the_physiological_determinants_of_middle-_and_long-distance_running_performance_a_systematic_review/9619553)
- **Beattie et al. 2014 (Sports Med 44(6):845–865), 26 studies of competitive endurance athletes.** Strength training improved time-trial performance, economy, VO2max/peak power and maximal anaerobic running velocity. The authors recommend adding strength training and note that long interventions (>6 months) are probably needed for transfer to performance. — [Beattie 2014, PubMed 24532151](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24532151/)
- **2023 Sports Medicine meta-analysis on running economy across speeds:** high-load strength training, plyometrics and combined methods may improve running economy. Submaximal-load and isometric training appear less effective. — [doi:10.1007/s40279-023-01978-y (Essex repository PDF)](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/37272/1/s40279-023-01978-y.pdf)
- **Huiberts 2024:** trained and highly trained endurance athletes did not lose VO2max gains when lifting was added. Untrained people did. — [Huiberts 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933151/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default (moderate):** for endurance-primary users, schedule 2 strength sessions/week. Bias them toward heavy compound lower-body lifts and some plyometrics, not light high-rep "endurance" circuits. The 2 sessions/week figure is an inference from the typical study designs in these reviews, not a tested dose-response; the reviews' exact frequency ranges were not verified in this research.
- For runners, place lifting after the key run on the same day, or on easy-run days. This keeps the hard run fresh (inference from Q2: order serves the primary goal).
- **Ask the user:** whether they have an endurance event or performance goal (e.g., 5k/10k/half time) and its date. This decides whether endurance or strength is primary.
- **Folklore check, "lifting makes runners slow or heavy":** contradicted. Body composition and VO2max were typically unchanged, and economy and time trials improved.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the training frequency, load ranges or durations within these reviews' included studies, so "2x/week heavy" is an inference.
- No source found covered strength training's effect on cycling, rowing or swimming specifically. Beattie's population is mixed endurance athletes.

## 5. Minimum effective dose: strength, hypertrophy, VO2max

### Takeaway
Strength: in trained men, one hard set of 6–12 reps at about 70–85% 1RM, 2–3 times a week per lift, still produces significant 1RM gains, though suboptimal ones (moderate). For powerlifters, about 3–6 heavy working sets of 1–5 reps per lift per week across 1–3 sessions is the suggested minimum (weak: mixed-methods, partly expert-opinion). Hypertrophy: dose-response is graded, with more sets giving more growth and diminishing returns. Low volumes still work, but the data do not pin a precise floor (moderate for the dose-response shape, weak for any floor). VO2max: 3 × 10-minute sprint-interval sessions a week (1 min of hard work per session) improved VO2peak about as much as 3 × 50 min of moderate cycling in sedentary men (moderate: one strong RCT plus supporting meta-analyses).

### Cited Findings
- **Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2020 (Sports Med 50(4):751–765; online 2019), systematic review, resistance-trained men.** "performing a single set of 6-12 repetitions with loads ranging from approximately 70-85% 1RM 2-3 times per week with high intensity of effort is the threshold." A later citing paper describes the effect as "suboptimal, though still significant" for squat and bench press. — [Androulakis-Korakakis 2020, doi:10.1007/s40279-019-01236-0](https://read.qxmd.com/read/31797219/the-minimum-effective-training-dose-required-to-increase-1rm-strength-in-resistance-trained-men-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis); [PMC8449772 (citing paper)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/8449772)
- Limits noted in secondary summaries: thin data for deadlift 1RM, trained women and highly strength-trained athletes. — [summary via search results; see original](https://read.qxmd.com/read/31797219/the-minimum-effective-training-dose-required-to-increase-1rm-strength-in-resistance-trained-men-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis)
- **Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021 (Front Sports Act Living 3:713655), powerlifters and coaches, five mixed-methods studies.** Suggested minimum is about 3–6 working sets of 1–5 reps per lift per week, >80% 1RM, RPE about 7.5–9.5, across 1–3 sessions. Single autoregulated singles at RPE 9–9.5 are an even smaller option, but gains are "less likely to be meaningful." Adding 2–3 back-off sets at about 80% of the single's load "may produce greater gains over 6 weeks." — [Androulakis-Korakakis 2021, doi:10.3389/fspor.2021.713655](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/text)
- A secondary review says the minimum-dose approach is not optimal for long-term development, but it can maintain or improve strength over short cycles with minimal time and fatigue. — [MTNTactical research review (secondary)](https://mtntactical.com/knowledge/research-review-minimum-training-dose-for-1rm-strength-improvement-in-trained-lifters/)
- **Hermann et al. (single-set training):** training a single set to failure did not improve strength more than stopping at 2 reps in reserve. — [Hermann et al., RGU repository](https://rgu-repository.worktribe.com/output/2788957/without-fail-muscular-adaptations-in-single-set-resistance-training-performed-to-failure-or-with-repetitions-in-reserve) (from a search snippet only; full results unverified)
- **Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017 (J Sports Sci), 15 studies, 34 treatment groups.** Treating weekly sets as a continuous variable gave a significant dose-response for hypertrophy (p=0.002). The categorical comparison (<5 / 5–9 / ≥10 sets per muscle per week) was only a trend (p=0.074). Data were insufficient to say whether >10 sets adds more. — [Schoenfeld 2017 (author PDF)](https://researchgate.net/profile/Brad-Schoenfeld/publication/305455324_Dose-response_relationship_between_weekly_resistance_training_volume_and_increases_in_muscle_mass_A_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis/links/59dc0269458515e9ab4527d6/Dose-response-relationship-between-weekly-resistance-training-volume-and-increases-in-muscle-mass-A-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis.pdf)
- **Pelland et al. (SportRxiv 2024; reportedly published in Sports Med 2026), 67 studies, 2,058 participants, multilevel meta-regressions.** Both hypertrophy and strength rise with weekly set volume with diminishing returns, and the returns diminish more sharply for strength. Frequency had a clearly positive effect on strength even after adjusting for volume. For hypertrophy the frequency effect was compatible with negligible. "Fractional" set counting (indirect sets = 0.5) fit best. — [Pelland preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908); a 2026 critique disputes the frequency modeling: [Preprints.org 202610.1022](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202610.1022) (not peer reviewed). Effect sizes were taken from secondary summaries, not the paper.
- **Gillen et al. 2016 (PLoS One 11(4)), sedentary men, 12 weeks, 3 sessions/week.** Sprint interval training (3 × 20 s all-out cycle sprints at about 500 W, inside a 10-min session with warm-up and cool-down) compared with moderate continuous training (50 min/session) and controls. VO2peak rose about 19% in both training groups. Insulin sensitivity and muscle mitochondrial content improved similarly, "despite a five-fold lower exercise volume and time commitment." Small n (SIT 9, MICT 10, control 6). — [Gillen 2016, PMC4846072](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4846072)
- **Weston et al. 2014 (Sports Med), 32 trials of low-volume HIT.** A mean protocol of 13 sessions produced a "likely moderate" VO2max improvement of 6.2% (90% CL ±3.1%) in active non-athletic males vs control. Gains were larger in sedentary people and unclear in athletes. — [Weston 2014, PubMed 24743927](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24743927/)
- **Sloth et al. 2013 (Scand J Med Sci Sports), 19 SIT studies of 2–8 weeks.** VO2max effect size g=0.63, with increases of about 4–13%. — [Sloth 2013, PubMed 23889316](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23889316/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default for a "minimum" or time-crunched plan (moderate):** 2 full-body sessions/week. Give each main lift or muscle group 1–3 hard sets of about 6–12 reps (about 70–85% 1RM). Stop near failure (0–2 RIR), not necessarily at it (Hermann, weak). Sessions of about 20–30 min are realistic at this dose. 3 sessions/week is better when the user can spare them, because frequency independently helps strength (Pelland).
- **For time-crunched strength-sport users (weak):** 3–6 heavy sets of 1–5 reps per competition lift per week, plus optional back-off sets.
- **Hypertrophy-focused users with little time (moderate on direction, weak on floor):** start at a low weekly set count per muscle and offer more sets as time allows. More sets give more growth, with diminishing returns. Because frequency barely matters for hypertrophy, the generator can concentrate sets into fewer sessions for size goals without penalty.
- **Generator default for minimum-dose cardio (moderate):** 2–3 short interval sessions/week (e.g., 3 × 20 s hard sprints within 10 min, or similar low-volume HIIT) for users with little time. Offer moderate continuous cardio for users who prefer it or for whom all-out efforts are inappropriate (see Q7 for older adults and beginners). The two modes produced equivalent VO2peak gains in Gillen.
- **Ask the user:** minutes available per session and sessions per week (the two inputs that matter most), and whether they're comfortable with all-out effort (sprints) or prefer steady work.
- **Folklore check, "you need 3 hours a week to see results":** contradicted. Single-set 2–3×/week protocols increase 1RM in trained men, about 30 min/week of SIT matched 150 min/week of moderate training for VO2peak in sedentary men, and the strength-mortality optimum is 30–60 min/week (Q6).
- **Folklore check, "you must train to failure":** not supported for strength in the single-set comparison (Hermann; weak, single study).

### Gaps
- I found no validated per-muscle weekly set floor for hypertrophy. Schoenfeld's categories were not significant, and I could not verify Pelland's exact curve values.
- Androulakis-Korakakis 2020 covers trained men. I found no equivalent minimum-dose review for untrained people, women or older adults. Beginners likely respond to even less (general consensus, not verified here).
- Gillen covered sedentary men only (n≈9 per arm). The equivalence has not been shown in women, older adults or trained people in that trial.
- I found no meta-analysis newer than 2014 on low-volume HIIT and VO2max in this session (a 2022 Sports Med review appeared only as a reference).

## 6. Public-health guidelines (WHO 2020, US PAG 2018, ACSM) and the "weekend warrior" evidence

### Takeaway
The WHO 2020 and US 2018 guidelines agree: 150–300 min/week moderate or 75–150 min/week vigorous aerobic activity, plus muscle-strengthening of all major muscle groups on ≥2 days/week. Bouts of any length count, and some activity is better than none. These are strong recommendations on moderate-certainty evidence. Strength training's mortality benefit peaks around 30–60 min/week in cohort data (moderate, observational). Packing the weekly volume into 1–2 days ("weekend warrior") gives similar mortality benefit to spreading it out (moderate, observational).

### Cited Findings
- **WHO 2020 (Bull et al., BJSM 54:1451–1462), adults 18–64.** "at least 150–300 min of moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity, or at least 75–150 min of vigorous-intensity aerobic physical activity, or an equivalent combination... throughout the week" (strong recommendation). "muscle-strengthening activities at moderate or greater intensity that involve all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week" (strong recommendation, moderate-certainty evidence). More than 300 min moderate or 150 min vigorous gives additional benefit (conditional recommendation). — [WHO 2020 / Bull 2020, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955)
- WHO 2020: "some physical activity is better than none". "MVPA bouts of any duration now count towards these recommendations," replacing the 2010 requirement of ≥10-min bouts. Dose-response with mortality and CVD is curvilinear (moderate certainty). "There was no evidence to support a dose-response association with higher volumes of muscle-strengthening activities." — [Bull 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955)
- WHO 2020 states the 150–300 range "captures the maximal risk reductions" and that going beyond it "does not appreciably further decrease the risk of major outcomes such as all-cause or CVD mortality." — [Bull 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955)
- **US Physical Activity Guidelines, 2nd ed. (Piercy et al., JAMA 2018;320(19):2020–2028).** Adults should do muscle-strengthening activities on 2 or more days a week. Older adults should do multicomponent activity that includes balance training alongside aerobic and muscle-strengthening activity. Physical activity reduces fall-related injury risk in older adults. — [Piercy 2018, doi:10.1001/jama.2018.14854](https://www.qxmd.com/r/30418471); [Harvard Nutrition Source summary](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/2018/11/13/new-physical-activity-guidelines-for-americans/)
- **Momma et al. 2022 (BJSM), 16 prospective cohort studies.** The association between muscle-strengthening activity and mortality is J-shaped, with maximum risk reduction at about 30–60 min/week for all-cause mortality, CVD and total cancer. For diabetes, risk fell sharply up to 60 min/week and then gradually. All-cause RR reportedly 0.85 (95% CI 0.79–0.93). Combined muscle-strengthening and aerobic activity was associated with about 40% lower mortality vs neither. The effect of higher volumes is "unclear." — [Momma 2022, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2021-105061](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9209691); RR and 40% figures via [BMJ press summary](https://bmjgroup.com/90-120-weekly-minutes-of-strength-training-may-be-optimal-for-lowering-death-risk/) (secondary)
- **Dos Santos et al. 2022 (JAMA Intern Med), US National Health Interview Survey cohort.** All-cause mortality HR vs inactive: weekend warrior (1–2 sessions/week) 0.92 (95% CI 0.83–1.02), regularly active (≥3 sessions/week) 0.85 (95% CI 0.83–0.88). "Given the same amount of total MVPA, weekend warrior participants had similar all-cause and cause-specific mortality rates as regularly active participants." Reported n=350,978 with a median 10.4 years of follow-up (secondary source). — [Dos Santos 2022, PubMed 35788615](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35788615/); [Healio summary](https://cm.healio.com/news/primary-care/20220711/physical-activity-confers-similar-benefits-when-spread-out-or-concentrated-in-fewer-days)
- **O'Donovan et al. 2017 (JAMA Intern Med 177(3):335–342),** pooled English and Scottish Health Surveys, 63,591 adults, 561,159 person-years. The weekend-warrior pattern was associated with lower all-cause, CVD and cancer mortality. A figure of about 30% lower all-cause mortality circulates, but I could not confirm it from a primary source. — [BJSM blog with reference](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/2024/05/13/the-weekend-warrior-strikes-again-more-evidence-that-the-risk-of-death-is-reduced-in-people-who-exercise-once-or-twice-per-week/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default (strong):** every plan the generator emits, whatever the goal, should hit at least 2 strength days/week covering all major muscle groups. That is the one guideline target the app owns. Show aerobic minutes against the 150–300 moderate / 75–150 vigorous band, counting vigorous minutes double (the "equivalent combination" in WHO).
- **Generator default (moderate):** count every logged bout of cardio, including short ones, toward the weekly aerobic band. WHO dropped the 10-min minimum.
- **Generator default (moderate):** if the user can only train 1–2 days/week, generate a weekend-warrior plan without warning that it's inferior for health. Mortality outcomes are similar at equal total volume. For strength progress, put 2 sessions on non-consecutive days if possible (inference from the strength-frequency data in Q5, not from the weekend-warrior studies).
- Do not push strength volume far beyond about 60 min/week on health grounds alone. Momma's J-shape means the benefit beyond that is unclear. This is observational and confounded, so it is not a reason to cap goal-driven users.
- **Ask the user:** days available per week (1–2 is acceptable) and whether they want the app to track guideline minutes at all. Some strength-focused users won't care.
- **Folklore check, "you need 3 hours a week":** WHO's floor is 150 min moderate (2.5 h) or 75 min vigorous (1.25 h), "some is better than none," and benefits begin below the floor. The strength-training optimum is 30–60 min/week.

### Gaps
- **ACSM:** I did not retrieve ACSM position stands in this session (e.g., Garber et al. 2011 on exercise quantity and quality; the 2009 progression models stand). Their content is not cited here and should be checked separately before the report quotes ACSM.
- I could not confirm the PAG 2018 wording on bouts or its evidence grades from the primary JAMA text.
- Weekend-warrior evidence is entirely observational (self-reported activity, mortality endpoints). It does not address fitness or strength adaptation.
- Momma 2022's exact pooled RR came from a press summary and was not verified against the paper.

## 7. Older adults and beginners: safety and priorities (balance, power)

### Takeaway
For adults 65+, WHO 2020 strongly recommends varied multicomponent activity that emphasizes functional balance and strength training at moderate or greater intensity on ≥3 days/week. High-certainty evidence shows that balance and functional exercise reduce falls. The NSCA 2019 position statement supports resistance training, including power training, for older adults, including frail ones. Beginners' evidence: lifting does not cost them strength or muscle when cardio is added. They get the largest relative VO2max gains from low-volume HIIT, though lifting can blunt those gains slightly in the untrained.

### Cited Findings
- **WHO 2020, older adults (65+), "As for adults, plus":** "older adults should do varied multicomponent physical activity that emphasises functional balance and strength training at moderate or greater intensity on 3 or more days a week, to enhance functional capacity and to prevent falls" (strong recommendation). Since 2010 this has applied to all older adults, not only those with poor mobility. — [Bull 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955)
- WHO 2020 evidence statements: "High-certainty evidence demonstrates that balance and functional exercises reduce the rate of falls." Moderate-certainty evidence shows that multicomponent activity (balance, strength, endurance, gait and function training) may reduce fall-related injury and helps bone health and osteoporosis prevention. High-certainty evidence shows an inverse dose-response between aerobic volume and functional limitation. — [Bull 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955)
- WHO 2020 defines multicomponent activity as combining aerobic, muscle-strengthening and balance training in a session, done at home or in a group. — [Bull 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955)
- US PAG 2018: older adults should do multicomponent activity including balance training, and activity reduces fall-related injury risk. — [Piercy 2018](https://www.qxmd.com/r/30418471)
- **NSCA position statement (Fragala et al. 2019, JSCR 33(8):2019–2052).** Covers program design, adaptations, functional benefits, and frailty, sarcopenia and chronic conditions. States that a well-designed program can improve muscular power and neuromuscular function in older adults, and lists mobility and physical function as benefits. — [Fragala 2019, PubMed 31343601](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31343601/); [full-text copy](https://is.muni.cz/el/fsps/podzim2020/e057/um/Resistance_Training_for_Older_Adults__Position.1.pdf?kod=bk2240)
- Schumann 2022: age (<40 vs >40) did not moderate concurrent-training effects on strength, power or hypertrophy. — [Schumann 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7)
- Weston 2014: low-volume HIT gains in VO2max were larger in sedentary people than in active ones. — [Weston 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24743927/)
- Huiberts 2024: untrained people (but not trained) had smaller VO2max gains with concurrent training than with endurance alone. — [Huiberts 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933151/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default for users 65+ (strong):** ≥3 days/week that include balance and functional strength work. A lifting day can carry a short balance block (single-leg stance, step-ups, carries) to satisfy this without extra days. Keep ≥2 days of all-major-muscle strengthening.
- **Generator default for users 65+ (moderate):** include intent-to-move-fast (power) variants of familiar lifts, e.g., fast concentric sit-to-stand or step-up with a controlled lowering, once technique is stable. The NSCA statement supports power training. The specific exercise choices are an inference, not an extracted recommendation.
- For older or deconditioned users, default cardio to moderate continuous or submaximal intervals, not all-out sprints. The SIT evidence (Gillen) comes from younger sedentary men, and no source here establishes all-out safety in older adults (inference; see Gaps).
- **Beginners (moderate):** same-session lifting plus cardio is fine and the order barely matters. Ramp volume conservatively for adherence. The minimum dose from Q5 is a reasonable starting point.
- **Ask the user:** age band (or birth year); any balance problems or falls in the past year; medical conditions or clinician restrictions (a safety gate, not diagnosis); comfort with high-effort intervals.
- **Folklore check, "older adults should avoid heavy or fast lifting":** contradicted by the NSCA statement's endorsement of resistance and power training for older adults, including frail ones (moderate; position statement, not a meta-analysis).

### Gaps
- I did not extract NSCA-specific dosing (sets, reps, %1RM, power-training velocity) or its safety screening guidance from the full text.
- No source here addressed pre-participation screening (e.g., ACSM's 2015 screening algorithm). That should be researched separately before the app gives medical-adjacent prompts.
- I found no meta-analytic data on HIIT safety in adults 65+ in this session.
