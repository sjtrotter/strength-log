# Endurance-training programming for general fitness and goal events (evidence-graded defaults for a cardio plan generator)

Scope: running, walking, cycling (outdoor/stationary), rowing, elliptical. Goals: general aerobic fitness, a distance (first 5K/10K/half), a pace/time target, fitness tests (2-mile run, PACER/20-m shuttle, Cooper 12-min). Grades used below: **strong** (multiple consistent meta-analyses/RCTs or position stand), **moderate** (one good meta-analysis or several consistent cohorts), **weak/contested** (small/conflicting studies, or methodological dispute), **preference** (no outcome difference; pick for adherence/convenience).

Research note: ~25 searches/fetches. Several numbers come from abstracts or indexed summaries, not full texts; those are flagged. The brief's "Rosenblat 2022" and "Casado 2022 (Sports Medicine)" citations could not be found as stated; the nearest real papers are listed (Rosenblat 2019 and 2025; Casado 2022 in IJSPP; Campos et al. 2022 in IJSM).

## 1. Intensity distribution: polarized vs pyramidal vs threshold, recreational vs trained

### Takeaway
Programs dominated by low-intensity volume (pyramidal or polarized, roughly 70-80%+ easy) beat threshold-heavy programs, but polarized vs pyramidal is close to a wash: the latest IPD network meta-analysis finds no overall difference, with a hint that recreational athletes do slightly better on pyramidal and competitive athletes on polarized. Grade: "mostly easy" = **moderate-strong**; "polarized specifically" = **weak/contested**.

### Cited Findings
- Rosenblat, Perrotta & Vicenzino 2019 (J Strength Cond Res), RCTs of polarized (POL) vs threshold (THR): only 4 studies qualified for qualitative review, 3 for meta-analysis; time-trial effect favored POL, ES = -0.66 (95% CI -1.17 to -0.15), moderate. — [PubMed 29863593](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29863593/) (figures as reported in search index; full text not checked)
- Rosenblat et al. 2025, Sports Medicine 55(3):655-673, DOI 10.1007/s40279-024-02149-3: IPD network meta-analysis, 13 studies, 348 endurance athletes (150 recreational, 198 competitive), searched to Feb 2024. No difference POL vs pyramidal (PYR) for VO2max (SMD -0.06, p = 0.68) or time trial (SMD -0.05, p = 0.34); no significant difference between POL and any other TID. Subgroup: recreational vs competitive response differed for POL vs PYR on VO2max (SMD -0.63, p < 0.05); authors suggest competitive athletes may benefit from POL and recreational athletes from PYR. — [Univ. Stirling record](https://www.stir.ac.uk/research/hub/publication/2095779); [full-text copy](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Which-Training-Intensity-Distribution-Intervention-will.pdf)
- Oliveira, Boppre & Fonseca 2024, Sports Medicine 54(8):2071-2095, DOI 10.1007/s40279-024-02034-z: 17 studies (n = 437), 14 meta-analyzed. POL superior for VO2peak, small: SMD 0.24 (95% CI 0.01-0.48), I² = 0%. No difference for time trial: SMD -0.01 (95% CI -0.28 to 0.25). VO2peak advantage only in <12-week interventions (SMD 0.40; 0.08-0.71) and highly trained/national-level athletes (SMD 0.46; 0.10-0.82); none at ≥12 weeks (SMD 0.04) or in trained/developmental athletes (p = 0.82). No POL superiority for time-to-exhaustion or velocity/power at second threshold. — [PMC11329428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11329428/)
- Campos, Casado et al. 2022, Int J Sports Med 43(4):305-316, DOI 10.1055/a-1559-3623 (systematic review, middle/long-distance runners): programs with large low-intensity volume (pyramidal or polarized) produced bigger gains than threshold-heavy programs; suggests ~≥70% low intensity and ≤30% threshold + high intensity. — [PubMed 34749417](https://www.pubmed.gov/34749417/); [Thieme](https://thieme-connect.de/products/all/doi/10.1055/a-1559-3623)
- Casado, González-Mohíno, González-Ravé & Foster 2022, IJSPP: review of 10 studies of highly trained/elite distance runners; they typically train pyramidally, shifting toward polarized in the competition period. — [PubMed 35418513](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35418513/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default (all users):** ~75-80% of weekly cardio time easy (conversational, RPE ~3-4/10, talk test "can speak in full sentences"), the rest split between "comfortably hard" (threshold/tempo) and hard intervals. Express as a pyramidal week for recreational users: e.g., 3 sessions → 2 easy + 1 quality; 4-5 sessions → 3-4 easy + 1 quality (+ 1 more quality only for experienced/competitive users). Grade: moderate.
- Do not market "polarized" as superior to beginners; its small VO2peak edge is limited to short blocks and highly trained athletes, and time-trial performance does not differ. Grade: weak/contested; treat as preference for recreational users.
- For true beginners (first 8-12 weeks), essentially all volume can be easy; quality sessions are optional until consistency is established (inference from the low-intensity-dominant evidence plus the beginner injury/dropout data in section 4).
- Ask the user: sessions/week available; training history (none / some / regularly racing); goal type. Route "regularly racing / competitive" to a polarized option.

### Gaps
- No "Rosenblat 2022" meta-analysis found; possibly a conflation with the 2025 IPD NMA. No "Casado 2022 Sports Medicine" paper found.
- Almost all TID trials are in already-trained athletes; no good trial data on TID in previously sedentary adults.
- Zone-boundary methods vary across studies (Rosenblat 2025 reports this did not add heterogeneity, but the inconsistency limits translation to app zones).

## 2. HIIT/SIT vs moderate continuous training (MICT) for VO2max and performance

### Takeaway
Both work, and both produce large VO2max gains vs no exercise in healthy adults; HIIT has a small extra edge (~1 mL/kg/min), mostly with long intervals (≥2 min), ≥15 min of total work, and ≥4-12 weeks. Grade: **strong** that both work; **moderate** for HIIT's small advantage.

### Cited Findings
- Milanović, Sporiš & Weston 2015, Sports Medicine 45(10):1469-1481 (28 controlled trials, healthy adults 18-45, ≥2 weeks): vs no-exercise controls, endurance training +4.9 mL/kg/min and HIT +5.5 mL/kg/min (likely large); HIT vs endurance training: +1.2 ± 0.9 mL/kg/min favoring HIT (possibly small). Longer interventions and lower baseline fitness associated with larger HIT gains; age and work:rest ratio effects unclear. — [Teesside research portal](https://research.tees.ac.uk/en/publications/effectiveness-of-high-intensity-interval-training-hit-and-continu-3/) (summary figures; secondary sources show minor inconsistencies in moderator values)
- Wen et al. 2019, J Sci Med Sport 22:941-947 (RCTs in healthy, overweight/obese and athletic adults, searched to April 2018): vs control, even short intervals (≤30 s), low volume (≤5 min work) and short-term HIIT (≤4 weeks) improved VO2max (SMD 0.79-1.65). Long intervals (≥2 min), high volume (≥15 min work) and ≥4-12 weeks had larger effects (SMD 0.50-2.48). vs MICT, only long-interval, high-volume, ≥4-12-week HIIT was superior (SMD 0.65-1.07). — [PubMed 30733142](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30733142/)
- In children and adolescents, a 2019 meta-analysis concluded HIIT improves cardiorespiratory fitness more than endurance training (outcomes included VO2max/peak, 20-m shuttle run bouts, Yo-Yo distance). — [PMC6539300](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6539300)
- Costigan et al. 2015 (BJSM), adolescents: HIIT effects on cardiorespiratory fitness large, body composition medium. — [PubMed 26089322](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26089322/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default interval session (once a week for intermediate users; optional for beginners after ~4-8 weeks of consistency):** 4-6 × 3-4 min hard (RPE 7-8/10, "can say only a few words") with equal or slightly shorter easy recovery; total hard work 12-20 min. This matches the Wen 2019 "long-interval, ≥15 min" profile that beats MICT. Grade: moderate.
- Short sprint-interval (≤30 s) sessions are a valid time-saver option (works vs control) but should not be presented as superior to steady cardio. Grade: moderate.
- HIIT's edge is ~1 mL/kg/min, smaller than the gain from simply training. The generator should never sacrifice consistency for intensity. Grade: inference from Milanović.
- Ask: available time per session (if ≤20 min, an interval option is reasonable); any cardiac or medical conditions (screen before vigorous work, per ACSM pre-participation practice, not researched here).

### Gaps
- Did not retrieve evidence on HIIT adherence/dropout vs MICT in free-living users or HIIT injury rates for running specifically.
- Bacon et al. 2013 "VO2max trainability and HIIT" meta-analysis surfaced ([PMC3774727](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3774727/)) but was not read.

## 3. Volume progression and running-injury risk (10% rule, weekly change, ACWR, single-session spikes)

### Takeaway
The 10% weekly rule has never been shown to prevent injury (the one RCT found no effect). Cohort data point to large jumps (>30% over two weeks in novices) and, in the newest and largest study, a single run exceeding the longest run of the past 30 days by >10%, as the risk signal, while week-to-week ratios and ACWR were not useful. ACWR is methodologically contested. Grade: "10% rule prevents injury" = **folklore (RCT null)**; single-session spike cap = **moderate (one large exploratory cohort)**; ACWR = **weak/contested**.

### Cited Findings
- Buist et al. 2008 (GRONORUN RCT, Am J Sports Med): 532 novice runners preparing for a 4-mile event randomized to a standard 8-week program (n = 268) or a graded 13-week program based on the 10% rule (n = 264). Running-related injury: 20.8% graded vs 20.3% standard, p = 0.90. — [Univ. Groningen record](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/no-effect-of-a-graded-training-program-on-the-number-of-running-r/); [trial protocol, BMC Musculoskelet Disord, DOI 10.1186/1471-2474-8-24](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/1471-2474-8-24.pdf)
- A secondary source reports weekly progression of ~10.5% (graded) vs ~23.7% (standard) in GRONORUN; not confirmed in the primary abstract. — [Running Physio](https://www.running-physio.com/skimpyresearch/)
- Nielsen et al. 2014, JOSPT, DOI 10.2519/jospt.2014.5164: 1-year prospective cohort, 874 healthy novice runners with GPS watches. Those who increased distance by >30% over a 2-week period appeared more vulnerable to distance-related injuries than those under 10%; association varied by injury type (patellofemoral pain, ITB syndrome, medial tibial stress syndrome, patellar tendinopathy). Authors suggested novices keep progression under 30% per 2-week period. — [Aalborg Univ. record](https://vbn.aau.dk/da/publications/excessive-progression-in-weekly-running-distance-and-risk-of-runn); [JOSPT patient page](https://www.orthobethesda.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/jospt-running-increased-mileage.pdf)
- Ramskov et al. 2018 (Run Clever RCT): no difference in injury risk between schedules that progressed running volume vs running intensity in recreational runners. — [Aalborg Univ. record](https://vbn.aau.dk/da/publications/progression-in-running-intensity-or-running-volume-and-the-develo)
- Frandsen et al., BJSM, published 26 Aug 2025: ~5,200 runners tracked with Garmin devices. Overuse injury rate rose significantly when a single session's distance exceeded the longest run of the previous 30 days by >10%. vs ≤10%: small spikes (>10-30%) ~64% higher, moderate (>30-100%) ~52% higher, large (>100%) ~128% higher (HRR 2.28). No relationship for the week-to-week ratio; ACWR showed an inverse (negative) dose-response. Described as exploratory; biomechanical factors not included as confounders. — [BJSM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/?p=11751); [LIH research portal](https://researchportal.lih.lu/en/publications/how-much-running-is-too-much-identifying-high-risk-running-sessio/); [Physiotutors summary](https://www.physiotutors.com/research/running-injury-prevention/)
- A 2022 NYC Marathon study (secondary report) tied rapid training-volume spikes to injury, with equal risk across runners including novices. — [FORE summary](https://foreonline.org/?p=16181) (secondary; primary not read)
- ACWR critique: Impellizzeri and colleagues argue ACWR lacks physiological, biological and computational rationale as a causal injury factor and simplified Banister's additive model into an unjustified ratio; their 2021 Sports Medicine paper "Time to Dismiss ACWR and Its Underlying Theory" reports contrived chronic-load substitutions also disprove its etiological theory. — [PMC7534938](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938)
- Lolli et al. 2017 (BJSM): mathematical coupling (acute period included in chronic) produces spurious correlation; example ACWR 1.71 vs 1.45 depending on inclusion. Counterpoint: an IJSPP 2019 elite basketball/weightlifting case study found coupling effects largely trivial. — [ECU record of the counterpoint](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/7321/); [Impellizzeri reference list](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/24733938.2018.1429014)
- Military data: Army Physical Readiness Training cut running mileage ~50% and replaced long runs with intervals and agility work; control group adjusted relative risk of time-loss overuse injury was 1.5 vs the new program, with APFT scores higher or similar. — [DTIC ADA523652](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA523652.pdf); [DTIC ADA506005](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA506005.pdf) (which report holds which figure was not verified)
- Secondary report: Marine recruits cutting running mileage 40% saw 54% fewer leg stress fractures with essentially unchanged aerobic fitness; Navy lower-mileage divisions had fewer injuries and equal 1.5-mile improvement. — [Kaiserslautern American](https://www.kaiserslauternamerican.com/constant-running-causes-overuse-injuries/) (newspaper; primary papers not checked)

### Inferences
- **Generator default rule (running):** cap any single run at ≤110% of the longest run in the last 30 days (Frandsen 2025). This is the most evidence-aligned, simplest guardrail and is computable from the app's own logs. Grade: moderate.
- **Secondary guardrail (running, novices):** keep 2-week distance growth under ~30% (Nielsen 2014). Grade: moderate (cohort). The 10% weekly figure can be kept as a conservative weekly default for UX simplicity, but the app must not claim it prevents injury. Grade: preference.
- Do not implement ACWR as a safety feature. Grade: weak/contested; the 2025 cohort even found an inverse association.
- After a layoff, re-anchor "longest run in last 30 days" to what was actually run, so the cap automatically tightens after a break.
- Military data suggest diminishing returns of high run volume for test-type fitness; more running is not automatically better for short tests (see section 7).
- Ask: longest run (time or distance) in the last month; current runs/week; any running injury in the past year (strongest known risk factor; see section 4).

### Gaps
- No evidence found applying the spike rule to cycling, rowing or elliptical; low-impact modes likely tolerate faster progression but this is unverified.
- Frandsen 2025 DOI not captured; exact HRR CIs not retrieved.
- Gabbett/proponent rebuttals on ACWR not retrieved.

## 4. Beginner running programs (run/walk, couch-to-5K): dropout and injury

### Takeaway
Novices get injured at roughly 2x the rate per hour of recreational runners, and in a modified couch-to-5K cohort only 27% completed; dropout tied to injury and rigid progression. Run/walk is a reasonable on-ramp but evidence that it lowers injury is lacking. Grade: injury-rate figures **moderate-strong**; couch-to-5K dropout **moderate (single cohort)**; run/walk prevents injury **weak/unproven**.

### Cited Findings
- Videbæk et al. 2015, Sports Medicine 45(7):1017-1026 (13 studies): injuries per 1000 h of running were 17.8 (95% CI 16.7-19.1) in novices vs 7.7 (6.9-8.7) in recreational runners; range across studies 2.5-33.0; heterogeneous injury definitions. — [PMC4473093](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4473093/); [PubMed 25951917](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25951917/)
- Buist et al. 2010 (BJSM), novice and recreational runners preparing for a 4-mile race: 30.1 (95% CI 25.4-34.7) injuries per 1000 h. — [PubMed 18487252](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18487252/)
- Relph et al. 2023, IJERPH 20(17):6682, DOI 10.3390/ijerph20176682: modified 9-week couch-to-5K, 110 participants (81.8% female, mean age 47.1). Only 27.3% completed; 19% reported an injury (21 injuries); previous injury raised new-injury odds (OR 7.56, 95% CI 2.06-27.75). Qualitative themes: MSK injury, negative emotions about non-completion, programme design; authors recommend injury-prevention advice and more flexible designs. Earlier literature reported up to 49% injury incidence in such programs. — [PMC10487403](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10487403/)
- A secondary summary of Relph reports 48.2% had quit by mid-point and 64.5% by the end (not verified against full text). — [Kintec blog](https://www.kintec.net/blog/why-most-couch-to-5k-programs-fail/)
- Hottenrott et al. 2016: 42 non-elite runners randomized to run/walk vs run-only marathon. Similar finish times; cardiac biomarkers rose in both (no cardiovascular-load reduction); run/walk reported less muscle pain and fatigue (p = 0.006). Injury not measured. — [PubMed 25467199](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25467199/)
- Buist 2008 (section 3): a 13-week graded program did not reduce injury vs an 8-week program in novices (~20% injured in both). — [Univ. Groningen](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/no-effect-of-a-graded-training-program-on-the-number-of-running-r/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default for "first 5K" from inactive:** 3 sessions/week, run/walk intervals (e.g., start ~1 min run / 1.5-2 min walk × 20-30 min total), easy effort throughout, ~8-12 weeks to continuous 5K. Grade: preference on the exact ratio; moderate on "easy effort, 3x/week" being adequate.
- **Adaptive, not calendar-locked:** a week can be repeated or skipped down without "failing" the plan, since dropout tracks rigid progression and injury (Relph). Advance to the next step only when the current step felt manageable (e.g., RPE ≤5 and no pain the next day). Grade: moderate rationale, preference on mechanics.
- Previous injury is the strongest predictor; ask about it and, if yes, start one level easier and slow progression. Grade: moderate.
- Present run/walk as a fatigue-management and on-ramp tool, not an injury-prevention guarantee.
- Ask: currently able to walk 30 min briskly? able to run 1 min continuously? past running injury? target race date (if any)?

### Gaps
- No RCT found comparing run/walk vs continuous-run programs on injury in beginners.
- No data found on optimal couch-to-5K length (8 vs 9 vs 12 weeks) with dropout as outcome.

## 5. Prescribing intensity without lab tests (talk test, RPE, HR from age, pace from a race)

### Takeaway
Talk test and RPE are valid, free anchors for the first ventilatory threshold (easy vs moderate boundary). Age-predicted HRmax has ±~10-12 bpm individual error, so HR zones from 220-age are a rough guide; Tanaka (208 - 0.7 × age) is modestly better. Race-based pace prediction (Riegel) is well calibrated up to the half marathon but too optimistic for the marathon. Grade: talk test/RPE **moderate**; HRmax formula imprecision **strong**; Riegel ≤ half **moderate**.

### Cited Findings
- Talk test systematic review (Reviews in Cardiovascular Medicine 2022, 10 studies, 414 subjects, mostly coronary artery disease): last positive talk-test stage correlated with the first ventilatory threshold; reliability acceptable but only one study had adequate reliability analysis; no standardized protocol. — [IMR Press](https://www.imrpress.com/journal/RCM/23/7/10.31083/j.rcm2307225/htm)
- A 2022 Sports Medicine review of subjective methods (DOI 10.1007/s40279-022-01690-3) reportedly found talk test and RPE valid against both ventilatory and respiratory compensation thresholds; comfortable speech likely possible below VT/LT and not above (Reed & Pipe narrative review). — reported via search summary; [IMR Press review](https://article.imrpress.com/journal/RCM/23/7/10.31083/j.rcm2307225/bc9c7933c0faffad0afcfc9f932a0c55.pdf) (the Sports Medicine paper itself was not opened; treat as secondary)
- Tanaka, Monahan & Seals 2001 (JACC 37:153-156): HRmax = 208 - 0.7 × age, derived from 351 studies and validated in 514 lab-tested adults; 220-age underestimates HRmax in older adults. — [PDF](https://webs.um.es/jlyuste/documts/tanaka.pdf)
- HERITAGE Family Study (sedentary adults, cycle ergometer): standard error 12.4 bpm for 220-age, 11.4 bpm for Tanaka. — [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/measured-maximal-heart-rates-compared-to-commonly-used-age-based-/)
- Lach et al. 2021 (Frontiers in Physiology, DOI 10.3389/fphys.2021.695950), physically active adults: 220-age mean errors up to 9 bpm; Tanaka among the most accurate; adding body composition/fitness improved prediction only slightly. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.695950/pdf)
- Vickers & Vertosick 2016 (BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil), recreational runners: Riegel formula well-calibrated up to half marathon but dramatically underestimates marathon time (≥10 min too fast for half of runners); marathon mean squared error 381 for Riegel vs 228 (one prior race + training model) and 208 (two prior races). Best predictors: weekly training mileage and previous race times. Sample size reported as 2,303 or 2,497 depending on source. — [MSKCC record](https://synapse.mskcc.org/synapse/works/101585); [Slate](https://www.slate.com/articles/sports/sports_nut/2014/10/running_calculator_introducing_slate_s_marathon_time_predictor_a_better.html)
- A 2025 Frontiers in Physiology study found a new half-marathon-based prediction model had accuracy similar to Daniels' VDOT; Riegel valid up to half marathon but improvable for longer. — [Frontiers 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1718298/full)
- ACSM 2011 position stand (Garber et al., MSSE 43(7):1334-1359, DOI 10.1249/MSS.0b013e318213fefb): ≥150 min/week moderate (≥30 min × ≥5 days) or ≥75 min/week vigorous (≥20 min × ≥3 days); continuous or accumulated in bouts. — [PubMed 21694556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default intensity language (primary):** three effort bands keyed to talk test + RPE (0-10): Easy = full sentences, RPE 3-4; Steady/Tempo ("comfortably hard") = short phrases, RPE 5-6; Hard intervals = a few words, RPE 7-9. These map onto the 3-zone model used in the TID literature. Grade: moderate.
- **HR (secondary, only if the user has a sensor):** use Tanaka 208 - 0.7 × age, show zones as ranges, and let a user-entered measured HRmax (or observed max in logged sessions) override it. Label it an estimate (±~10 bpm). Never present 220-age zones as exact. Grade: strong on imprecision.
- **Pace (for pace/time goals):** derive training paces from a recent race or time trial (e.g., a 5K or 2-mile/3,200 m test). Use Riegel (T2 = T1 × (D2/D1)^1.06) for predictions up to the half marathon; for marathon, add a margin or require weekly-mileage input. Grade: moderate. VDOT tables are an equivalent alternative (preference).
- Re-test pace every ~6-8 weeks (or auto-infer from logged hard efforts) to refresh zones (preference; no outcome data found).
- Ask: has a heart-rate monitor? known HRmax? recent race or test time and distance (and date)?

### Gaps
- Exact 2011 ACSM %HRR intensity bands not retrieved (only the 1998 stand's 40/50-85% HRR via a secondary summary).
- Shookster et al. 2020 (220-age accuracy) not retrieved directly.
- Riegel exponent value 1.06 is the standard published form; exact error statistics for 5K→10K/half not retrieved.
- Talk-test validation in healthy recreational runners specifically is thin (most data are cardiac patients).

## 6. Cycling, rowing and elliptical: progression and cross-modality transfer

### Takeaway
VO2peak measured on elliptical is similar to treadmill, and replacing 20-50% of running with cycling has not reduced running VO2max or performance over short trials, but transfer is incomplete for running-specific economy and for well-trained runners. No modality-specific progression trials were found; progress by duration first, then intensity (power/resistance), using the same effort bands. Grade: substitution for general fitness **moderate**; transfer for performance goals **weak/contested**; specific progression rules for bike/row/elliptical **no direct evidence (preference)**.

### Cited Findings
- A 2026 meta-analysis (Menges et al., Frontiers in Sports and Active Living, per secondary write-up): 7 RCTs ≥4 weeks; no significant difference between run-only and cycle-only training for treadmill VO2max, cycling VO2max or running performance; substitution 20-50% of running load; run-only favored treadmill VO2max, cycle-only favored cycle VO2max. — [Marathon Handbook](https://marathonhandbook.com/cycling-for-runners/) (secondary; original not read)
- Pizza et al. 1995: cycling cross-training during periods of increased run load was detrimental to running economy. — [Coach Science Abstracts](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol42/pizza.htm)
- Triathlete study (14 moderately trained): central adaptations from cycling transfer to running when cycle intensity is near-maximal. — [Coach Ray summary](https://www.coachray.nz/2022/06/26/science-on-sunday-high-intensity-cycle-interval-training-improves-cycling-and-running-performance-in-triathletes/) (secondary)
- Elliptical vs treadmill acute maximal tests: no difference in VO2max (47.9 vs 47.3 mL/kg/min, 20 recreationally active adults, randomized order) — [JSSM](https://jssm.org/jssm-05-662.xml-Fulltext); another study found treadmill higher (49.8 vs 44.7) — [ProQuest record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_733944684) (which figure belongs to which study was not fully verified); elliptical display estimates may not predict VO2peak accurately — [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4018778?lng=en)
- Treadmill, elliptical and rowing: similar VO2peak in 9 males, but maximal fat oxidation higher on treadmill. — [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4068219)
- Milanović 2015 and Wen 2019 (section 2) pooled HIIT/MICT across modalities including cycling, i.e., the VO2max response is not run-specific. — [Teesside](https://research.tees.ac.uk/en/publications/effectiveness-of-high-intensity-interval-training-hit-and-continu-3/); [PubMed 30733142](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30733142/)

### Inferences
- **General-fitness goal:** treat all five modalities as interchangeable for the weekly minutes target (ACSM 150/75 min). Let users mix. Grade: moderate.
- **Running goal (distance/pace/test):** keep the majority of aerobic work as running; allow cycling/elliptical/rowing as substitutes for some easy volume (up to ~50% in the short term) or when injured. Grade: weak-moderate.
- **Progression default for bike/row/elliptical:** (1) duration of continuous easy work up to the target session length; (2) then add an interval session; (3) then raise intensity via watts (stationary bike/rower), resistance level or cadence/stroke rate, holding RPE bands constant. Distance on stationary machines is device-dependent; prefer time + watts. Grade: preference (extrapolated from general aerobic principles; no modality trials found).
- No impact-based spike cap is evidenced for non-running modes; the generator can progress these faster (e.g., 10-20% time/week) but this is an inference, not a finding.
- Elliptical-console calorie/VO2 readouts should not be trusted for zone prescription.
- Ask: which machines/modes are available; does the bike/rower show watts; outdoor vs stationary.

### Gaps
- No RCT found comparing long-term VO2max training adaptations between rowing or elliptical and running.
- No evidence found on rowing-specific progression (e.g., 2 km erg time-trial pacing rules) in recreational users.
- Menges 2026 meta-analysis and the "50% substitution, 5K unchanged" trial were not read in primary form.

## 7. Test-specific training (2-mile run, PACER/20-m shuttle, Cooper 12-minute)

### Takeaway
These tests are all strongly aerobic and moderately correlated with VO2max; HIIT reliably improves cardiorespiratory fitness and shuttle-run performance in youth, and military data show cutting run volume while adding intervals keeps or improves test scores with fewer injuries. Test-specific pacing/practice is sensible but has little direct trial evidence. Grade: HIIT improves shuttle/field-test performance **moderate**; low-volume + intervals for military run tests **moderate**; test-specific practice/pacing **weak (inference)**.

### Cited Findings
- Mayorga-Vega et al. 2016, PLoS ONE (DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0151671), 123 studies, 200 correlations: walk/run field tests have low-to-moderate validity for VO2max (rp 0.42-0.79); 12-min test rp = 0.78 (0.72-0.83), the best of the time-based tests; validity not moderated by sex, age or VO2max level. — [PMC4795745](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4795745)
- Youth: HIIT improved cardiorespiratory fitness more than endurance training, with 20-m shuttle run bouts among the outcomes. — [PMC6539300](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6539300)
- Trained adolescent athletes (2026 meta-analysis, 35 studies, 988 athletes): HIIT improved VO2max and 20-m multistage fitness test performance vs non-HIIT controls; I² = 79.4%. — reported via search summary; specific source link not isolated (see [PMC11604620](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11604620) and [Frontiers 2024 school HIIT](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2024.1487572/full) for related evidence)
- Army PRT (lower mileage, intervals + agility): overuse injury RR 1.5 in the traditional-training control group; APFT (includes 2-mile run) scores higher or similar. — [DTIC ADA523652](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA523652.pdf)
- Navy (secondary): lower-mileage divisions improved 1.5-mile run time as much as higher-mileage divisions. — [Kaiserslautern American](https://www.kaiserslauternamerican.com/constant-running-causes-overuse-injuries/)

### Inferences
- **2-mile / Cooper 12-min (continuous ~10-20 min max efforts) default:** 3-4 runs/week: 1 interval session (e.g., 4-6 × 400-800 m or 2-4 min at slightly faster than goal 2-mile pace, equal recovery), 1 tempo (15-25 min "comfortably hard"), 1-2 easy runs. Set goal pace from a baseline test; re-test every 4-6 weeks. Grade: moderate (HIIT + military data), preference for exact reps.
- **PACER / 20-m shuttle:** same aerobic base plus short intervals with turns/changes of direction (shuttle-style repeats), and practicing the beep-test pacing at least once before the test. Grade: moderate for HIIT; weak for turn-specific practice (no direct trial found).
- Test-day practice: include a full practice test or time trial 2-3 weeks before the event and a lighter final week (taper). Grade: preference (no evidence retrieved in this pass).
- Converting a goal score: use test-specific norms (e.g., Army/school standards) as the target and derive required pace (2 mi time ÷ 2 per mile; Cooper target distance ÷ 12 min). For PACER, translate target level/laps to the required shuttle speed. These are arithmetic, not evidence claims.
- Ask: which test, target score/time, test date, current baseline (do a baseline test in week 1 if unknown), age/sex if the target is a graded standard.

### Gaps
- No pooled 20-m shuttle effect size found for general adolescents or adults.
- No trial found isolating 2-mile-specific interval prescriptions in civilians.
- Taper evidence for short tests not researched in this pass.

## 8. General aerobic fitness dose, and folklore check

### Takeaway
For general health and fitness, ACSM's 150 min/week moderate or 75 min/week vigorous is the anchor; running even once a week or under 50 min/week is associated with ~27% lower all-cause mortality, with no clear extra benefit from more. Several popular rules are not supported: the 10% rule, the "fat-burning zone" as a necessary fat-loss intensity, "you must run every day", and "more running is always better". Grade: ACSM dose **strong (position stand)**; low-dose running benefit **moderate (observational meta-analysis)**.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2011 position stand: ≥150 min/week moderate (≥30 min × ≥5 d) or ≥75 min/week vigorous (≥20 min × ≥3 d); multiple shorter bouts acceptable. — [PubMed 21694556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/)
- Pedisic et al. 2020, BJSM 54(15):898: 14 studies, ~232,000 participants, 25,951 deaths. Any running participation associated with 27% lower all-cause, 30% lower cardiovascular and 23% lower cancer mortality. Benefits seen at the lowest exposures (about once a week or <50 min/week); no significant dose-response for frequency, duration, pace or total volume. — [VU eprint PDF](https://vu-9.eprints-hosting.org/40291/1/Pedisic_etal%282020%29-Is_running_associated_with_alower_risk.pdf); [BJSM info](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/15/898.info) (minimum-dose detail partly from press coverage)
- FATmax: 2020 meta-analysis of RCTs in obese subjects: FATmax training reduced body weight (MD -4.30 kg), fat mass (-4.03 kg) and waist (-3.34 cm) vs control, with few studies. — [PMC7663534](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7663534/). A 2026 meta-analysis reported similar reductions vs controls (weight -3.66 kg; fat mass -2.86 kg). — [Biology of Sport 2026](https://www.termedia.pl/doi/10.5114/biolsport.2026.159565). FATmax varies widely between individuals (~30-65% VO2max) and sits ~5.8% VO2max below lactate/gas-exchange threshold. — [Eur J Appl Physiol 2022 PDF](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00421-022-04936-0.pdf) (attribution of the 30-65% figure to a specific review not verified). No head-to-head superiority over other intensities for fat loss was found in these sources.
- 10% rule: RCT null (Buist 2008, section 3). — [Univ. Groningen](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/no-effect-of-a-graded-training-program-on-the-number-of-running-r/)
- Higher run volume beyond a threshold adds injuries without fitness gains (military). — [DTIC ADA523652](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA523652.pdf); [Kaiserslautern American](https://www.kaiserslauternamerican.com/constant-running-causes-overuse-injuries/)

### Inferences
- **General-fitness generator default:** 3-5 cardio sessions/week totaling 150 min moderate (or 75 min vigorous, or a mix where 1 vigorous min ≈ 2 moderate min per ACSM equivalence), mostly easy, with 0-1 interval sessions; any modality. Progress by adding ~5-10 min per session or one session per week every 1-2 weeks until the target is reached, then add intensity. Grade: strong for target, preference for step size.
- **Folklore flags the app should avoid in copy and logic:**
  - "10% rule prevents injury": not supported (RCT null). Use the single-session spike cap instead.
  - "Fat-burning zone": low-intensity exercise does oxidize a larger fraction of fat and FATmax training reduces fat mass vs no exercise, but there is no evidence here that it beats other intensities for fat loss; the zone is individual and unknowable without testing. Do not offer a "fat burn" mode.
  - "You must run every day": benefit is seen from about once a week; 3 days/week is the ACSM vigorous floor; military data show cutting run volume did not hurt test fitness. Rest days are normal.
  - "No pain, no gain": no supporting evidence found; pain is the app's signal to hold or regress progression (prior injury is the strongest injury predictor and dropout tracks injury in beginners).
  - "Polarized is the secret": small, inconsistent advantage limited to trained athletes.
  - "220-age is your max HR": ±10-12 bpm individual error.
- Ask: primary goal (health/fitness vs event vs test); days/week; minutes/session; available modalities; injury history; HR monitor; recent race/test result.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence found on "no pain, no gain" (no studies sought it specifically); the flag is an inference.
- No primary evidence retrieved comparing FATmax vs HIIT head-to-head on fat loss.
- ACSM 11th/12th edition Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription (latest %HRR/RPE tables and pre-participation screening) were not retrieved in this pass.
- No evidence retrieved on progression templates for half-marathon/10K (long-run share of weekly volume, taper length); defaults for those distances in a generator would rest on coaching convention plus the spike cap from section 3.

## 9. Running by event group: shared principles and taper (scope addition from owner)

Sprinting (60-400 m) is out of scope (researched separately). Cross-cutting evidence that applies to every group below: low-intensity-dominant intensity distribution (section 1), the single-session spike cap (section 3), and the taper data in this section. Most event-specific evidence is descriptive (what world-class athletes do) or observational (what recreational finishers did), not RCTs; grades reflect that.

### Takeaway
Across 800 m to ultra, the same backbone holds (mostly easy volume, spike-capped long runs, volume-cut tapers that keep intensity); what changes by event is volume, long-run length, the share and type of hard work, and taper length. Taper principle (cut volume ~40-60%, keep intensity) is **moderate-strong**.

### Cited Findings
- Bosquet et al. 2007, Med Sci Sports Exerc (27 studies; runners, swimmers, cyclists, performance outcomes): optimal taper ~2 weeks (overall effect 0.59 ± 0.33) with training volume decreased exponentially by 41-60% (effect 0.72 ± 0.36), intensity and frequency unchanged; performance did not improve from reducing intensity alone. — [PubMed 17762369](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17762369/); [Coach Science Abstracts](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol131/bosquet.htm). The often-quoted "8-14 days" and "~3% improvement" are secondary paraphrases. — [Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/tapering/); [British Rowing](https://plus.britishrowing.org/2024/06/03/peaking-for-performance/)
- World-class 5K/10K/marathon runners start tapering 7-10 days before the main race. — [Haugen et al. 2022, Sports Med Open (UiT record)](https://munin.uit.no/handle/10037/26245)
- Smyth & Lawlor 2021, Frontiers in Sports and Active Living (DOI 10.3389/fspor.2021.735220), >158,000 recreational marathoners' logged training: strict (volume keeps falling week over week) 3-week tapers were associated with the largest benefit, median 5 min 32 s (2.6%) faster than a minimal taper; longer (3-4 week) and stricter tapers beat shorter/relaxed ones; women benefited more for tapers ≤3 weeks. Observational. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.735220); [Running Magazine summary](https://runningmagazine.ca/?p=81962)


### Inferences
- Taper length scales with event: ~7-10 days for 800 m-10K, ~10-14 days for the half, ~3 weeks for the marathon and ultras; in every case cut volume, keep some race-pace work, keep frequency. Grade: moderate.
- Event-group routing in the wizard: ask target event first, then goal time (or "finish"), race date, recent race, current weekly distance/runs, longest run in last 30 days; each event section below lists its extra questions.

### Gaps
- No taper trials specific to middle-distance, half-marathon or ultra recreational runners were found.

## 10. Middle distance (800 m to the mile / 3,000 m)

### Takeaway
Middle distance is the most anaerobic of the endurance events (aerobic share roughly 60-75% at 800 m, 75-85% at 1,500 m), so plans keep a large easy aerobic base but include more race-pace and lactate-tolerance intervals and more strength/plyometric work than longer events, with the 800 m skewed most toward speed. Evidence is descriptive (elite practice), so most defaults are **weak / expert-consensus**.

### Cited Findings
- Seiler/Haugen, Enoksen, Tønnessen & Sandbakk 2021, Sports Medicine 51:1835-1854, DOI 10.1007/s40279-021-01481-2 ("Crossing the Golden Training Divide"), position-statement style review of world-class 800/1500 m training: aerobic energy contribution ~60-75% (800 m) and ~75-85% (1,500 m), with a wider range at 800 m; lactate tolerance and lactate production intervals are regular sessions, particularly for 800 m runners (intervals typically 200-600 m at 800-1,500 m race pace with 1-3 min recoveries); 800 m runners do more strength, power and plyometric training than 1,500 m runners; a 60/40 split of annual sessions below vs at/above anaerobic threshold is cited as a distinguishing feature between the two events (which event gets which split could not be confirmed from the snippet). — [UiA record](https://uia.brage.unit.no/uia-xmlui/handle/11250/2789638); [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4087086?lng=en); [PMC8363530](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8363530)
- Esteve-Lanao et al. 2005 (MSSE), 8 subelite runners: time in zone 1 (below VT) dominated training (4,581 ± 979 min vs 1,354 Z2 and 487 Z3) and correlated with faster race times (r = -0.97, p = 0.008, ~10 km cross-country; r = -0.79, p = 0.06, ~4 km). — [Univ. Bath record](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/how-do-endurance-runners-actually-train-relationship-with-competi/)

### Inferences
- **Weekly volume default:** lowest of the distance groups. For recreational milers, 3-5 runs/week; the long run is modest (~60-75 min max or ~20-25% of weekly volume) since the event lasts 2-10 min. Grade: preference/expert convention.
- **Intensity distribution:** still pyramidal (mostly easy), but the hard fraction is larger than for marathoners. Weekly quality: 1 session of VO2max/race-pace intervals (e.g., 6-8 × 400 m at mile pace, 2 min jog), 1 threshold session or (for 800 m) a lactate-tolerance session (e.g., 3-4 × 300-500 m at 800 m pace, long recovery) in the competition phase; plus short strides/hill sprints 1-2×/week and 1-2 strength sessions. Grade: weak (elite descriptive) for exact sessions; moderate for "mostly easy."
- **Progression:** base (easy volume + strides) → add threshold → add race-pace/lactate work in the last 4-6 weeks. Taper short: ~7-10 days, cut volume ~40-60%, keep a little race-pace work (Bosquet logic). Grade: moderate for taper principle; weak for event specifics.
- **Fitness levels:** beginners targeting a first fast mile should mainly build aerobic base; the anaerobic sessions matter mostly for already-trained runners. Grade: inference.
- **Ask:** target event (800 / 1,500 / mile / 3K), goal time, recent race or a 1-mile/1,600 m time trial, current runs/week and weekly distance, access to a track, strength-training experience (the app already tracks lifting).

### Gaps
- Weekly km figures for world-class 800/1,500 m runners and their taper practice were not retrievable (full text behind redirect/captcha).
- No evidence found on training recreational or masters middle-distance runners specifically.

## 11. Distance (5K to 10K)

### Takeaway
5K/10K performance tracks mostly-easy volume plus one or two quality sessions (threshold and VO2max intervals). Elite 5K/10K runners do ≥80% of volume at low intensity; in subelite runners, more time in zone 1 predicted faster 10 km times and a randomized trial favored a low-intensity-heavy program. Grade: low-intensity dominance **moderate**; workout specifics **weak/expert**.

### Cited Findings
- Haugen, Sandbakk, Seiler & Tønnessen 2022, Sports Medicine - Open (review of world-class 5,000 m, 10,000 m and marathon runners): mid-preparation weekly distance 130-190 km for track (5K/10K) runners vs 160-220 km for marathoners, the difference mostly from longer sessions; 11-14 sessions/week; ≥80% of running volume at low intensity year-round; taper starts 7-10 days out; track runners race 9 ± 3 times/year vs marathoners 6 ± 2. — [UiT record](https://munin.uit.no/handle/10037/26245); [UiA record](https://uia.brage.unit.no/uia-xmlui/handle/11250/3007163); [PubMed 35362850](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35362850/)
- Esteve-Lanao 2005 (subelite): time in zone 1 correlated r = -0.97 with ~10 km cross-country time. — [Univ. Bath](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/how-do-endurance-runners-actually-train-relationship-with-competi/)
- Esteve-Lanao et al. 2007 (J Strength Cond Res), 12 subelite runners randomized to more zone-1 vs more zone-2 training: larger improvement in simulated 10.4 km race in the zone-1 group (-157 ± 13 s vs -121.5 ± 7.1 s, p = 0.03). — [Universidad Europea record](https://abacus.universidadeuropea.com/entities/publication/7fbfa682-6302-499f-9a81-4e468521d082)
- Campos/Casado 2022 (section 1): ~≥70% low intensity, ≤30% threshold + high in middle/long-distance runners. — [PubMed 34749417](https://www.pubmed.gov/34749417/)
- Riegel prediction is well calibrated up to half marathon (section 5), so 5K ↔ 10K conversions are reliable enough for pacing. — [MSKCC record](https://synapse.mskcc.org/synapse/works/101585)

### Inferences
- **Fitness levels:** first 5K = run/walk program (section 4); first 10K / improving 5K = 3-4 runs/week; performance 5K/10K = 4-6 runs/week.
- **Weekly volume:** first 10K ~15-30 km/week peak; improving runners ~25-50 km/week. Long run ~25-30% of weekly volume, building toward ~1-1.5× race distance for 10K (~60-90 min). Grade: preference/expert convention; no outcome trial found for these numbers.
- **Intensity / workouts:** ~80% easy. 1 quality session/week for 3-run weeks, 2 for 4+ runs: (a) threshold/tempo (20-30 min "comfortably hard" continuous or 3-4 × 8-10 min), (b) VO2max intervals (5-6 × 3-4 min or 800 m-1 km at ~5K pace, ~equal recovery; matches Wen 2019 long-interval profile). Grade: moderate (HIIT/TID evidence), preference for exact reps.
- **Progression:** grow weekly time by small steps while respecting the single-run cap (≤110% of the longest run in 30 days); add quality only after 3-4 weeks of consistent easy running; every 3rd-4th week a lighter week (preference; no RCT found).
- **Taper:** 7-10 days, volume down ~40-60%, keep a short race-pace session. Grade: moderate (Bosquet + elite practice).
- **Ask:** target event (5K or 10K), goal time or "just finish," race date, recent race/time trial, current weekly distance and runs/week, longest run in last 30 days.

### Gaps
- No RCT on recreational 5K/10K plan structures (e.g., 2 vs 1 quality sessions) found.
- No evidence retrieved on optimal long-run length for 5K/10K.

## 12. Half marathon and marathon

### Takeaway
For recreational half and full marathoners, higher weekly volume and a longer longest run are the strongest training correlates of faster finish times, without a measurable injury penalty in the one cohort that checked; tapers of about 3 weeks with steadily falling volume were linked to ~2.6% faster marathons. Grade: volume/long-run correlates **moderate (meta-regression + cohorts, observational)**; taper length for marathon **moderate (very large observational)**.

### Cited Findings
- Doherty, Keogh, Davenport, Lawlor, Smyth & Caulfield 2020, J Sci Med Sport 23(2):182-188, DOI 10.1016/j.jsams.2019.09.013: meta-regression of 85 studies, 137 cohorts (25% female) of elite and recreational marathoners. Average weekly running distance, number of weekly runs, maximum weekly distance and related training behaviors were each negatively associated with marathon time (R² 0.38-0.81, p < 0.001); 7.27% non-finisher rate. — [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4058441); [PubMed 31704026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31704026/) (per-variable coefficients not retrieved)
- Fokkema et al. 2020, Scand J Med Sci Sports 30(9):1692-1704, DOI 10.1111/sms.13725: 556 half-marathon and 441 marathon recreational runners (Netherlands). Half: weekly volume >32 km and longest run >21 km linked to faster finish; volume <25 km linked to slower finish (β 13.44 min, 95% CI 5.34-21.55). Marathon: <40 km/week slower, >65 km/week faster; longest run <25 km associated with slower finish. High volume and long runs also linked to less late-race pace decline. No associations between these training characteristics and running-related injuries. — [PMC7496388](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7496388/); [Erasmus repository](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/127621)
- Hokkaido Marathon cohort (Eur J Appl Physiol, 2023, 494 finishers): monthly training volume was the most important predictor of marathon time, but only when distance per workout exceeded a threshold. — [PubMed 36207466](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36207466/)
- Tanda & Knechtle 2015 (Open Access J Sports Med): weekly distance and mean training pace predicted marathon and 100 km times; pace was more influential for the marathon, volume for 100 km. — [PMC4425319](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4425319)
- Vickers & Vertosick 2016: Riegel underestimates marathon time (≥10 min too fast for half of runners); a model adding weekly mileage cut error (MSE 381 → 228). — [MSKCC record](https://synapse.mskcc.org/synapse/works/101585)
- World-class marathoners: 160-220 km/week mid-preparation, ≥80% low intensity, taper from 7-10 days. — [UiT record](https://munin.uit.no/handle/10037/26245)
- Smyth & Lawlor 2021 marathon taper data (section 9). — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.735220)
- Hottenrott 2016: run/walk in non-elite marathoners gave similar finish times with less post-race soreness/fatigue. — [PubMed 25467199](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25467199/)

### Inferences
- **Entry prerequisites (gate in the wizard):** half marathon from roughly comfortable 5K/10K running; marathon from roughly a comfortable half or ~30+ km/week base. Grade: preference.
- **Weekly volume targets (from Fokkema thresholds):** half ≥25 km/week at peak, ideally >32 km; marathon ≥40 km/week, ideally >65 km/week for time goals. Present as "what faster finishers did," not causal. Grade: moderate (observational).
- **Long run:** half: build to ≥18-21 km (or ~2 h cap for slower runners); marathon: build to ≥25 km, typically 28-32 km (or ~3 h cap). Build long runs under the single-session cap (≤110% of the 30-day longest), which naturally sets a 14-20-week plan length for most novices. Grade: moderate for "long runs matter"; spike cap moderate; time caps are coaching convention (weak).
- **Intensity distribution:** ~80% easy; for half: 1 threshold session (tempo, cruise intervals) + optional VO2max work; for marathon: threshold work plus marathon-pace segments inside long runs in the final 6-8 weeks. Pyramidal is the default for recreational runners (Rosenblat 2025). Grade: moderate for distribution, weak for session specifics.
- **Frequency:** more runs/week associated with faster marathons (Doherty); default 4 runs/week for marathon novices, 5-6 for time goals, if the user's schedule allows. Grade: moderate (observational).
- **Taper:** half ~10-14 days; marathon ~3 weeks with volume falling each week (e.g., ~-20-25%, -40%, -60%), intensity kept. Grade: moderate (Bosquet + Smyth).
- **Goal-time sanity check:** predict from a recent 10K/half with Riegel for the half; for marathon, add a margin or use a model including weekly mileage, and warn when goal pace exceeds the prediction. Grade: moderate.
- **Run/walk option** for first-time marathoners as a fatigue tool. Grade: moderate (one RCT, non-injury outcomes).
- **Ask:** half or full, race date (sets plan length), goal time or "finish," recent race (distance, time, date), current weekly distance, runs/week available, longest run in last 30 days, run/walk preference, injury history.

### Gaps
- Per-variable effect sizes from Doherty 2020 not retrieved.
- No RCTs found comparing long-run lengths or marathon-pace work in recreational runners.
- Half-marathon-specific taper data not found (only general Bosquet and marathon Smyth data).

## 13. Ultra distances (beyond the marathon: 50K, 50 mi, 100K, 100 mi, 24 h)

### Takeaway
Ultra performance and finishing are predicted mostly by weekly volume and the longest training run (and marathon PB), with pace mattering less than for the marathon. Injury is very common (77% of ULTRA participants reported a run-stopping injury in the prior year); lower high-intensity volume, more experience, and enough (not too little) preparation volume were protective. Evidence is small observational cohorts: **weak-moderate**. Back-to-back long runs are coaching convention: **preference**.

### Cited Findings
- 169 male 100 km finishers: training speed, weekly running km and age predicted race time (r² = 0.43); weekly volume negatively associated with race time. — [PubMed 21319608](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21319608/)
- Tanda & Knechtle 2015: for 100 km, training volume was more influential than training pace (the reverse of the marathon). — [Dove Press](https://www.dovepress.com/effects-of-training-and-anthropometric-factors-on-marathon-and-100-km--peer-reviewed-article-OAJSM); [PMC4425319](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4425319)
- 24-hour race, 63 male recreational ultrarunners: longest training session before the event and marathon PB were the strongest predictors; authors suggested ~3:20 marathon PB and a long training run of ≥60 km for maximal distance. — [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4023113?lng=en); a separate 24 h study also found marathon PB the strongest predictor — [PMC3781907](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3781907)
- ULTRA study baseline (Hoffman & Krishnan; Lucia et al., PLoS ONE 2014), 1,212 ultrarunners: 77% had at least one injury in the prior year that impeded running; older age, more ultra experience and lower high-intensity training volume associated with less injury. — [Runners Connect summary](https://runnersconnect.net/are-ultramarathoners-different/) (secondary; primary paper not opened)
- Comrades 2018 retrospective (106 runners, published 2025): lower training distance in the 12 weeks before the race associated with higher injury risk (p = 0.02), mostly during/after the race. — [SAJSM](https://journals.assaf.org.za/index.php/sajsm/article/view/8559)
- Two Oceans 2019 prospective (119 runners): moderate negative association between external training load and injury (r = -0.56, p = 0.025); non-gradual weekly changes (up or down) may raise injury/illness risk. — [UCT repository](https://open.uct.ac.za/items/6c038b37-6df9-451a-84df-55627b3f7ac7)
- Training load-injury modelling in ultrarunners with generalized additive models (2025). — [PMC12327882](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12327882/) (not read; listed for completeness)
- Back-to-back long runs: coaching sources recommend two consecutive long runs (e.g., 40-50 km each) in peak 50 mi/100K blocks; other coaches call them useful but not necessary. — [Marathon Handbook](https://marathonhandbook.com/ultramarathon-long-runs/); [iRunFar](https://www.irunfar.com/ultramarathon-training-volume) (coaching opinion)
- The 2019 ISSN position stand by Tiller et al. covers ultra nutrition, not training volume. — [SHU repository PDF](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/25874/2/s12970-019-0312-9.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Entry gate:** require a completed marathon (or equivalent long-run history) before generating a 50K+ plan; beginners are routed to the marathon plan first. Grade: preference, supported by marathon-PB-as-predictor data.
- **Weekly volume:** the dominant lever; progress volume primarily via a longer long run and more easy time, keeping high-intensity volume low (ULTRA). Use time-on-feet rather than distance for trail events (vert and terrain make distance misleading). Grade: weak-moderate.
- **Long run:** build to ~60-80% of race distance for 50K-50 mi, or several 4-6 h runs / back-to-back weekends for 100K-100 mi, under the single-session spike cap. Grade: weak (24 h cohort supports a long longest run; exact fractions are convention).
- **Intensity distribution:** very easy-dominant; 0-1 quality sessions/week (threshold or hill work); include hiking for mountain ultras. Grade: weak.
- **Progression:** gradual and steady; avoid both sudden jumps and drops (Two Oceans), and avoid under-preparing (Comrades). Grade: weak-moderate.
- **Taper:** ~2-3 weeks, volume down 40-60% (extrapolated from Bosquet/marathon data; no ultra-specific taper evidence found). Grade: weak.
- **Ask:** race distance and type (road/trail, elevation gain), cutoff time or goal, race date, marathon PB, current weekly volume (time and distance), longest run in last 30 days, availability for long weekend sessions, injury history, walk-break plan.
