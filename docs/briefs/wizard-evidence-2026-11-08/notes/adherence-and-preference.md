# Adherence, user preference and autonomy, and schedule flexibility (evidence for the setup wizard)

Evidence grades used throughout: **strong** (consistent meta-analytic or very large-cohort evidence), **moderate** (systematic reviews with mixed designs, or several consistent RCTs), **weak/contested** (small trials, correlational data, or conflicting results), **preference** (no outcome difference, so the user's taste should decide).

Scope note: this session ran about 17 searches and fetches. Where a figure comes from a search snippet or a secondary source rather than the primary paper, the bullet says so. References I know but could not verify this session are listed under Gaps, not presented as findings.

## 1. Does letting people choose exercises, intensity, or modality improve adherence or outcomes?

### Takeaway
Self-selection reliably improves how exercise *feels*: more pleasure, more enjoyment, lower perceived exertion. In the trials found, it costs little or nothing in strength or hypertrophy. Direct evidence that choice improves long-term *adherence* is thin: one small RCT and SDT meta-analyses that show small effects. Grade: affect benefit **moderate**; no outcome penalty **moderate** for strength, **weak** overall; adherence benefit **weak** (plausible, not established).

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis (Oliveira et al. 2015): whether self-selected sessions feel better than imposed ones depends on the intensity of the imposed session. When imposed intensity is above what people would choose, affect is worse. When intensity is matched, the gap narrows. — [Oliveira et al. 2015, PMC4523714](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4523714/)
- In separate experiments, choice and intensity each contribute to affect. "Disentangling the effects of choice and intensity" found that self-selected and imposed intensity differ in affective response and preference. — [Psychology of Sport and Exercise 2013, S1469029213000320](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1469029213000320)
- Adolescent girls: intensity did not differ between self-selected and prescribed sessions, yet affect was significantly more positive in the self-selected session. That points to an effect of choice itself, beyond intensity. — [PMC4182279](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4182279/)
- Women with obesity: self-selected aerobic intensity was compared with prescribed intensity on pleasure. The authors only *hypothesize* an adherence benefit; they did not measure adherence. — [PMC11843731](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11843731/)
- Ekkekakis's integrated model makes the theoretical case for self-paced exercise. It holds that intensity above the ventilatory/lactate threshold produces displeasure, and that displeasure undermines adherence. — [Ekkekakis et al. 2008, "Exercise, Affect, and Adherence: An Integrated Model and a Case for Self-Paced Exercise," JSEP](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/23439133_Exercise_Affect_and_Adherence_An_Integrated_Model_and_a_Case_for_Self-Paced_Exercise)
- Resistance training, older women and men, 12-week RCT: self-selected loads gave strength gains similar to imposed loads, with more positive affect and lower RPE. Adherence was not a measured outcome. — [PMC11653500](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11653500/)
- Resistance training, untrained men: self-selected repetition duration gave muscle mass and strength gains comparable to controlled duration. — [PMC7061915](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7061915/)
- Single-session crossover: self-selecting leg-extension load, reps, and rest produced more enjoyment and arousal than an identical prescribed session. Affect only. — [PubMed 40505704 (Psychol Sport Exerc 2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40505704/)
- Counterpoint on load self-selection: women who self-selected resistance-training intensity chose loads that are typically below strength-building thresholds, and the presence of a personal trainer raised the self-selected load. (Title/abstract level only; the full text was not read.) — [Ratamess et al., "Self-Selected Resistance Training Intensity in Healthy Women: The Influence of a Personal Trainer"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5555217_Self-Selected_Resistance_Training_Intensity_in_Healthy_Women_The_Influence_of_a_Personal_Trainer)
- Choice and adherence, six-week RCT in 30 older adults: the choice group reported more autonomy and higher adherence than no-choice controls (0.935 ± 0.04 vs 0.791 ± 0.05, p < 0.05). Small sample, short duration. (Taken from a search-result summary; the URL-to-study mapping is likely but not confirmed.) — [JEMS / scapps.org](https://scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/915)
- Trials registered but not yet reported: an autonomy-based versus standard resistance prescription non-inferiority RCT (120 sedentary adults, 3 months, adherence tracked), and a predetermined versus self-selected repetition RCT (60 sedentary adults, 18–45). No results were found. — [NCT05371587](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05371587); [NCT04895865](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04895865)
- A systematic review of 53 studies concluded that self-selected intensity, affect-based prescription, and social support are effective strategies for increasing adherence. This came from a search summary; the specific review and its citation were not identified. Treat as unverified. — (source not pinned; see Gaps)

### Inferences
- **Default:** the generator should *propose* a complete program, so the user never faces a blank slate, and make every exercise swappable within its movement pattern. Choosing the exercise has no measurable cost in the trials above and is the most plausible place for autonomy to pay off.
- **Load is the exception.** Unsupervised novices tend to under-select load (Ratamess), so the generator should set the progression and loading. The user should be able to override it, with RPE/RIR-based autoregulation as the escape valve, rather than choosing loads from scratch.
- **Ask:** "Any exercises you hate or can't do (equipment, pain)?" and "Which do you enjoy?" A blocklist plus favorites is low-friction and captures most of the affect benefit.
- **Cardio modality and intensity:** let the user pick the modality (run, bike, row, walk). For beginners, default conditioning intensity to talk-test/self-paced moderate rather than prescribing work above threshold, per the Ekkekakis affect model.
- **Folklore flag:** "The best program is the one with the most science behind it, whatever the user likes." Not supported. Within the range of evidence-reasonable programs, outcome differences are small (see Q5 on frequency, and the self-selection trials above). Affect and enjoyment predict continued participation (Q2). A program the user abandons produces nothing.

### Gaps
- No completed RCT with long-term adherence as the primary outcome was found comparing self-selected versus prescribed *exercise selection* in resistance training.
- The "53-study systematic review" figure could not be traced to a citation this session.
- Ladwig, Hartman & Ekkekakis 2017, "Affect-based exercise prescription," ACSM's Health & Fitness Journal 21(2):10–15: reference entry found only, content not read.

## 2. Enjoyment and affective response as predictors of adherence

### Takeaway
How good exercise feels *during* moderate-intensity exercise prospectively predicts future physical activity. Post-exercise affect does not. Affective judgments (enjoyment) also predict habit formation. Grade: **moderate** (consistent across systematic reviews, but mostly observational, with small to moderate correlations).

### Cited Findings
- Rhodes & Kates 2015 (systematic review, PRISMA): a positive change in affective response *during* moderate-intensity exercise was linked to future physical activity. Post-exercise affect had a null relationship. Annals of Behavioral Medicine 49:715–731, doi 10.1007/s12160-015-9704-5. — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Can-the-Affective-Response-to-Exercise-Predict-and-Rhodes-Kates/7a57d236a8c5f1e516f4051f2ad53f5e60eab378)
- Rhodes and colleagues have published three reviews of affective response/judgment and physical activity (2009, 2015 with Kates, 2019). The 2009 review drew on 82 correlational and 20 experimental studies. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2021 systematic review, 10.3389/fpsyg.2021.628993](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.628993)
- Affective–Reflective Theory (Brand & Ekkekakis 2018): momentary affect drives fast, automatic approach or avoidance of exercise, and reflective processes are needed for long-term change. This is a theoretical framework, not an effect estimate. — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Affective%E2%80%93Reflective-Theory-of-physical-inactivity-Brand-Ekkekakis/e8dd79834fcf5baf316bcbefc1c1fc6a0b5aef56)
- Narrative review and conceptual framework of affective determinants of physical activity. — [PMC7735992](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7735992/)
- Kaushal & Rhodes 2015: affective judgments predicted habit formation in new gym members (β = .13), alongside consistency, low complexity, and environment (see Q4). — [PubMed 25851609](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25851609/)
- SDT review (Teixeira 2012): intrinsic motivation, meaning enjoyment and interest, was more predictive of *long-term* adherence, while identified regulation (valuing the outcomes) was more predictive of early adoption. — [Teixeira et al. 2012, IJBNPA 9:78, doi 10.1186/1479-5868-9-78](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1479-5868-9-78)

### Inferences
- **Default:** avoid early sessions that feel punishing. Beginners should start with conservative volume and RIR (e.g., 2–3 reps in reserve) and no near-failure sets in the first weeks. The first sessions should feel achievable. This is a design inference from "affect during exercise predicts return", not a tested app intervention.
- **Ask, lightly:** a one-tap post-session "how did that feel?" (e.g., a 3–5-point scale). The generator can then deload or swap when sessions are consistently rated unpleasant. Self-monitoring of affect is cheap and is the predictor that matters.
- **Frame early motivation around valued outcomes** (identified regulation helps adoption). Over time, surface enjoyment and mastery cues such as PRs and streak-free progress views (intrinsic motivation helps maintenance).

### Gaps
- The full Rhodes & Kates 2015 text was not read, so pooled correlation magnitudes are not given here.
- A "Rhodes 2017" enjoyment paper was requested. The search attributes the 2017 date to the print issue of the 2015 review, but there is also Rhodes, McEwan & Rebar 2019 on affective judgments ("Theories of physical activity behaviour change: A history and synthesis of approaches", Psychol Sport Exerc), which I did not verify.

## 3. Dropout rates in beginner programs and gyms, and predictors of dropout

### Takeaway
Most beginners stop. In the largest dataset available (about 389k app users, 2026), only about 10% of beginners were still training weekly at 12 months, and median time to dropout was 19 weeks. The strongest modifiable predictor is **consistency in the first 28 days**. The commonly quoted "about 50% drop out within 6 months" is a secondhand statistic that varies a lot by population. Grade: magnitude of dropout **strong** (large cohort, consistent direction); "first month predicts" **strong** as an association, **not causal**.

### Cited Findings
- Conti, Marzagão, Galpin & Schoenfeld 2026, Frontiers in Sports and Active Living, doi 10.3389/fspor.2026.1855668. Fitbod app, n = 389,481 adults (100,709 beginners). Adherence = at least 1 workout/week with up to 6 missed weeks tolerated; dropout = more than 6 weeks without activity, assessed from day 29 to day 365. — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2026.1855668/full)
  - 52-week adherence: beginners 10.1%, intermediate 18.3%, advanced 25.8%. The Discussion calls the latter two "six months," which conflicts with the Results; the Results say 52 weeks. — same source
  - Median dropout time for beginners was 19 weeks; the preprint said 14 weeks. — same source; [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/709)
  - Early consistency (active days in the first 28 days, per 1 SD) was the strongest predictor: HR 0.73 (0.72–0.74) at week 11, 0.87 at week 26, 0.91 at week 40. The protective association weakens over time. — same source
  - Age: beginner 52-week adherence was 8.4% (18–30), 10.5% (31–40), 12.4% (41–50), 13.3% (51+). Sex: male 11.4% vs female 8.0%; pooled HR male vs female 0.86. These effects are modest. — same source
  - Greater equipment diversity and more emphasis on resistance exercise were associated with lower dropout risk. — same source
  - Longer app-generated workouts were protective mainly among users who also trained frequently (week 11: HR 0.67 long vs 0.79 short per SD of frequency). — same source
- Supervision (exploratory meta-analysis): adherence was 91.5% (95% CI 82.7–96.0) supervised versus 87.1% (71.2–94.9) unsupervised in RT trials, with overlapping CIs. Adherence was lower in previously untrained participants. — [Role of Supervision in Resistance Training, SR/MA](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/359808022_The_Role_of_Supervision_in_Resistance_Training_an_Exploratory_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-Analysis)
- Trial context: in a sarcopenic obesity SR, overall dropout was 9%, increasing with intervention duration, and supervised interventions beat self-monitored ones. — [Airiti Library record](https://www.airitilibrary.com/Article/Detail/P20250716001-N202601230009-00003)
- The "up to 50% drop out within 6 months" figure is commonly traced to Linke et al. 2011. Other sources give 40–70% for gyms, 15% for supervised senior programs, and 48% for older veterans. — [Older veterans, PMC7743800](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7743800/); [Dropout from exercise programs for seniors](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7466555_Dropout_from_Exercise_Programs_for_Seniors_A_Prospective_Cohort_Study)
- Most dropout from exercise training among sedentary adults with overweight/obesity happens before or within 2–3 months of onset. Practical barriers (time, location) predicted not starting; functional limitations predicted dropout. — [Translational J ACSM, Determinants of Dropout](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/acsm-tj/fulltext/10.1249/tjx.0000000000000190~determinants-of-dropout-from-and-variation-in-adherence-to)
- Umbrella review (chronic disease and older adults): ten reviews showed that longer interventions have lower adherence. Lack of time is the most commonly reported barrier. — [PMC7922504](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7922504/)

### Inferences
- **The first four weeks are the product.** The wizard's job is to produce a plan the user will *actually complete* in month one. That argues for a frequency the user commits to, defaulting conservatively, rather than the theoretical optimum.
- **Default dropout handling:** treat any gap shorter than about 6 weeks as "still a member," which matches the cohort's operational definition. Resume with a re-entry session, not a reset to zero and not a guilt prompt (see Q4).
- **Folklore flag:** "You need a coach to progress." Not supported as stated. Supervised versus unsupervised RT adherence CIs overlap (91.5% vs 87.1%). Supervision does help some clinical populations, and unsupervised novices under-load (Q1). The defensible version is "structure and progression rules help". An app can supply those.
- **Don't over-index on demographics** in the wizard. Age and sex effects are modest, and asking about them for adherence-prediction purposes adds friction for little value.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis pooling dropout specifically for beginner, non-clinical, gym-based resistance training was found.
- Conti 2026 is observational, from an app with self-selected users. Early consistency may mark motivation rather than cause adherence.
- Linke et al. 2011 (the 50% figure) was not checked at source.

## 4. Flexible versus fixed scheduling, missed-day handling, habit formation

### Takeaway
No evidence was found that training on fixed weekdays beats a flexible rotation for outcomes or adherence. Habit research says what matters is **consistent repetition, low complexity, and stable cues**, not a calendar. A single missed opportunity did not derail habit formation (Lally 2010, per secondary sources). One small RCT found flexible nonlinear periodization (training-zone choice based on daily readiness) produced equal or better strength in beginners. Grade: "fixed days are required" is **unsupported (folklore)**; "consistency builds habit" is **moderate**; "flexible periodization works at least as well" is **weak** (one small RCT).

### Cited Findings
- Lally et al. 2010, European Journal of Social Psychology, doi 10.1002/ejsp.674: 96 participants repeated a self-chosen daily behavior in a consistent context for 84 days. Median time to the automaticity plateau was 66 days, with a range of 18–254 (254 is a model extrapolation beyond 84 days). More complex behaviors (exercise) took longer. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit); [The Behavioral Scientist, "What Lally et al. (2010) actually found"](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/how-long-to-form-a-habit)
- Lally (2026 interview): the 66-day figure is often taken out of context. It applies to some people for some habits, not most people most of the time. — [University of Surrey](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally)
- Missing a single day did not materially reduce habit formation in Lally 2010. This is reported by secondary sources only; not verified in the primary paper this session. — [Spring.org.uk summary](https://www.spring.org.uk/?p=7463)
- Kaushal & Rhodes 2015, J Behav Med, doi 10.1007/s10865-015-9640-7. 111 new gym members followed for 12 weeks. At least 4 sessions/week for 6 weeks was the minimum associated with establishing an exercise habit, and habit strength largely plateaued by week 6. Predictors of habit change: consistency (β = .21), low behavioral complexity (β = .19), environment (β = .17), affective judgments (β = .13). Small, single-setting sample. — [PubMed 25851609](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25851609/); [UVic PDF](https://www.uvic.ca/research/labs/bmed/assets/docs/Kaushal,%20Rhodes,%202015.pdf)
- Time-of-day consistency (National Weight Control Registry, n = 375): exercising at a consistent time was associated with more weekly exercise, and consistency mattered more than which hour. Cross-sectional. — [Schumacher et al. 2019, Obesity, doi 10.1002/oby.22535](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/oby.22535)
- A registered trial is testing rigid versus flexible exercise routines for habit durability. Its rationale states earlier work suggested rigid routines may produce *less* durable habits. No results found. — [NCT03634423, "Enduring Exercise Habits"](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03634423)
- McNamara & Stearne 2010, JSCR 24(8):2012–2017. 16 beginner college weight-training students were randomized to flexible nonlinear (FNL; the trainee chooses the day's training zone by readiness) or standard nonlinear periodization. FNL improved leg press more, with no difference in chest press or standing long jump. n = 8 per group. — [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4019534?lng=en); secondary summary in [PMC9987427](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9987427/)

### Inferences
- **Default: a rotation (next session in sequence), not weekday binding.** The user sets a *target frequency* (sessions/week). The app shows "next up" whenever they open it. If the user wants calendar days, offer them as optional reminders, not as the program's structure. This matches the evidence (consistency of repetition, not calendar identity) and removes the "missed Monday" failure mode.
- **Missed-day handling:** never skip ahead or mark a session "failed". The next session is simply the next one in the rotation. After a long gap (e.g., 2+ weeks), offer a lighter re-entry session or a small load reduction, then resume progression. Do not reset program state. The tolerance threshold is a design choice; the 6-week window in Conti 2026 is an operational definition, not a physiological finding.
- **Support habit cues without requiring them:** optionally ask "When do you usually train?" (morning, lunch, evening, varies) for reminder timing. A consistent time is associated with more exercise (correlational), so encourage it, but never enforce it.
- **Readiness flexibility (FNL-style):** allowing the user to swap today's heavy day for a lighter one on a bad day has weak but positive evidence and fits the autonomy and affect findings. It is reasonable as an in-session option, not a wizard question.
- **Folklore flag:** "You must train on fixed days." Not supported. The habit literature centers on repetition in a stable context, and Lally reports a single missed occasion doesn't break formation.
- Kaushal & Rhodes' "4 times a week for 6 weeks" should **not** become a default frequency. It is one small cohort and conflicts with the dropout data, which favor an achievable commitment. Use it, if at all, as an explanation of why consistency early matters.

### Gaps
- No RCT directly comparing fixed-weekday versus rotation-based scheduling on adherence was found.
- The Lally missed-day finding was not verified in the primary paper. The 18–254 range is reported by secondary sources.
- The full McNamara & Stearne text was not read (protocol details, effect sizes).

## 5. Session length and frequency: preferences versus adherence and outcomes

### Takeaway
When weekly volume is matched, training frequency has little independent effect on strength or hypertrophy. So frequency and session length are largely a **preference** decision that should fit the user's life. Adherence data say frequency and consistency matter more than session length. Long programs see more dropout, and "lack of time" is the top barrier. Grade: frequency is outcome-neutral at matched volume **moderate-strong** (meta-analyses, some methodological dispute); session-length effects on adherence **weak**.

### Cited Findings
- Grgic et al. 2018, Sports Medicine (strength): the apparent benefit of higher frequency came mostly from higher volume. In volume-equated studies, frequency had no significant effect (p = 0.421). — [Springer, doi 10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x)
- Grgic, Schoenfeld & Latella 2019, J Sci Med Sport (hypertrophy review): under volume-equated conditions, frequency has no pronounced effect on muscle mass gains. — [ECU repository](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/5665)
- Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2016 (10 studies): no hypertrophy benefit from manipulating session frequency when weekly volume is matched. — [Springer PDF, doi 10.1007/s40279-016-0543-8](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-016-0543-8.pdf)
- Saric et al. 2018: 3 vs 6 sessions/week, volume equated, gave similar adaptations in resistance-trained men. — [ResearchGate PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/Jozo-Grgic/publication/327435932_Resistance_Training_Frequencies_of_3_and_6_Times_Per_Week_Produce_Similar_Muscular_Adaptations_in_Resistance-Trained_Men/links/5bd26d6892851c6b278f311d/Resistance-Training-Frequencies-of-3-and-6-Times-Per-Week-Produce-Similar-Muscular-Adaptations-in-Resistance-Trained-Men.pdf)
- Contested: critics argue "volume" in these meta-analyses was sets × reps, not work, and that set-matched comparisons favor somewhat higher frequency. This is a practitioner blog, not peer-reviewed. — [Henselmans](https://mennohenselmans.com/training-frequency-2018-meta-analysis-review/)
- Adherence: in the Fitbod cohort, frequency and consistency were more predictive than session length, and longer sessions helped mainly in users who trained regularly. — [Conti et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2026.1855668/full)
- Longer *intervention duration* is associated with lower adherence (ten reviews), and lack of time is the most common barrier. Per-session length was not directly studied. — [Umbrella review, PMC7922504](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7922504/)

### Inferences
- **Ask:** "How many days a week can you *realistically* train?" (2–6) and "How long per session?" (e.g., about 30, 45, 60, 75+ min). These are the two highest-value wizard questions, since the evidence says outcomes are roughly frequency-neutral at matched volume, so the user's real constraint should drive structure.
- **Default:** for beginners who don't answer, use 3 days/week, about 45–60 min (full-body or rotation). This is a pragmatic default, not a measured optimum. Let the generator distribute weekly volume across whatever frequency is chosen, so a 2-day user gets longer sessions and a 5-day user gets shorter ones.
- **Folklore flag:** "Each muscle must be hit exactly 2×/week" or "more days = more gains." At matched volume, the evidence does not support strong frequency effects. The dispute is about whether *set-matched* higher frequency adds a little hypertrophy, which matters more to advanced lifters than to beginners.

### Gaps
- No trial was found randomizing per-session duration (e.g., 30 vs 60 min at matched weekly volume) with adherence as the outcome.
- No study was found on the *accuracy* of users' self-predicted availability versus actual completion, which would justify recommending "pick one fewer day than you think".

## 6. Goal-setting, self-monitoring, and behavior-change techniques in apps; onboarding friction

### Takeaway
Self-monitoring, feedback on behavior, and goal setting are the near-universal core of effective digital physical-activity interventions. Self-monitoring has the most consistent support, especially when combined with goals and feedback. Goal setting's *independent* moderating effect is inconsistent, and in one meta-regression near zero. SDT-based (autonomy-supportive) interventions have small but real effects on behavior. No direct evidence on onboarding length was found. Grade: self-monitoring + feedback **moderate**; goal setting as an independent active ingredient **weak/contested**; SDT interventions **moderate** (small effect, d ≈ 0.2).

### Cited Findings
- SDT intervention meta-analysis (Ntoumanis et al. 2021, Health Psychology Review 15(2):214–244, doi 10.1080/17437199.2020.1718529): 73 experimental studies. Small-to-medium changes in SDT constructs and health behaviors, small positive changes in physical and psychological health. Gains in need support and autonomous motivation were associated with behavior change. Effects were modest and heterogeneous. — [Ewha record](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-self-determination-theory-informed-interventio/); [Nottingham repository](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/3827204)
- Sheeran et al. 2020 (65 SDT intervention tests): overall d+ = 0.23. Significant effects for physical activity, sedentary behavior, diet, alcohol, and smoking, ranging from about 0.16 to 0.29. — [Birmingham record](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/self-determination-theory-interventions-for-health-behavior-chang/)
- Teixeira 2012 SR (66 studies): consistent positive relation between autonomous motivation and exercise. Mixed evidence for introjected regulation and health/fitness motives. Mostly non-experimental studies. — [IJBNPA 9:78](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1479-5868-9-78)
- Standalone digital BCIs, 18-RCT meta-analysis (npj Digital Medicine 2025): goal setting appeared in all included studies. Feedback on behavior (BCT 2.2, n = 17) and self-monitoring (BCT 2.3, n = 16) were the most common. The authors note goal-setting strategy differences relate to efficacy differences. Search-snippet level; the full text could not be fetched (paywall redirect). — [npj Digit Med, s41746-025-01827-4](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01827-4)
- Wearables + smartphone apps meta-analysis (2018): self-monitoring was an important and effective technique, especially combined with specific goal setting and performance feedback. — [Sports Medicine – Open, doi 10.1186/s40798-018-0157-9](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-018-0157-9)
- CVD-population PA apps, meta-regression: goal setting (behavior) had a near-zero moderating effect (β = 0.01, 90% CrI −0.15 to 0.19). Self-monitoring was the most-used BCT. — [PMC9261070](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9261070)
- Workplace mobile app meta-analysis: large within-group pre-post effect (d = 1.506) on PA, with self-monitoring, goal setting, and feedback the most reported BCTs. Pre-post designs cannot establish causality. — [BMC Digital Health 2026, s44247-026-00268-7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s44247-026-00268-7)
- JMIR mHealth SR (2020): low overall significant evidence for app effectiveness. Self-monitoring was positively associated with behavior change, with heterogeneous results. — [JMIR mHealth uHealth 2020;8(3):e17046](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2020/3/e17046/)
- Habit-formation DBCI review (JMIR 2024): a systematic review of digital intervention designs for habit formation. Content not extracted this session. — [JMIR 2024;e54375](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e54375)

### Inferences
- **Core loop the app should guarantee:** log (self-monitoring) → see progress against a target (feedback) → next target set automatically (goal). This loop is the evidence-backed part. The wizard needs only enough input to start it.
- **Goals:** ask *what* the user wants (strength, muscle, general fitness, endurance, a test or event) because it changes program structure. Avoid making them author numeric outcome goals in setup. The generator can derive sensible targets, and goal setting's independent effect is inconsistent.
- **Onboarding friction (inference, no direct evidence found):** the dropout data put the risk in the first 28 days. Every wizard question delays the first logged session. Keep the required questions to the few that change the program (goal, days/week, session length, equipment, experience, exclusions), defer everything else, and allow "skip, use defaults".
- **Autonomy support is a design stance, not a feature:** non-controlling copy, real choices, and rationale. The SDT effect is small (d ≈ 0.2) but consistent, and it costs nothing to apply.

### Gaps
- Not verified this session: Michie et al. 2013 BCT Taxonomy v1 (Ann Behav Med, doi 10.1007/s12160-013-9486-6), Romeo et al. 2019 (JMIR, smartphone apps and PA meta-analysis), and Mönninghoff et al. 2021 (JMIR, long-term mHealth PA effectiveness). The report writer should treat these as known canonical references that were not checked here.
- The PA-specific subgroup effect from Ntoumanis 2021 was not retrieved.
- No empirical study was found on the effect of wizard or onboarding length on fitness-app retention.

## 7. Explaining the "why": does rationale or education improve adherence?

### Takeaway
"Providing a meaningful rationale" is a standard component of autonomy-supportive interventions, and those bundles improve motivation quality and exercise. No trial was found that isolates rationale alone. Grade: **weak** for rationale as an independent ingredient; **moderate** for the autonomy-support bundle that includes it.

### Cited Findings
- SDT-informed exercise intervention pilot RCT (n = 64): increased exercise and motivation quality. The effect on exercise was mediated by motivation quality and identified regulation, i.e., valuing the activity's purpose. — [Weman-Josefsson et al. 2015, selfdeterminationtheory.org PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/2015_WemanJosefssonEtAl_Mechanisms.pdf)
- Autonomy-supportive behavior is typically operationalized as choice + meaningful rationale + non-controlling language + acknowledging the person's perspective, delivered as a bundle. — [J Behav Med 2016, s10865-016-9722-1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10865-016-9722-1); [Ntoumanis et al. 2021 (Birmingham PDF)](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/95428472/ibaa022.pdf)
- Teixeira 2012: identified regulation (personally valuing exercise outcomes) predicted early adoption. — [IJBNPA 9:78](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1479-5868-9-78)

### Inferences
- **Default:** short, optional "why" text attached to choices the generator makes (why this rep range, why a deload, why the next weight). It should be one line, expandable, and never a lesson the user must read to proceed. That targets identified regulation without adding friction.
- **Don't** front-load education in the wizard. Explain at the moment a decision affects the user, e.g., when a load is reduced.

### Gaps
- No RCT isolating rationale or education versus none on exercise adherence was found.
- The Silva et al. PESO women's weight-management RCT (autonomy-supportive intervention improved exercise adherence) was surfaced only by search snippet. The exact citation and URL were not confirmed.

## 8. Synthesis for the wizard: what to ask, what to let users choose, defaults, and folklore

### Takeaway
Ask only what changes the program. Propose a complete, editable plan. Let users choose *what* they do (exercises, modality, days) while the generator owns *how much and how it progresses*. Schedule as a rotation against a weekly frequency target, with forgiving gap handling.

### Cited Findings
- The early-consistency predictor (HR about 0.73 per SD of active days in the first 28 days) and the 10% 12-month beginner adherence rate set the stakes. — [Conti et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2026.1855668/full)
- Frequency is roughly outcome-neutral at matched volume. — [Grgic 2018](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x)
- Self-selection preserves strength outcomes and improves affect. — [PMC11653500](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11653500/)
- Affect during exercise predicts future activity. — [Rhodes & Kates 2015](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Can-the-Affective-Response-to-Exercise-Predict-and-Rhodes-Kates/7a57d236a8c5f1e516f4051f2ad53f5e60eab378)
- Habit formation rests on consistency and low complexity. — [Kaushal & Rhodes 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25851609/)

### Inferences
**Ask (required, each changes the program):**
| Question | Why | Evidence grade | Default if skipped |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary goal (strength / muscle / general fitness / endurance / specific test) | Changes structure | preference | General strength + fitness |
| Days per week you can realistically train | Outcome-neutral at matched volume, so fit life | moderate-strong (neutrality) | 3 |
| Typical session length | Time is the top barrier | weak (adherence), preference | 45–60 min |
| Equipment available | Feasibility | n/a | Full gym |
| Training experience | Starting load and progression | n/a | Beginner |
| Exercises to avoid (pain, dislike, no equipment) | Affect and autonomy | moderate (affect) | none |

**Let choose later (in-app, not the wizard):** exercise swaps within a pattern, cardio modality, reminder time, a readiness-based "lighter today" swap (FNL, weak), and favorite exercises.

**Generator owns, user can override:** loads, progression, deloads, and weekly volume distribution. Novices under-load when self-selecting (Ratamess). Show a one-line "why".

**Scheduling defaults:** rotation ("next session"), weekly frequency target, no fixed weekdays unless the user opts in for reminders, no "missed" state. Gap of 2+ weeks → offer a re-entry session; never reset progress.

**Folklore the evidence does not support:**
- "The most scientific program wins regardless of preference." Outcome differences among reasonable programs are small, and affect and enjoyment predict continued training.
- "You must train on fixed days." Habits rest on consistent repetition and cues, not calendar identity, and a single missed occasion doesn't break formation.
- "You need a coach to progress." Supervised vs unsupervised adherence CIs overlap. What's supported is that structure and progression rules help, and an app can provide those.
- "Hit each muscle exactly N×/week or you lose gains." This is volume-driven, and frequency is roughly neutral at matched volume.
- "66 days to form a habit." This is a median with huge variance, and the author herself says it is over-generalized.
- "Half of gym-goers quit within 6 months" as a precise fact. The figure is secondhand and population-dependent (15–70% across sources). The app-cohort data are worse for beginners at 12 months: only about 10% still adherent.

### Gaps
- All default numbers in the table are pragmatic design choices informed by the evidence, not values tested in trials. They should be presented to users as starting points.
