## 1. G02, the session estimate: is 45 s a realistic set, and what does the estimate leave out?

### Takeaway
45 s is a fair average for a bilateral set of about 10 to 12 reps. It runs about 2x long for heavy sets of 3 to 5 reps and too short for sets of 15 to 20 reps, for per-side (unilateral) sets and for timed holds over 45 s. Rest, not the work interval, dominates the total, so the flat figure matters less than it looks; the bigger misses are per-side sets, timed sets and the cool-down left out of the budget (W05). Verdict: **CHANGE TO a rep-scaled set time of about 3 s per rep (Wilk 2021 consensus: 2 to 5 s of time under tension per rep at volitional tempo), doubled for per-side sets, with timed sets counted at their planned duration; label the per-set setup allowance as practice.**

### Cited Findings
- A review of movement tempo puts volitional-tempo repetitions at "approximately 2–5 s of TUT for one repetition", and gives consensus set-level time under tension of 2 to 20 s for strength sets and 20 to 70 s for hypertrophy sets (its Table 3). It states that no TUT value has been shown to be optimal. — [Wilk, Zajac & Tufano 2021, Sports Med 2021, doi:10.1007/s40279-021-01465-2, PMC8310485](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8310485/)
- Measured concentric-only durations at volitional tempo, in 10 trained men (squat 1RM 175 kg, bench 1RM 140 kg), rose with load. Squat: 0.61 s at 40% 1RM, 0.83 s at 70%, 1.11 s at 90%. Bench: 0.47 s at 40%, 0.87 s at 70%, 1.43 s at 90%. Maximal intended velocity shortened the concentric by 0.06 to 0.42 s. These are single fresh reps, and eccentric time was not measured. — [Trybulski et al. 2022, Biol Sport 2022, doi:10.5114/biolsport.2022.106160, PMC9331330](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9331330/)
- In 12 resistance-trained men, self-selected rep duration yielded more reps than a fixed 2 s:2 s tempo with less TUT per rep, and total TUT per session did not differ. — [Nóbrega et al. 2018, JSCR 32(9):2419, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000002493, PMID 29481458](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29481458/). A citing paper reports the self-selected tempo as about 1.5 s per phase, so about 3 s per rep **[unverified; from a search summary, not the paper]**.
- ACSM's 2009 recommendation of a moderate tempo for untrained lifters (2 s concentric, 2 s eccentric, so about 4 s per rep) is quoted in a training study that found self-selected and 2:2 tempos equally effective over 8 weeks in untrained men. — [PeerJ 2020 training study (doi:10.7717/peerj.8697), PMC7061915](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7061915/)
- A narrative review of time-efficient training describes the typical program as 2 to 4 sets of 8 to 10 exercises for 3 to 12 reps with 2 to 5 min rest, and says that "including a warm-up and stretching, traditional strength training programs often exceed an hour". It does not give a per-set work time. — [Iversen et al. 2021, Sports Med, doi:10.1007/s40279-021-01490-1, PMC8449772](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772/)
- Code check: `SessionBudget.SET_SECONDS = 45` is applied to every set kind (ramp, top, back-off, work) and every tracking type (weighted, reps, timed). Rest from `RestPolicy` (ramp 90 s, top 180 s, back-off 120 s, weighted work 90 s, light 60 s) is added after every set, the last set of each exercise included, so the last rest also stands in for the move to the next exercise. Warm-up is a flat 300 s. Nothing is budgeted for the cool-down. — `domain/.../planner/SessionBudget.kt:22-35`, `standards/RestPolicy.kt`

### Inferences
- At 2 to 4 s per rep (Wilk; Trybulski's concentric plus a typical 1 to 2 s eccentric), the work part of a set is about 10 to 20 s for 5 reps, 20 to 40 s for 10, 30 to 60 s for 15 and 40 to 80 s for 20. Unracking, bracing and re-racking add an unknown amount on top. So 45 s overestimates heavy low-rep sets by about 20 to 30 s, is about right at 10 to 12 reps, and underestimates 15 to 20 reps by 0 to 35 s.
- A per-side set (split squat 8 a side, single-leg RDL 10 a side) is two sets of work back to back, about 50 to 80 s. A 60 s plank or a 30 to 45 s carry counted at 45 s is off by its own planned length. Those two cases are where the flat 45 s errs most, and they are common in Run strength blocks (R48: single-leg accessory) and older-adult days (O08, O09: balance holds, carries).
- Ramp sets carry the 45 s figure plus 90 s of rest each. In practice light ramp sets go quicker than working sets, so a three-ramp main may be overbudgeted by 1 to 3 min. That error and the low-rep error both run long, so a Strength day tends to be overestimated and a high-rep Muscle or General day underestimated. The dose ladder therefore trims a Strength day more than needed and a high-rep day less than needed.
- Since rest is 60 to 180 s against 15 to 60 s of work, a 30 s error per set over 15 to 20 sets moves a 60 min estimate by roughly 7 to 10 min. That decides whether the dose ladder cuts an accessory, so it matters even though rest dominates.
- Suggested replacement (the per-rep figure is evidence-based; the setup allowance is practice): set seconds = setup (about 10 s, practice) + reps x 3 s (Wilk 2–5 s; self-selected about 3 s), with reps x sides for per-side exercises, and a timed set's planned duration in place of reps x 3 s. Ramp sets could keep a shorter rest in the estimate if the timer allows it.

### Gaps
- I found no time-motion study that measured whole-set duration (setup through re-rack) in recreational gym users, nor any study of real transition time between exercises. The setup allowance has no source.
- I found no direct measurement of self-selected eccentric duration across loads; the about 1.5 s per phase figure is unverified.

## 2. Warm-up and cool-down rules (W01 to W05) and the sources issue #574 cites

### Takeaway
All three citations in issue #574 check out. Fradkin 2010 found warm-up improved performance in 79% of criteria. Behm 2016 found a -1.1% performance cost for static stretches under 60 s per muscle, against -4.6% at 60 s or more. Van Hooren & Peake 2018 found an active cool-down "largely ineffective" for recovery. For lifting in particular, a general warm-up adds little once the main lifts are ramped: it raised 1RM in one small trial but did not raise reps in submaximal training. So the 2-minute general item plus 4 or 5 dynamic moves is defensible practice, but the dose per move has no evidence and should be labeled practice. The cool-down's 30 s stretches are supported per stretch (Bandy 1997), but the weekly dose is below what improves range of motion, and leaving the cool-down out of the minutes promise is a product inconsistency, not an evidence question.

### Cited Findings
- **Fradkin 2010 (verified).** 32 high-quality studies (PEDro 6.5 to 9, mean 7.6), warm-ups that included more than stretching; warm-up improved performance in **79% of the criteria examined**, with "little evidence to suggest that warming-up is detrimental." The authors note few well-conducted RCTs. Populations were mixed and mostly athletic. The DOI in the issue is correct; the PMID is 19996770. — [Fradkin, Zazryn & Smoliga 2010, JSCR, doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181c643a0, PMID 19996770](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19996770/)
- **Behm 2016 (verified).** Testing immediately after stretching showed static stretching (SS) **-3.7%**, dynamic stretching (DS) **+1.3%** and PNF **-4.4%**. Dose-response: **≥60 s per muscle group -4.6%, <60 s -1.1%**. SS gave **+2.2%** at long muscle lengths. When post-stretch dynamic activity was included, there was "no clear performance effect". SS and PNF had no clear effect on all-cause or overuse injury, and no injury data exist for DS. ROM gains typically lasted under 30 min. Population: healthy active individuals. — [Behm et al. 2016, Appl Physiol Nutr Metab, doi:10.1139/apnm-2015-0235, PMID 26642915](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26642915/)
- **McGowan 2015.** A narrative review: active warm-up raises temperature and metabolic readiness and can elicit post-activation potentiation, and warm-up design has developed "largely on a trial-and-error basis." The abstract gives no minute or intensity figures, and I could not reach the full text to check any. — [McGowan et al. 2015, Sports Med, doi:10.1007/s40279-015-0376-x, PMID 26400696](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26400696/)
- **Warm-up for resistance training.** In 15 men doing 4 sets of bench press, squat and curl at 80% 1RM to failure, neither a specific warm-up, a 10-min aerobic warm-up nor both changed total reps or the fatigue index against no warm-up. — [Ribeiro et al. 2014, Percept Mot Skills, doi:10.2466/25.29.PMS.119c17z7, PMID 25153744](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25153744/)
- In 13 trained participants, a 20-min bike warm-up at 60% HRmax plus a specific warm-up raised leg-press 1RM **8.4%** over the specific warm-up alone. — [Abad et al. 2011, JSCR 25(8):2242, doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181e8611b, PMID 21544000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21544000/)
- Iversen 2021 advises: "General warm-up should not be prioritized when time is of an essence"; a "specific warm-up can be useful when training with heavy loads (> 80% of 1RM)"; and the first reps of a higher-rep set act as their own warm-up. — [Iversen et al. 2021, PMC8449772](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772/)
- A 33-study meta-analysis (n = 921) found that raising muscle temperature improves rate-dependent function (voluntary **+3.7%/°C**, ES 0.28) but not maximum force (-0.2%/°C, ES 0.08), with no difference between active and passive warming and no effect of warm-up specificity. — [Wilson, Nunes & Blazevich 2025, J Sport Health Sci, doi:10.1016/j.jshs.2025.101024, PMC12357318](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12357318/)
- **Van Hooren & Peake 2018 (verified).** An active cool-down is "largely ineffective" for same-day and next-day performance. It does not appear to prevent injuries and does not significantly reduce soreness or speed recovery of range of motion, stiffness or markers of muscle damage. Preliminary evidence says it does not blunt long-term adaptation. The review defines an active cool-down as 5 to 15 min of low to moderate exercise and advises keeping it under about 30 min to protect glycogen resynthesis. Static stretching before or after exercise "does not reduce muscle soreness." — [Van Hooren & Peake 2018, Sports Med 48(7):1575, doi:10.1007/s40279-018-0916-2, PMC5999142](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5999142/)
- **Stretch dose for flexibility.** In 93 adults with tight hamstrings stretching 5 days a week for 6 weeks, 30 s held as well as 60 s, and once a day as well as three times. — [Bandy, Irion & Briggler 1997, Phys Ther 77(10):1090, doi:10.1093/ptj/77.10.1090, PMID 9327823](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9327823/)
- A 23-study review found that weekly stretching time drives range-of-motion gains, with ≥5 min per week per muscle and ≥5 days a week of static stretching beneficial; time within a single session did not matter. — [Thomas et al. 2018, Int J Sports Med, doi:10.1055/s-0044-101146, PMID 29506306](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29506306/)
- ACSM recommends flexibility exercises for each major muscle-tendon group, **60 s total per exercise, ≥2 days a week**. — [Garber et al. 2011, ACSM position stand, MSSE, doi:10.1249/MSS.0b013e318213fefb, PMID 21694556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/)

### Inferences
- Issue #574 says "stretching after training does improve flexibility over time" and cites Van Hooren & Peake. That review does not say it; the claim is supported by Bandy 1997, Thomas 2018 and Garber 2011, so the citation should move.
- **W01** (120 s general item, then 4 or 5 dynamic moves ranked by the day's muscles). **LABEL AS PRACTICE.** The evidence favors some warm-up (Fradkin 79%) and dynamic over long static stretching (Behm DS +1.3%), and says the specific ramp sets on main lifts carry most of the value for lifting (Ribeiro; Iversen). No study tests 120 s, or 4 to 5 moves. The one study showing a general warm-up adding 1RM used 20 min (Abad), so the 2 min item is a time-saving compromise, not a dose from evidence. That is fine, because the app's ramp sets remain.
- **W02** (reps or seconds per move). **LABEL AS PRACTICE.** I found no study that sets a dose per move for leg swings, squats, arm circles and the like. Behm's <60 s per muscle threshold concerns static stretching and does not apply.
- **W03** (30 s stretches, 180 to 300 s total). **KEEP 30 s per stretch** (Bandy 1997: 30 s = 60 s), but **LABEL AS PRACTICE** for the total. At 2 to 4 sessions a week, 30 s per muscle gives 1 to 2 min per muscle per week, under ACSM's 60 s per exercise on ≥2 days and Thomas's ≥5 min per week. If the cool-down is ever framed as building flexibility, **CHANGE TO 2 x 30 s (60 s) per muscle** (Garber 2011). The owner's no-claim copy rule makes this low-stakes.
- **W04** (fixed muscle lists for cardio-only days). **LABEL AS PRACTICE.** It is anatomically sensible, and Van Hooren notes some evidence that a cool-down should use the muscles just worked, but no source picks the lists.
- **W05** (cool-down outside the estimate). **CHANGE:** when the cool-down switch is on, add its 3 to 5 min to the session estimate, or say plainly that the minutes cover warm-up and training only. Evidence cannot settle this. It is a promise-keeping issue: a user who picks 45 min and does the suggested cool-down finishes at 48 to 50 min plus estimate error.
- **G02's 300 s warm-up.** **KEEP** as practice. It matches what the card shows (120 s plus 4 or 5 moves is about 4 to 6 min), and ramp sets are budgeted separately.

### Gaps
- McGowan 2015 full text not read; no minute or intensity recommendation from it is verified.
- No trial compares a 2 min general warm-up plus dynamic moves against ramp sets alone before submaximal lifting in recreational lifters.
- No evidence on dynamic warm-up dose per move (reps or seconds).

## 3. Concurrent rules (C03 to C08): ordering, spacing, the 20 min run reserve, and strength blocks on run plans

### Takeaway
Same visit versus separate days does not change maximal strength or hypertrophy (Schumann 2022). Only explosive strength suffers when the modes share a session (SMD -0.31 with ≤20 min between them, no penalty at ≥3 h), and trained lifters may lose some lower-body 1RM in same-session designs. Lifting first adds about 4 kg or 7% of lower-body strength and leaves VO2max unchanged. So C03's same-visit default at three or fewer days is defensible for general users, and the 20 min easy run in C05 is fine as practice: WHO counts bouts of any length, and ACSM's vigorous floor is 20 min. The weak spots: C04's 1+1 split gives a lift-primary user only one strength day (below WHO's two), and C08 and the power case need separation, not order. Two strength sessions a week for runners is supported (Blagrove: 2 to 3, with 1 enough to maintain in season); the 25 min block length is practice.

### Cited Findings
- **Schumann 2022** (43 studies, 1,090 participants, healthy adults, supervised ≥4 weeks, strength-only controls). Maximal strength SMD **-0.06** (95% CI -0.20 to 0.09), explosive strength **-0.28** (-0.48 to -0.08), hypertrophy **-0.01** (-0.16 to 0.18). The explosive-strength penalty appeared within the same session, defined as ≤20 min between modes (SMD **-0.31**, 95% CI -0.62 to -0.01, p = 0.043), and not when sessions were ≥3 h apart. Maximal strength and hypertrophy showed no moderation by modality (cycling 24 studies, running 16), by frequency (>5 vs <5 weekly sessions), by training status, by age, or by same session vs same day vs different days. Order subgroups were to be compared "when a sufficient number of studies were available". — [Schumann et al. 2022, Sports Med 52:601, doi:10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7, PMC8891239](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8891239/)
- **Eddens 2018** (10 studies, healthy adults, ≥5 weeks). Resistance before endurance improved lower-body dynamic strength by a weighted mean difference of **6.91%** (95% CI 1.96 to 11.87). There was no order effect on lower-body hypertrophy (1.15%, CI -1.56 to 3.87), static strength (-0.04%), VO2max or body fat. — [Eddens, van Someren & Howatson 2018, Sports Med 48(1):177, doi:10.1007/s40279-017-0784-1, PMC5752732](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5752732/). The first review's notes cite the correct PMID (28917030); the abstract's DOI ends 0784-1.
- **Murlasits 2018.** Strength first gave lower-body 1RM **+3.96 kg** (95% CI 0.81 to 7.10); order had no effect on VO2max (**0.39 ml/kg/min**, CI -1.03 to 1.81). — [Murlasits, Kneffel & Thalib 2018, J Sports Sci 36(11):1212, doi:10.1080/02640414.2017.1364405, PMID 28783467](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28783467/)
- **Wilson 2012** (21 studies, 422 effect sizes). Concurrent training ES: hypertrophy 0.85 vs 1.23 for strength alone, strength 1.44 vs 1.76, power 0.55 vs 0.91. Running, but not cycling, significantly reduced hypertrophy and strength. Endurance frequency (r -0.26 to -0.35) and duration (r -0.29 to -0.75) correlated negatively with outcomes. These correlation figures are now verified against the abstract (the first review flagged them). — [Wilson et al. 2012, JSCR 26(8):2293, doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e31823a3e2d, PMID 22002517](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22002517/)
- Lower-body 1RM penalty in trained people ES -0.35, same-session designs only (from the first review, not re-read here). — [Petré et al. 2021, PMC8053170](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8053170)
- **Effect of lifting on the run that follows.** Residual fatigue from a resistance session can impair the quality of endurance sessions for hours to days through altered recruitment, worse running economy, soreness and lower glycogen; the link to long-term endurance adaptation is "not fully established". — [Doma, Deakin & Bentley 2017, Sports Med, doi:10.1007/s40279-017-0758-3, PMID 28702901](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28702901/)
- **Blagrove 2018** (24 studies of middle- and long-distance runners with ≥6 months' experience, 469 participants aged 17 to 45, PEDro 4 to 6). Running economy improved 2 to 8%. "The majority of studies that demonstrated improvements … scheduled ST 2–3 times per week". One study used 1 session a week and still improved economy over 8 weeks. Beattie's 40-week study kept gains when runners dropped from 2 sessions a week to 1 in season. Heavy RT was 2 to 6 sets of 3 to 10 reps above 70% 1RM; plyometrics 1 to 6 exercises, 30 to 228 foot contacts a session; Støren used half-squats only, 4 x 4 at 4RM, 3 times a week. The review recommends "a recovery period of > 3 h … following high-intensity running training before ST", and notes that strength placed right after low-intensity, non-depleting runs may be a good arrangement. — [Blagrove, Howatson & Hayes 2018, Sports Med 48(5):1117, doi:10.1007/s40279-017-0835-7, PMC5889786](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889786/)
- **Minimum aerobic session length.** The PAGAC systematic review (29 articles) found that bouts under 10 min are associated with health outcomes, including all-cause mortality; randomized trials tested only ≥10 min bouts. — [Jakicic et al. 2019, MSSE, doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000001933, PMC6527142](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6527142/). WHO 2020: "bouts of any duration now count", with a target of 150 to 300 min moderate or 75 to 150 min vigorous a week. — [Bull et al. 2020, BJSM 54:1451, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955)
- ACSM: moderate cardio ≥30 min a day on ≥5 days (≥150 min a week), or vigorous ≥20 min a day on ≥3 days (≥75 min a week), and adults below these targets "still can benefit". — [Garber et al. 2011, PMID 21694556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/)
- For VO2peak, three 10 min sessions a week of 3 x 20 s all-out sprints matched 50 min moderate sessions (about +19%, small n, sedentary men) (from the first review). — [Gillen et al. 2016, PMC4846072](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4846072)
- A minimum of ≥4 weekly sets per muscle in the 6 to 15 RM range is the time-efficient floor. — [Iversen et al. 2021, PMC8449772](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772/)

### Inferences (per-rule verdicts)
- **C03** (Together: separate days at 4+ days, same visit below). **LABEL AS PRACTICE.** Schumann found no maximal strength or hypertrophy difference between same session, same day and different days, so the cut at 4 days is a convenience choice and does no harm for general or hypertrophy users. Exception: for a Strength sub-goal with A year or more of training, and for any sprint or power goal, the evidence favors separate days or ≥3 h apart (Schumann explosive SMD -0.31 same session; Petré ES -0.35 in trained lifters). **CHANGE** the default for those users to separate days whenever days allow.
- **C04** (separate-day splits 1+0, 1+1, 2+1, 2+2, 3+2, 3+3). **LABEL AS PRACTICE**, but flag 1+1. A lift-primary user on 2 separate days gets one strength day, under WHO's and ACSM's "2 or more days" (strong). Blagrove's 1 session a week maintains gains but does not build them. Since Both defaults to same visit below 4 days, this only reaches users who choose separate days. Suggest same visit at 2 days, or keep two strength exposures.
- **C05** (Both, lift first, same visit: 20 min run reserve, else a 20 min easy run). **LABEL AS PRACTICE; KEEP 20 min.** No study sets a minimum effective run length. WHO and PAGAC count any bout (Jakicic 2019), ACSM's vigorous daily floor is 20 min, and 10 min sprint-interval sessions raised VO2peak (Gillen). Lift first is supported for lower-body strength (Eddens +6.91%, Murlasits +3.96 kg) and does not cost VO2max. Two caveats. First, 3 x 20 min easy runs give 60 moderate min a week, well under WHO's 150; any copy or weekly display should not imply the plan meets the aerobic floor. Second, at 45 min, 20 min for the run and 5 min warm-up leave about 20 min of lifting, roughly 6 to 8 sets at 90 to 180 s rest. Spread over three days that clears Iversen's ≥4 sets per muscle a week only for the muscles the mains cover; it is a minimum-dose plan, not a hypertrophy plan. Doma 2017 suggests the run after lifting will feel harder; an easy run tolerates that.
- **C06** (cardio first, same visit: run plan plus two strength blocks). **KEEP 2 blocks a week** (Blagrove: 2 to 3 sessions in most positive studies, 1 enough to maintain). **LABEL the 25 min block length (R49) AS PRACTICE.** It fits Blagrove's protocols, such as Støren's 4 x 4 half-squats, but no study sets a session length. Placement matters more than length: the blocks sit on easy-run days, which matches Blagrove's note on strength after low-intensity runs. Never put a block straight after an interval or threshold session (Blagrove: >3 h after high-intensity running). The missing plyometrics (R48) remain the bigger gap. Blagrove includes plyometrics in most effective programs (30 to 228 contacts a session), and the first review already said so.
- **C07** (Run and Sprint use Strength lift rules). **KEEP / LABEL AS PRACTICE.** Blagrove's effective programs used heavy loads (>70% 1RM, 3 to 10 reps), which Strength rules match.
- **C08** (Both with a sprint sub-goal: sprint layout, Which first and Together not asked). **LABEL AS PRACTICE.** Not asking "Which first" is consistent with the evidence. For power, distance between the modes matters more than order (Schumann: the same-session penalty is gone at ≥3 h). The sprint layout should keep sprints first or on separate days and never place heavy legs the day before speed work, as the first review's 4-day row says.
- **Running vs cycling.** The signals conflict: Wilson 2012 found running worse, Schumann 2022 found no modality effect across 40 studies, and the first review noted Wilson's difference may hinge on an outlier [unverified]. Keep modality as user preference. Nothing here argues for steering Both users off running.

### Gaps
- Schumann's order subgroup result (aerobic first vs strength first within a session) is not reported in the text I read; the order evidence rests on Eddens and Murlasits.
- No study identifies a minimum length for an easy run that still drives aerobic adaptation in recreational adults; 20 min is coaching convention, consistent with ACSM's vigorous floor.
- No trial compares 25 min with longer strength blocks for runners; Llanos-Lagos et al.'s dose-response meta-analyses on running economy were not reached in this pass.

## 4. General rules G03 to G05, and which rules would most mislead a real user

### Takeaway
G03 (6:00 per km), G04 (setup option ranges and defaults) and G05 (equipment presets) are product defaults, and evidence can at most bound them: label them practice. Ranked by how far they would mislead a real user: the session estimate's handling of per-side and timed sets and of high-rep days (G02), plus the unbudgeted cool-down (W05), break the minutes promise most often. Next come the 1+1 split leaving a lift-primary user with one strength day (C04) and the same-visit default for trained strength or power users (C03). The warm-up and cool-down doses mislead no one as long as the app keeps its no-claim copy.

### Cited Findings
- WHO 2020 and ACSM 2011 set ≥2 strength days a week on all major muscle groups (strong recommendation). — [Bull et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955); [Garber et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/)
- The first review's required-question defaults (3 days, 45 to 60 min, full gym, beginner, general strength plus fitness) are its own synthesis, not a study result. — `docs/briefs/wizard-evidence-2026-11-08/report.md`
- Typical program sessions "often exceed an hour" once warm-up and stretching are included. — [Iversen et al. 2021, PMC8449772](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772/)

### Inferences
- **G03** (0.36 s per meter, 6:00 per km, for distance sets with no time aim). **LABEL AS PRACTICE.** It is plausible as a recreational easy pace, but many new runners' easy pace is slower, around 7:00 to 8:00 per km, so the estimate undercounts their time and the day overruns. Prefer the user's logged pace once any run exists. No primary source located (see Gaps).
- **G04** (days 1 to 6, minutes 30 to 90, defaults 3 days, 60 min, Full gym, New). **LABEL AS PRACTICE.** The 3-day default meets WHO's ≥2 strength days. The 60 min default sits at the top of the first review's 45 to 60 min band.
- **G05** (equipment presets). **LABEL AS PRACTICE** (product decision; nothing to verify).
- **Most misleading, in order:**
  1. G02 with W05: a 45 or 60 min choice that routinely runs 5 to 10 min over on high-rep, per-side or timed-set days, or once the suggested cool-down is done.
  2. C04's 1+1 split: one strength day a week, against a strong guideline.
  3. C03 for trained Strength and power users: the same-visit default where the only measurable interference sits, though the effect is small (SMD -0.31 explosive; ES -0.35 lower-body 1RM in trained lifters).
  4. C05's 20 min run: harmless as training, but 3 x 20 easy min is 60 of WHO's 150 weekly minutes, which matters only if the app implies the aerobic floor is met.

### Gaps
- I found no primary study of typical recreational easy-run pace (for G03). Race-result aggregates exist but give race pace, not easy pace, and are not peer-reviewed.
- The setup and transition time between sets and exercises in real gyms is unmeasured in the literature I found.
