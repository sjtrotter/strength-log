# Sprint and speed training programming (60 m to 400 m) for recreational and competitive non-elite runners

Evidence grades used throughout: **strong** (consistent meta-analytic evidence from RCTs), **moderate** (meta-analyses with mixed designs or real methodological caveats, or several consistent RCTs), **weak/contested** (small trials, observational data, conflicting reanalyses, or coaching consensus with little controlled testing), **preference** (no outcome difference expected, so the user's taste or logistics should decide).

Scope note: about 18 searches and fetches this session. Two Springer pages (the 2026 resisted-sprint umbrella review and the Haugen review on springer.com) were behind an auth redirect. Haugen 2019 was read in full on PMC. Li et al. 2025 was read in full. Alcaraz 2018, Seitz 2014, Rumpf 2016, van Dyk 2019, the 2021 Nordic reappraisal, Cuthbert 2020 and the hand-timing studies were read at abstract or search-snippet level only. A bullet marked "(snippet)" comes from a search summary, not the paper itself. A large caveat runs through everything below: most sprint-training trials study young, male team-sport athletes (mostly soccer) over 0-30 m. Very few trials study recreational adults training for 200-400 m. Haugen et al. 2019 say this explicitly.

## 1. Acceleration vs max-velocity vs speed-endurance training: what works for 60-100 m vs 200-400 m

### Takeaway
Running fast is the best-supported way to get faster. Sprint-specific training showed a moderate effect in a 48-study review (Rumpf 2016, ES about -1.0). The type of work should match the distance. Short accelerations and flying sprints at >95% effort, with near-full rest, are the core for 40 yd to 100 m. For 200-400 m, add sprint-specific endurance (80-150 m at >95%, long rest) and speed-endurance work. Grades: "sprint training improves sprint times" **moderate-strong** (consistent, but mostly team-sport youth over short distances). Specific session categories and their distances and rests come from **coaching consensus** (weak but coherent). Speed-endurance work for 200-400 m: **weak-moderate** (soccer and endurance-athlete trials, not sprinters).

### Cited Findings
- Rumpf et al. 2016 (JSCR, brief review of 48 studies, 1,485 subjects): specific sprint training had a moderate effect on sprint performance (ES = -1.00; -3.23% change). Combined methods averaged a small ES of -0.51 across distances. (Abstract and snippet level.) — [Rumpf et al. 2016, JSCR 30(6)](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2016/06000/effect_of_different_sprint_training_methods_on.35.aspx); [ECU repository](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/1872/)
- Haugen, Seiler, Sandbakk & Tønnessen 2019 (Sports Med Open 5:44, doi:10.1186/s40798-019-0221-0) integrate the science with best practice. Their Table 2 gives these session categories (intensity as % of max velocity):
  - **Acceleration:** 10-50 m, >98%, 2-7 min rest, 100-300 m per session, 48 h before the next hard session.
  - **Max velocity (flying):** 10-30 m flying, after a 20-60 m build-up; >98%; 4-15 min rest; 50-150 m per session; 48-72 h.
  - **Sprint-specific endurance:** 80-150 m, >95%, 8-30 min rest, 300-900 m per session; 48-72 h.
  - **Speed endurance:** 60-80 m, 90-95%, 2-4 min between reps and 8-15 min between series, 600-2,000 m per session; 48-72 h.
  - **Resisted:** 10-30 m, 80-95%, 3-6 min rest, 50-200 m.
  - **Assisted:** 10-30 m, ≤105%, 5-15 min rest, ≤100 m.
  - **Tempo:** 100-300 m, 60-70%, 1-3 min rest, 1,000-2,000 m per session; 24 h.
  — [Haugen et al. 2019, PMC6872694](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)
- Haugen 2019: athletes hold top speed for only about 10-30 m. Young athletes might do 20 m from a crouch with 2 min rest; elite sprinters might do 40 m from blocks with about 7 min rest. The sprint-specific endurance rule of thumb is 1-2 min of rest per second of maximal sprinting. An elite example is 4-6 × 150 m with 20-30 min rest. — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)
- Haugen 2019, "polarized" concept from best practice: sprinting should be either ≥95% or <70% of max velocity. The 70-95% band is generally avoided. End a session when performance drops off or technique deteriorates. — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)
- Haugen 2019 on periodization by distance:
  - "Short-to-long" (Charlie Francis) starts with short sprints and power work, then moves toward max velocity and sprint-specific endurance.
  - "Long-to-short" is typically used by long-sprint (400 m) specialists.
  - The review gives no separate numeric prescriptions for 200 m or 400 m.
  — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)
- Haugen 2019 caveat: most sprint research uses young team-sport athletes doing short maximal sprints. Elite sprinters train across a much wider range of distances, intensities and recoveries, so the research and practice bases differ. — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)
- Bangsbo-framework definitions:
  - **Speed endurance production:** about 30 s all-out efforts, with rest more than 6× the work duration.
  - **Speed endurance maintenance:** work:rest ratio below 1:4, so lower intensity.
  — [GSSI SSE #126 (Mohr & Iaia)](https://www.gssiweb.org/en/sports-science-exchange/article/sse-126-physiological-basis-of-fatigue-resistance-training-in-competitive-football)
- Iaia & Bangsbo 2010 review (snippet): speed endurance training improved intense short-duration and repeated high-intensity performance lasting 30 s to 4 min, even in endurance-trained athletes on reduced volume. — [Iaia & Bangsbo, via PMC4578774 / search summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4578774)
- RCT in 18 sub-elite soccer players (4 weeks, in-season; snippet): adding speed endurance production and maintenance training both improved high-intensity performance, and production had the larger effect. — [KU publication record](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/the-effect-of-two-speed-endurance-training-regimes-on-performance)
- 2025 Scand J Med Sci Sports review of speed endurance training: most interventions used running or cycling, and not all used sport-specific field tests. (Snippet; full text not read.) — [Speed Endurance Training to Improve Performance (PDF)](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Speed-Endurance-Training-to-Improve-Performance.pdf)
- Realistic improvement rates:
  - Top-100 world sprinters in their early 20s improve about 0.1-0.2% per year.
  - Norwegian national-level sprinters improved 1.3-1.4% from age 18.
  - Peak age is typically 25-26.
  — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default, goal 40 yd to 100 m / sport speed:** 2 sprint sessions per week, at least 48 h apart.
  - **Session A, acceleration:** 4-8 × 10-30 m from a 2- or 3-point start; 2-3 min rest for short reps, about 1 min per 10 m as a working rule. Total 100-200 m for a novice.
  - **Session B, max velocity:** 3-5 × 10-20 m flying after a 20-30 m build-up; 4-6 min rest. Total 50-100 m flying.
  - **Optional third day:** tempo at <70% for conditioning.
  - Stop the session when rep times slow noticeably (see Q6) or form breaks.
  - Grade: structure is coaching consensus. That the dose works is moderate.
- **Generator default, goal 200-400 m:** keep one acceleration/max-velocity session, because top speed sets the ceiling. Replace the second session with a sprint-specific endurance session, for example 3-4 × 100-150 m at >95% with 8-12 min rest (1-2 min per second of sprinting), or speed-endurance series of 60-80 m at 90-95%. Add 1 tempo session. For the 400 m specifically, the coaching tradition supports a long-to-short progression.
- **Progression:** progress volume within the Haugen session ranges first: add reps, then distance (for example acceleration 10→20→30 m, sprint endurance 80→120→150 m). Keep intensity maximal and rest long. Use a 3:1 or 2:1 loading/deload pattern (Haugen reports this as best practice). Set expectations: a trained adult may gain only a few percent per season, so the app should not promise big time drops after the beginner phase.
- **Ask the user:** target event or distance (≤60 m / 100 m / 200 m / 400 m / sport); current best time if known; sprint experience (none / some / track background); days available; surface (track, grass, turf, road); any recent hamstring injury.
- **Folklore flags:**
  - "More volume is always better." Not supported. Best practice caps acceleration at 100-300 m and max velocity at 50-150 m per session, and ends sessions when performance drops.
  - "Sprinting at 80-90% builds speed." The polarized best-practice view avoids the 70-95% band for speed work. Sub-max running is tempo/conditioning, not speed training. Grade: weak (coaching consensus).

### Gaps
- No controlled trials were found on recreational adult 200 m or 400 m runners that compare session types. The 200-400 m defaults extrapolate from coaching practice and soccer/endurance speed-endurance trials.
- Rumpf 2016's distance-specific effect sizes (0-10 m vs 30+ m) were not visible at abstract level.
- No dose-response meta-analysis of total weekly sprint volume for non-elite adults was found.

## 2. Rest intervals and weekly sprint volume; resisted and assisted sprints

### Takeaway
Speed work needs long rest: 2-7 min for accelerations and 4-15 min for flying sprints (Haugen best practice). Short-rest sprinting trains a different quality (speed endurance or repeated-sprint ability), not top speed. Resisted sled sprints improve short sprints about as well as normal sprinting, perhaps more for 0-10 m acceleration. Per the Alcaraz 2018 meta-analysis, dose (≥160 m per session, 2-3×/week, ≥6 weeks) matters more than load. Grades: long rest for speed quality is **moderate** (physiology and best practice, little direct RCT). Resisted sprinting ≈ unresisted for overall sprint gains is **moderate**. Resisted > unresisted for 0-10 m is **weak-moderate** (effect present but with publication-bias concerns). Assisted sprints: **weak**, little evidence, with injury concern.

### Cited Findings
- Alcaraz et al. 2018, Sports Med (doi:10.1007/s40279-018-0947-8), meta-analysis of 13 studies searched through January 2018 (abstract/snippet):
  - Load intensity did not determine the size of the sprint improvement.
  - Effective programs used about 160 m or more per session, about 2,680 m over the whole program, 2-3 sessions per week, for at least 6 weeks.
  - Rigid surfaces appeared to enhance the effect.
  — [Alcaraz et al. 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-018-0947-8)
- Cross et al. published a comment on Alcaraz 2018, and the authors replied. Contents not retrieved. — [Cross et al. comment](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-018-01038-w); [Authors' reply](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-018-01037-x)
- Li, Chen & Zhang 2025, Front Physiol 16:1711992 (doi:10.3389/fphys.2025.1711992): 16 studies, 404 participants, mostly male soccer players.
  - Linear sprint (0-30 m): resisted SMD 0.65 (0.48-0.82); unresisted SMD 0.42 (0.26-0.58). Between-method difference not significant (p = 0.057).
  - 0-10 m: resisted outperformed unresisted, SMD 0.81 (0.49-1.13), p < 0.001.
  - Change of direction favored resisted (p = 0.043).
  - Adults: SMD 0.72 (0.52-0.92). Youth: 0.81.
  - Programs ran 4-12 weeks at 1-4 sessions per week. Loads in the included studies ranged from 10-20% body weight up to 80% body weight. No load moderator analysis was done.
  - Caveats: trim-and-fill pooled SMD fell to 0.55 (95% CI -0.12 to 1.22, crossing zero). Egger's test was significant (p = 0.003) despite GRADE listing publication bias as "not suspected". There are several internal table inconsistencies.
  — [Li et al. 2025, PMC12689304](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12689304/)
- A 2026 Sports Medicine umbrella review of resisted sprint training exists (doi:10.1007/s40279-026-02538-w), but its content could not be accessed. — [Umbrella review 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-026-02538-w)
- A 2024 team-sport meta-analysis concluded that sled training over 5-50 m decreases sprint times (abstract-level). — [J Bodyw Mov Ther 2024, S1360859224005849](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1360859224005849)
- A 2026 meta-analysis explored distance, load and surface as moderators of resisted sled training. Title only; results not read. — [Sports 14(9):395, doi:10.3390/sports14090395](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports14090395)
- Haugen 2019 on sled loads and assisted sprints:
  - Load bands by velocity decrement: light <10%, moderate 10-15%, heavy 15-30%, very heavy >30%. Cross et al. suggest about a 50% velocity reduction maximizes power output.
  - UK Athletics advocates only light loads. Resisted sprinting is common in preparation and deprioritized in competition season.
  - Assisted sprints: practitioners are reluctant because of injury risk; tailwind sprints are the preferred natural alternative.
  — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)
- Rest:
  - Haugen's best-practice rests: acceleration 2-7 min; max velocity 4-15 min; sprint-specific endurance 8-30 min (1-2 min per second sprinted). Short rests (2-4 min) appear only in the 90-95% speed-endurance category.
  - Bangsbo-framework speed endurance maintenance (work:rest <1:4) is by definition lower intensity than production (rest >6× work).
  — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/); [GSSI SSE #126](https://www.gssiweb.org/en/sports-science-exchange/article/sse-126-physiological-basis-of-fatigue-resistance-training-in-competitive-football)
- Intensive sprint sessions need at least 48 h of recovery, and consecutive-day sprinting is rarely used. — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)

### Inferences
- **Rest default:** prescribe rest by distance. Minimum about 1 min per 10 m for accelerations, so 2 min for 20 m and 3 min for 30 m. Use 4-6 min for flying reps. Use 1-2 min per second sprinted for 80-150 m reps, so about 8-12 min for a 12-15 s rep. The rest timer should default to these and allow "ready when ready, but not sooner than X". Grade: moderate/consensus.
- **Weekly volume default (novice to intermediate):** 2 sessions per week. About 100-200 m of max-effort work per session, building toward Alcaraz's ≥160 m-per-session range. Run blocks of at least 6 weeks before judging progress. Advanced users: up to about 300 m acceleration, or a sprint-endurance session up to about 600 m.
- **Sleds:** optional, offered only if the user has a sled. Default to light-to-moderate loads (about 10-20% body mass, or roughly a 10-15% speed drop) over 10-30 m, as one acceleration-session variant during preparation blocks. Never make a sled required. Unresisted sprints get most of the benefit. Do not program assisted/towing sprints by default (weak evidence, injury concern).
- **Ask:** "Do you have access to a sled/harness?"; "What surface do you sprint on?" (track/turf is better than grass for sleds per Alcaraz; grass may be kinder for novices' hamstrings and shins, but that is a preference/weak point).
- **Folklore flags:**
  - "Short rest makes you faster." Not supported for speed. Short rest lowers rep quality and turns the session into speed-endurance or conditioning work. That is a valid goal for 200-400 m or sport fitness, but it is not max-speed training.
  - "Heavier sleds are better." Alcaraz found load did not determine improvement. Heavy loads are an option, not a requirement.

### Gaps
- Petrakos et al. 2016 (Sports Med, systematic review of resisted sled training) was found only as a citation; its findings could not be verified this session.
- No direct RCT comparing rest-interval lengths for chronic sprint-speed gains in non-elite adults was found. The rest defaults rest on best practice plus acute-fatigue reasoning.
- The 2026 umbrella review's conclusions and the Cross et al. comment's content are unknown.

## 3. Resistance training and plyometrics for sprint performance

### Takeaway
Getting stronger in the lower body transfers to sprint speed. Across studies in the Seitz 2014 meta-analysis, squat strength gains and sprint gains correlate at r = -0.77. Plyometrics give small-to-moderate sprint improvements (SMD about 0.24-0.61 depending on population). Strength and plyometrics complement sprinting; they do not replace it. Rumpf 2016 found sprint-specific training produced the largest effects. Grades: strength transfers to sprint **moderate-strong**. Plyometrics improve sprint **moderate** (small-moderate). Combined is better than sprint alone **weak-moderate**. "Heavy lifting makes sprinters slow" is **not supported**.

### Cited Findings
- Seitz et al. 2014, Sports Med (doi:10.1007/s40279-014-0227-1), meta-analysis of 15 studies, 510 subjects, 85 effect sizes: a very large correlation between squat-strength ES and sprint ES (r = -0.77, p = 0.0001). Authors' conclusion: increases in lower-body strength transfer positively to sprint performance. (Abstract level. A secondary summary says 2 days/week gave the best results; not verified in the primary paper.) — [Seitz et al. 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-014-0227-1); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/264246303_Increases_in_Lower-Body_Strength_Transfer_Positively_to_Sprint_Performance_A_Systematic_Review_with_Meta-Analysis)
- Rumpf 2016: specific sprint training ES -1.00 (moderate) vs combined methods averaging ES -0.51 (small). A secondary blog reports a small strength-training ES of about 0.31 that shrinks with distance; not verified against the paper. — [Rumpf et al. 2016](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2016/06000/effect_of_different_sprint_training_methods_on.35.aspx)
- de Villarreal et al. 2012 (JSCR) plyometric meta-analysis (snippet):
  - Modest effect on 20 m sprint (ES = 0.24).
  - Sprint gains were maximized with programs under 10 weeks, at least 15 sessions, high-intensity work, and more than 80 jumps per session.
  — [de Villarreal et al. 2012, JSCR](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2012/02000/the_effects_of_plyometric_training_on_sprint.35.aspx)
- Adult female athletes, 2025 meta-analysis: plyometrics improved sprint, SMD = -0.61 (-0.83 to -0.39), medium across 10, 20 and 30 m; I² = 37.5%. — [PMC12477254](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12477254/)
- Athletically trained adults, mixed sex (snippet): sprint SMD 0.46 (0.01-0.81), dependent on complexity, volume and rest. — [ResearchGate 396034613](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/396034613_Effects_of_plyometrics_training_on_lower_limb_strength_power_agility_and_body_composition_in_athletically_trained_adults_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis)
- Plyometric umbrella review (Sports Med Open 2022): small sprint effect in individual-sport meta-analyses, moderate in general athlete samples (snippet). — [Ramirez-Campillo et al. umbrella review, doi:10.1186/s40798-022-00550-8](https://sportsmedicine-open.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40798-022-00550-8)
- Haugen 2019 on strength work:
  - Strength training 2-3×/week in preparation, in 4-6 week blocks: hypertrophy → max strength → explosive/power/plyometrics.
  - Ballistic exercises at up to about 60% 1RM are potent for power; heavier loads serve the force component.
  - Heavy strength training may have short-term negative effects on sprinting, and vertical heavy lifting does not automatically improve horizontal force. Combining strength with sprint training makes positive transfer more likely.
  - Plyometrics: bilateral and unilateral bounds, hops and jumps, medicine-ball throws; most volume in preparation. Very short-contact plyometrics may raise Achilles risk.
  — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)
- Li et al. 2025 recommend pairing resisted sprinting with vertically oriented strength or plyometric training, because resisted sprinting alone did not improve vertical jump more than unresisted. — [Li et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12689304/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default:** 2 lower-body strength sessions per week.
  - Main lifts: squat or trap-bar deadlift, a hip hinge (RDL), and a unilateral lift (split squat or step-up), at roughly 3-5 sets of 3-6 reps at moderate-heavy loads. For novices, start at the app's existing strength progression.
  - Each strength session opens with a small plyometric block: 40-80 ground contacts, using pogos, broad jumps, bounds, hurdle hops, and a med-ball throw.
  - Reuse the app's existing lifting engine and progression for the strength part, and keep the sprint-specific additions minimal (SSOT: one strength progression model).
- **Plyometric progression:** novices start with low-intensity contacts (pogos, skips, box jumps) at about 40 per session and build toward 80+ over 6-10 weeks before adding bounding or depth jumps. de Villarreal's moderators point to ≥15 sessions and >80 jumps per session as the effective range.
- **Taper the lifting before a test/race:** maintain intensity, cut volume (Haugen: 40-60% volume reduction over 2-3 weeks for strength/power sports).
- **Ask:** lifting experience and current squat/deadlift if known; equipment; whether the user also has a strength goal (if so, this becomes a concurrent program; see Q5).
- **Folklore flag:** "Sprinters shouldn't lift heavy / lifting makes you slow and bulky." Not supported. The Seitz meta-analysis shows strength gains track sprint gains. The real caveats are timing (heavy sessions can leave short-term fatigue, so don't lift heavy legs right before a speed session) and transfer (gym strength alone is not enough; keep sprinting).

### Gaps
- The Seitz 2014 frequency and intensity moderator details and any per-distance breakdown were not verified at full-text level.
- No meta-analysis specific to recreational adult sprinters (rather than team-sport athletes) was found for strength or plyometric transfer.
- The optimal load (heavy vs ballistic) for non-elite sprinters is unresolved in the sources read.

## 4. Hamstring injury risk and prevention for sprinters

### Takeaway
Hamstring strain is the signature sprint injury. The Nordic hamstring exercise (NHE) is the best-studied preventive measure. Van Dyk 2019 pooled 8,459 athletes and found programs including the NHE halved injury rate (RR 0.49). A 2021 reappraisal limited to true RCTs found the effect inconclusive (RR 0.59, 95% CI 0.27-1.29). Low-volume NHE (as few as 48 reps over 6 weeks) still builds eccentric strength. Regular, progressive exposure to near-max sprinting appears protective, and sudden spikes appear harmful (observational). Grades: NHE reduces injury **moderate, contested**. Low-volume NHE builds eccentric strength **moderate**. Regular max-speed exposure is protective and spikes are risky **weak-moderate** (observational, team sports).

### Cited Findings
- van Dyk, Behan & Whiteley 2019, BJSM 53(21):1362-1370 (doi:10.1136/bjsports-2018-100045), meta-analysis of 8,459 athletes: programs including the NHE had injury RR 0.49 (95% CI 0.32-0.74, p = 0.0008), "reduce hamstring injuries by up to 51%". A secondary source reports the effect held irrespective of sport, sex, age and level. — [BJSM article](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/early/2019/02/26/bjsports-2018-100045.long); [QxMD record](https://read.qxmd.com/read/30808663/including-the-nordic-hamstring-exercise-in-injury-prevention-programmes-halves-the-rate-of-hamstring-injuries-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-8459-athletes)
- Impellizzeri et al. 2021, J Clin Epidemiol 140:111-124, "Why methods matter in a meta-analysis: a reappraisal showed inconclusive injury preventive effect of Nordic hamstring exercise" (abstract/snippet):
  - Only 5 of the 15 studies in van Dyk randomized to the NHE.
  - Those 5 RCTs gave RR 0.56 (0.20-1.52). Updated with one more RCT: RR 0.59 (0.27-1.29); 95% prediction interval 0.10-3.29.
  - Their conclusion: protective evidence is inconclusive and mostly at high risk of bias.
  — [UTS OPUS record](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/151354); [Physio Network summary](https://www.physio-network.com/research-reviews/thigh/why-methods-matter-in-a-meta-analysis-a-reappraisal-showed-inconclusive-injury-preventive-effect-of-nordic-hamstring-exercise/)
- Physio Network's reading of the reappraisal: a key flaw in the 2019 analysis was including studies where the NHE was bundled with other exercises. — [Physio Network](https://www.physio-network.com/research-reviews/thigh/why-methods-matter-in-a-meta-analysis-a-reappraisal-showed-inconclusive-injury-preventive-effect-of-nordic-hamstring-exercise/)
- Cuthbert et al. 2020, Sports Med, meta-analysis of 13 studies (snippet): after ≥6-week interventions, eccentric strength effects were very large for both high-volume (g = 2.12) and low-volume (g = 2.28) NHE. The motivation was poor NHE adoption, possibly due to high prescribed volume. — [Salford repository](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/1366029/the-effect-of-nordic-hamstring-exercise-intervention-volume-on-eccentric-strength-and-muscle-architecture-adaptations-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analyses)
- Behan et al. 2022 dose-response RCT (snippet):
  - As few as 48 NHE reps over 6 weeks increased strength, but low volumes did not increase biceps femoris fascicle length.
  - Fascicle-length gains needed either high initial volume tapered to lower volume, or progressively increasing volume.
  — [ACU research bank](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8yq99/the-dose-response-of-the-nordic-hamstring-exercise-on-biceps-femoris-architecture-and-eccentric-knee-flexor-strength-a-randomized-interventional-trial)
- Cholp & Zemková 2025 meta-analysis (snippet): high-volume NHE improved eccentric strength (g = 0.77, 0.49-1.06) and fascicle length (g = 0.43, 0.20-0.65). Low-vs-high comparison not visible. — [Front Physiol 2025, doi:10.3389/fphys.2025.1631205 (PDF)](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1631205/pdf)
- Malone et al. 2017 (Gaelic football), as cited by a secondary review: high chronic loads and exposure to maximal-velocity running were associated with lower injury risk, while large week-to-week swings in high-speed exposure were associated with higher risk. Primary paper not read. — [SimpliFaster review](https://simplifaster.com/articles/hamstring-injury-prevention-holistic/)
- Edouard et al. 2019, "Sprinting: a potential vaccine for hamstring injury?", is a perspective piece, not a trial. — [Sport Perf Sci Reports (PDF)](https://arodes.hes-so.ch/record/4408/files/published%20version.pdf?version=1)
- Edouard et al. 2024, professional football: hamstring injury risk was associated with sprint mechanical output. A multifactorial program including high-speed sprinting was evaluated against a control season (non-randomized). — [Edouard et al. (PDF)](https://arodes.hes-so.ch/record/8458/files/published%20version.pdf)
- Haugen 2019: two-thirds of hamstring injuries in competitive sprinters occurred in the transition from specific preparation to competition season, a period of large volume cuts and rising intensity. Risk appears higher when training speed is far below competition speed. — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default:** include NHE (or a regression: band-assisted Nordic, or eccentric slider leg curl) in every sprint-goal program, 1-2×/week. Two schemes:
  - **Low-volume:** 2-3 sets × 4-6 reps (about 10-20 reps/week). Builds eccentric strength and is easy to stick with.
  - **Fascicle-length emphasis:** start at about 2 × 5 and progress toward 3 × 8-10 over 6-10 weeks, then maintain at low volume.
  Add a hip-dominant hamstring lift (RDL) in the strength sessions. Present the NHE as "likely helpful, cheap, low-risk", not as proven to halve injuries.
- **Exposure management (the bigger lever for an app):**
  - Never let weekly max-speed volume jump sharply. A conservative cap is about +10-20% per week.
  - Insert a deload every 3-4 weeks.
  - Keep some near-max running every week, even when deloading, so there is no long gap followed by sudden max sprints.
  - On return from a break of 2+ weeks, reset to an introductory sprint volume.
  The ~10-20% cap is a heuristic, not an evidence-derived number.
- **Warm-up:** a mandatory progressive warm-up before every sprint session (drills, then build-ups at about 60/75/90% before the first max rep). Best-practice consensus; not directly graded here.
- **Ask:** "Have you pulled a hamstring in the last 12 months?" If yes, start with a longer introductory phase, stress the NHE progression, and advise clearance from a clinician if symptoms are present. Also ask "How recently have you sprinted all-out?", which sets the introductory phase length.
- **Folklore flag:** "Static stretching prevents hamstring pulls." Not evaluated in the sources read; flagged as a gap rather than refuted.

### Gaps
- No RCT of NHE specifically in recreational track sprinters was found. Evidence comes from soccer, rugby and other team sports.
- The Malone 2017 primary paper and its exact thresholds were not read. The "U-shaped" description comes from a secondary source only.
- No trial evidence was found on a safe week-to-week progression rate for sprint volume in non-elite adults.

## 5. Combining sprint training with lifting in one week

### Takeaway
Sprinting and lifting coexist well. Elite best practice runs 2 sprint days and 2 strength days. Most coaches put strength the day after sprint work, so legs are fresh for sprints. A meta-analysis found adding sprint-interval work to resistance training did not blunt strength, jump or sprint gains. The rules that matter: sprint fresh, keep at least 48 h between hard sprint sessions, and don't schedule heavy leg work right before a speed day. Grades: low interference between sprint work and lifting **moderate**. Specific day order **weak** (best practice). Within-session order "speed before lifting" **moderate** (logic plus concurrent-training sequencing data, not sprint-specific).

### Cited Findings
- Haugen 2019 sample weeks:
  - Preparation: sprint Mon/Thu, tempo Wed/Sat, strength Tue/Fri, Sun off.
  - Mid-season: acceleration/max-velocity Mon, sprint-specific endurance Thu.
  - Most coaches schedule strength the day after sprint-specific training to avoid sore muscles when sprinting.
  - Intensive sprint sessions need ≥48 h before the next one.
  — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)
- Meta-analysis of RCTs (snippet): adding sprint interval training to resistance training did not significantly change lower-body strength (SMD = 0.01, p = 0.94), upper-body strength, jump or sprint performance vs resistance training alone, and it improved VO2max. A team-sport meta-analysis found similarly no sprint difference (SMD -0.01, p = 0.95). — [Sponet record: "Does sprint interval training cause interference in concurrent training?"](https://www.sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097987?lng=en)
- Murlasits et al. 2017 meta-analysis on concurrent training order: strength before endurance appears beneficial for lower-body strength, and aerobic capacity is unaffected by order (not sprint-specific). — [PubMed 28783467](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28783467/)
- Haugen 2019: heavy strength training may have negative short-term effects on sprint performance. — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default (4 days/week):** Day 1 sprint (acceleration) + plyometrics; Day 2 lower-body strength; Day 3 rest or upper/tempo; Day 4 sprint (max velocity or speed endurance); Day 5 lower-body strength + NHE. This mirrors Haugen's preparation week.
- **3 days/week:** combine. Sprint first, then lift in the same session, on 2 days at least 48 h apart, plus 1 strength-only or tempo day.
- **Same-session order:** warm-up → sprints → plyometrics → heavy lifts → NHE/accessories. Speed and power work always comes before fatiguing work.
- **Do not schedule:** heavy squats or deadlifts or NHE the day before a max-velocity session; two hard sprint sessions on consecutive days.
- **If the user's primary goal is strength and sprinting is secondary,** keep 1 short sprint session per week (about 6-8 reps) placed after a rest day. Evidence suggests this won't harm strength gains.
- **Ask:** days per week available; which goal is primary (speed, or strength with speed); whether they play a sport (sport practice counts as sprint exposure and should reduce app-prescribed volume).
- **Folklore flag:** "You can't build strength and speed at the same time." Not supported for sprint-type work. The classic interference effect is mainly about high-volume endurance training.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly tests day order (strength the day before vs after sprint sessions) for sprint outcomes in non-elite adults. The guidance is best practice.
- How sport-practice running should offset app-prescribed sprint volume has no evidence base in the sources read.

## 6. Measuring progress without timing gates

### Takeaway
Hand timing is consistently about 0.2-0.25 s faster than electronic timing over a 40 yd dash. Most of the bias comes from the timer's reaction at the start. It is fairly reliable within the same person and setup, so it can track change, but it can't be compared with electronic or official times. Smartphone video apps (MySprint, SpeedClock) come much closer: bias around 0.13 s or less in validation work. A consistent protocol matters more than the tool. Grades: hand-timing bias **moderate**. App validity **moderate** (several validations, small samples). "Time with the same method every time" **strong** (measurement logic).

### Cited Findings
- 40 yd dash, college football players: hand timing averaged about 0.25 s faster than electronic timing, with high correlation (r about 0.98). Timer experience did not significantly affect accuracy. (Snippet.) — [Mayhew et al., "Comparison Between Hand and Electronic Timing of 40-yd Dash Performance in College Football Players"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/41013336_Comparison_Between_Hand_and_Electronic_Timing_of_40-yd_Dash_Performance_in_College_Football_Players); [Validity and Reliability of Hand- and Electronic-Timing for 40-Yard Sprint](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273784006_Validity_and_Reliability_of_Hand-_and_Electronic-_Timing_for_40-Yard_Sprint_in_College_Football_Players)
- Experienced and novice stopwatch timers recorded times faster than electronic timing by -0.22 ± 0.07 s and -0.26 ± 0.08 s respectively. Reaction-time latency at the start is the likely mechanism. (Snippet.) — [Hetzler et al., "Reliability and Accuracy of Handheld Stopwatches Compared With Electronic Timing in Measuring Sprint Performance"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/23446051_Reliability_and_Accuracy_of_Handheld_Stopwatches_Compared_With_Electronic_Timing_in_Measuring_Sprint_Performance)
- Some studies report much smaller hand-timing errors (0.04-0.05 s, ICC 0.99), which differences in distance and protocol explain. Start procedure strongly affects recorded sprint times. (Snippet.) — [Haugen et al., "The Difference Is in the Start: Impact of Timing and Start Procedure on Sprint Running Performance"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221737191_The_Difference_Is_in_the_Start_Impact_of_Timing_and_Start_Procedure_on_Sprint_Running_Performance); [Concurrent Validity and Inter-Rater Reliability of Hand-Held Measurements of Maximal Sprint Speed](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/365847690_Concurrent_Validity_and_Inter-Rater_Reliability_of_Hand-Held_Measurements_of_Maximal_Sprint_Speed)
- Smartphone app (SpeedClock-type video timing) vs timing lights: small systematic bias, mean difference 0.13 s. — [Stanton et al. 2016, "Validity of a Smartphone-Based Application for Determining Sprinting Performance," PMC4972912](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4972912/)
- MySprint has been concurrently validated against radar for force-velocity profiling. A 2025 elite-football study compared MySprint v2.0.1, GPS and radar and notes the newer version needed its own validation. — [PMC12136470](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12136470/)
- Timing gates are more accurate than manual systems but have their own errors, so the start and gate setup must be standardized. — [Haddad et al., "Advances in Electronic Timing Systems"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221767940_Advances_in_Electronic_Timing_Systems_Considerations_for_Selecting_an_Appropriate_Timing_System)
- Context for interpreting change: wind and other external factors affect sprint times, and an elite athlete's year-to-year change is small relative to that noise. — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default:** schedule a time trial every 4-6 weeks, at the end of a deload week, using the user's target distance (or 30 m / 40 yd for sport speed; 150 m or 300 m as a proxy for 400 m).
  - Store the timing method with every result: hand / phone-video app / gates / race-official.
  - Compare only like with like, and never present a hand time as equivalent to an electronic or official time.
  - If displaying an "electronic-equivalent", add about 0.24 s to hand times over 40 yd and label it as an estimate. That figure comes from American football conventions and these studies; it is not a universal constant.
- **Protocol fields to standardize:** start type (standing/3-point/blocks, or "first movement" vs "on the gun"), surface, shoes, wind/indoor, warm-up done, and best of 2-3 attempts with full rest. Record "best of N" rather than a single rep.
- **Change threshold:** with hand timing, treat changes under about 0.1 s over 40 yd as noise unless repeated across tests. This threshold is inferred from the reported SDs (about 0.07-0.08 s), not stated by a source.
- **In-session monitoring without gates:** use rep-time drop-off for the "stop the session" rule (Haugen's "stop when performance drops off"). A cut-off of about 3-5% slower than the session's best rep is a coaching heuristic, not evidence-derived. Many recreational users won't time every rep, so let RPE or "felt slow / form broke" also end the session.
- **Ask:** "How will you time yourself?" (nobody / a friend with a stopwatch / phone video app / gates / races only). This sets which tests the app offers and how results are labeled.
- **Folklore flag:** "My hand-timed 4.4 is the same as a 4.4 at the Combine." Not supported; hand times run about 0.2-0.25 s fast.

### Gaps
- Romero-Franco et al. 2017 (MySprint validation, the usual citation) was not retrieved, so its specific agreement statistics are not reported here.
- No validation was found for consumer GPS watches measuring short-sprint time or top speed in non-elite runners. Prior knowledge suggests GPS is poor for 40 m-scale efforts, but no source was checked this session.
- No source gave a smallest worthwhile change for recreational sprinters' 40 yd or 100 m times.
