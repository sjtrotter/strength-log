# Ask few questions, then let the logs steer

> **Status: research input for the wizard redesign, not a spec.** `STRENGTH_TRACKER_SPEC.md` and `docs/PLAN.md` stay the source of truth until a design doc amends PLAN.md. Several recommendations here (seeding loads, increments, miss and restart rules, the required questions) differ from current behavior that the pinned §11 numbers depend on; treat them as proposals for the design to adopt or reject, and re-verify the §11 numbers deliberately in any change that touches seeding or progression.

The literature supports a short setup wizard and a generator that adapts from what the user logs. About six answers change the plan: the goal, how many days a week the user can realistically train, minutes per session, equipment and its smallest load steps, training experience, and exclusions (exercises to avoid, pain, recent injury, bone or joint conditions, age band). Event and sprint goals add a few more, chiefly the event, its date, a recent result, current volume and the longest recent run. The defaults with the firmest support are floors. Every plan should include **at least two strength days a week covering all major muscle groups at high effort** (strong; WHO 2020 and the 2026 ACSM position stand). Each muscle should be trained about twice a week. Sets should stop **1 to 3 reps short of failure**, because training to failure is unnecessary (strong). Strength goals need heavier loads (**about 80% of 1RM or more**, strong). Size goals need enough sets (**10 or more per muscle per week**, with returns flattening near 18 to 20). Cardio should be **mostly easy**, and no single run should exceed **110% of the longest run in the past 30 days** (moderate). Loads should come from a rep target plus a reps-in-reserve (RIR) target updated from the log, never from a 1RM test. Schedules should be a rotation with forgiving gaps. Many tempting specifics (deload frequency, reset percentages, restart percentages, long-run fractions, sprint session layouts) rest on coaching convention, so they belong in the app as editable starting points. Adherence is what most of these choices protect: in a 389,481-user app cohort, only **about 10% of beginners were still training weekly at 12 months**, and consistency in the first 28 days was the strongest predictor. Several gym rules should never be encoded, including the 10% rule as injury prevention, mandatory failure, a deload every fourth week, "cardio kills gains," fixed weekdays, the "fat-burning zone," and 220 minus age as a true maximum heart rate.

## Grades and verification marks used throughout

Each recommendation carries one of four grades. **Strong** means consistent meta-analyses, an umbrella review, or a high-certainty guideline. **Moderate** means one or two good meta-analyses, several consistent trials, or a very large cohort, with some heterogeneity. **Weak-contested** means small trials, exploratory meta-regressions, observational data, syntheses that conflict, or coaching consensus that has seen little controlled testing; where a number is the researchers' own synthesis or a coaching convention, the text calls it practice. **Preference** means no outcome difference is expected, so the user's taste or logistics should decide. A hyphenated pair such as moderate-strong marks a range between two grades, and **folklore** means the evidence contradicts the claim. The marker **[verify]** flags a figure that the research notes say was recalled from memory or taken from a secondary source, a search-result summary, or an abstract snippet rather than the primary paper. Each needs checking against the primary source before it appears in app copy, and the section near the end lists them all.

The resistance-training recommendations lean on the **2026 ACSM position stand** (Currier, Schoenfeld, Phillips and colleagues), an umbrella review of **137 systematic reviews and more than 30,000 participants**, searched to October 2024, that replaced the 2009 "Progression models" stand. Most of the evidence it synthesizes comes from inexperienced trainees, and it explicitly ranks individualization and adherence above hitting particular prescription numbers ([Currier et al. 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)).

## Six required answers shape the plan, and the first month decides retention

The case for a short wizard comes from dropout data. Conti and colleagues followed **389,481 Fitbod users, 100,709 of them beginners**. Their 52-week adherence (at least one workout a week, with up to six missed weeks tolerated) was **10.1% for beginners, 18.3% for intermediates and 25.8% for advanced users**, and the median beginner dropped out at 19 weeks. Active days in the first 28 days were the strongest predictor (hazard ratio 0.73 per standard deviation at week 11, fading to 0.91 by week 40) ([Conti et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2026.1855668/full)). The study is observational, and early consistency may mark motivation rather than cause it. The paper's own Discussion describes the intermediate and advanced figures as six-month rates, which conflicts with its Results, and the preprint gave a 14-week median **[verify]**. Age and sex effects on adherence were modest, so asking about them to predict adherence adds friction for little value. No study of onboarding length was found, but every question delays the first logged session, and the risk sits in the first month (moderate for the stakes; the friction argument is an inference).

The second finding is about who decides what. Letting people pick their own exercise, intensity or modality reliably makes sessions feel better ([Oliveira et al. 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4523714/)), and in a 12-week trial in older adults, self-selected loads gave **strength gains similar to imposed loads** with more positive affect and lower exertion ([PMC11653500](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11653500/)). Evidence that choice improves long-term adherence is thin: one six-week trial in 30 older adults reported adherence of 0.935 with choice versus 0.791 without ([JEMS](https://scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/915)) **[verify]**, which came from a search summary with an unconfirmed study mapping. Load is the exception to letting users choose, because women selecting their own resistance-training loads chose weights typically below strength-building thresholds ([Ratamess et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5555217_Self-Selected_Resistance_Training_Intensity_in_Healthy_Women_The_Influence_of_a_Personal_Trainer)), a title-and-abstract-level finding. The design that follows is that **the user chooses what they do, and the generator owns how much and how fast**, with an override and RIR feedback as the escape valve (moderate for affect, weak-contested for adherence).

Goal setting has an inconsistent independent effect in app meta-analyses; one meta-regression found it near zero (beta 0.01) ([PMC9261070](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9261070)). Self-monitoring combined with feedback is the most consistent ingredient ([Sports Medicine Open 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-018-0157-9)). The wizard should therefore ask the goal type, which changes program structure, but should not ask users to author numeric outcome targets. The app's core loop of logging, seeing progress against a target, and getting the next target automatically is the evidence-backed part (moderate). Affect during moderate exercise prospectively predicts future activity, while affect after exercise does not ([Rhodes & Kates 2015](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Can-the-Affective-Response-to-Exercise-Predict-and-Rhodes-Kates/7a57d236a8c5f1e516f4051f2ad53f5e60eab378)), so early sessions should feel achievable, and a one-tap post-session rating gives the generator a cheap signal to lighten or swap (moderate for the predictor, practice for the mechanism).

Goal-specific questions belong behind the goal choice. Trained lifters can give a recent weight and reps for each main lift (10 reps or fewer) to seed an estimated 1RM, and can say whether they are comfortable judging reps in reserve. Running goals need the event, race or test date, goal time or "just finish," a recent race or time trial with its date, runs per week, weekly distance, longest run in the past 30 days, running injury in the past year, and whether a heart-rate monitor or a bike or rower with a watts display is available. Ultra goals add road or trail, elevation gain, cutoff, marathon best, and availability for long weekend sessions. Sprint goals add the event, best time if known, sprint background, surface, sled access, how recently the user sprinted all-out, any hamstring strain in the past 12 months, how they will time themselves, and whether sport practice already provides sprinting. Hybrid goals need to know which goal is primary and whether cardio and lifting happen in the same visit, the same day, or on separate days. Users 65 and older should be asked about falls or balance problems in the past year. Exercise swaps, favorites, cardio modality, reminder time, a "lighter today" swap, planned versus reactive deloads, and guideline-minute tracking can all wait until the user meets them in the app.

## Resistance dose comes down to effort, enough sets, and each muscle twice a week

**Volume has a positive dose-response with diminishing returns, and strength flattens much sooner than size** (strong for the shape, moderate to weak on where it flattens). ACSM 2026 says plainly that one set beats zero and two beat one. It reads the meta-regression evidence as plateauing beyond **about 2 to 3 sets per exercise for strength and about 18 to 20 weekly sets for hypertrophy**, and names **10 or more sets per muscle per week** as the level that enhances hypertrophy ([Currier et al. 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)). The plateau figures are ACSM's reading of a cited meta-regression, most likely Pelland and colleagues, whose full text the researchers did not read **[verify the plateau points against Pelland]**. Pelland's 67-study, 2,058-participant meta-regressions found a positive slope for both outcomes with returns diminishing "considerably more" for strength, and found that counting indirect sets as half a set fit best ([Pelland et al. 2026 preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460)). That gives the app one rule for set counting: a compound lift credits its secondary muscles 0.5 sets. Secondary summaries describe Pelland's curves as a square-root shape for hypertrophy and a reciprocal shape for strength ([train.fitness](https://train.fitness/personal-trainer-blogs/not-all-sets-are-created-equal)) **[verify]**, and the often-quoted gain of about 0.37% muscle growth per added weekly set from Schoenfeld 2017 comes from a secondary summary ([Nutrient Metrics](https://www.nutrientmetrics.com/en/evidence/schoenfeld-2017-volume-dose-response)) **[verify]**; neither belongs in copy unchecked. The evidence for trained lifters above 20 sets a week is thin.

**Frequency barely matters for hypertrophy once weekly volume is equal, and helps strength a little** (moderate). ACSM 2026 found hypertrophy unaffected by frequency from one day a week to more than five when volume was equated, and found strength enhanced at two or more days a week with no clear dose-response ([Currier et al. 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)). Pelland found frequency effects "consistently identifiable" only for strength ([Pelland preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460)). A 2026 preprint pooling 16 volume-equated trials found g = -0.06 with I² of 0% and could not distinguish one, two or three sessions a week ([Boutagy & McKean 2026](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.07.26365015v3.full)). The practical default is each muscle twice a week and each main barbell lift twice a week for strength goals (three times for trained users who have the days). Split choice is a preference that should follow from the days available, never from the goal. One day a week is acceptable when that is all the user has; its real cost is that a whole week's volume lands in one long session.

**Load matters for maximal strength and hardly at all for size, provided sets end near failure** (strong). ACSM 2026 reports that strength improves with loads of 80% of 1RM or more, while hypertrophy did not differ between 30% and 100% of 1RM ([Currier et al. 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)). The Lopez network meta-analysis found strength effects of SMD 0.60 for heavy loads (8RM or less) and 0.34 for moderate loads (9 to 15RM) relative to light loads, with no clear hypertrophy differences ([Lopez et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8126497/)). For healthy older adults, Borde's meta-analysis found the largest strength effects at **70 to 79% of 1RM** ([Borde et al. 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-015-0385-9)), so "light weights only" is not required for seniors. The same paper's often-cited best combination of 2 to 3 sets, 7 to 9 reps, two sessions a week and about 4 to 6 seconds between reps was recalled from memory **[verify]**.

**Training to failure is unnecessary for strength, size or power** (strong). ACSM 2026 says completing sets to momentary failure "does not enhance gains" and that 2 to 3 reps in reserve gives sufficient effort ([Currier et al. 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)). Grgic's meta-analysis found no significant difference for strength or hypertrophy, and when volume was not equated non-failure favored strength (ES -0.32) ([Grgic et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2095254621000077)). Exploratory meta-regressions suggest hypertrophy improves as sets end closer to failure while strength stays flat across a wide RIR range ([Robinson et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/)) (moderate to weak, because RIR was estimated). That supports a split default of about 2 RIR on compound strength lifts and 0 to 2 RIR on isolation and size work, with failure optional on the last set of safe machine or isolation exercises.

**Rest beyond about 90 seconds is a preference** (moderate to weak). A Bayesian meta-analysis of nine studies found a small hypertrophy benefit for rest longer than 60 seconds and no appreciable difference beyond 90 seconds, mostly in untrained people ([Singer et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/full)). ACSM 2026 found strength unaffected by rest shorter or longer than one minute. An older review suggesting more than two minutes for trained lifters' maximal strength was not re-fetched ([Grgic et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0788-x)) **[verify]**. The rest timer should default by exercise type and let the user extend freely; supersets of non-competing muscles are a valid time saver when sessions are short.

**Periodization model is a preference** (moderate for a small strength edge in trained lifters, strong for no hypertrophy difference). ACSM 2026 concluded that with progressive overload, periodization "is not significantly superior to nonperiodized programs" ([Currier et al. 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)). Among volume-equated studies, undulating beat linear periodization for 1RM only in trained participants (ES 0.61, 95% CI 0.00 to 1.22) and not in untrained ones (ES 0.06) ([Moesgaard et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/)). No resistance-training meta-analysis of block periodization was found. Novices should get one rep target per lift with simple progression, and intermediate or advanced users can opt into a heavy day and a moderate day per lift, which is also a good way to satisfy a preference for variety.

| Default | Maximal strength | Hypertrophy | General fitness | Healthy older adult |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sessions per week | 2 to 3 for beginners, 3 to 5 for trained (strong for at least 2) | Same; split is preference | 2 to 3 full-body (strong for at least 2) | 2 to 3 full-body, plus balance work on 3 or more days (strong) |
| Exposures per muscle | 2 per week; main lifts 2 to 3 (moderate) | 2 per week (moderate; 1 acceptable) | 2 per week (moderate) | 2 per week (moderate) |

| Main-lift reps and load | 5 to 8 reps for the first weeks, then 3 to 6 at 80% of 1RM or more (strong) | 6 to 15; 15 to 30 allowed on isolation or band work (strong) | 8 to 15 at moderate load (strong) | 6 to 10 at 70 to 80% for strength (moderate) |
| Effort | About 2 RIR, range 1 to 3 (moderate) | 0 to 2 RIR isolation, 1 to 2 compounds (moderate) | 2 to 3 RIR (moderate) | 2 to 4 RIR, no failure (weak, ACSM caution) |
| Rest | 2 to 3 min, top sets 3 to 5 (moderate) | About 2 min compounds, 60 to 90 s isolation (moderate) | 60 to 120 s (moderate) | 1.5 to 2 min (weak) |
| Extras | Lift the target exercise early in the session (ACSM, strong) | Eccentric overload helps (ACSM) | None | Fast-concentric power variants once technique is stable (moderate) |

Beginners should not see a near-failure set in their first weeks. Starting at 2 to 4 RIR and tightening to 1 to 3 after a few weeks of practice serves both technique and the affect finding above (practice). ACSM 2026 cautions that lifting to failure "may also be inadvisable" for older people, a plausibility argument rather than a demonstrated harm.

## The first working weight comes from a warm-up ramp, never a 1RM test

**No validated method predicts a novice's first load from body weight, sex or age** (no evidence found; the flow below is practice). The defensible approach starts a new lifter with the empty bar, a light dumbbell or the lowest machine plates for one or two technique sets, then adds weight in small steps across warm-up sets until a set at the target reps feels like **3 to 4 reps in reserve**. That weight becomes the first working weight, and the user logs reps and RIR.

From the first session or two, the app can treat reps plus reported RIR as reps-to-failure (8 reps at 2 RIR is roughly a 10RM) and compute an estimated 1RM with Epley or Brzycki, **only when reps-to-failure is 10 or fewer** (moderate). Equation accuracy in sedentary older adults improved when reps to failure were 10 or fewer ([Accuracy of seven equations](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/243666838_Accuracy_of_Seven_Equations_for_Predicting_1RM_Performance_of_Apparently_Healthy_Sedentary_Older_Adults)). The formulas themselves and the claim that individual estimates can be 10% or more off come from a secondary source ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-repetition_maximum)) **[verify]**. Nuzzo's analysis of 952 reps-to-failure tests across 7,289 people found that sex, age and training status hardly change the reps-to-%1RM relationship but the exercise does (the leg press allows more reps than the bench press at the same percentage), so any %1RM display needs per-exercise tables ([Nuzzo et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10933212/)). The researchers did not obtain the table values, so none should be quoted from these notes. Trained users who give a recent weight and reps can have their estimate seeded directly.

**1RM testing is reliable and unnecessary** (moderate). A pooled analysis of 32 studies found good to excellent test-retest reliability regardless of experience ([Grgic et al. 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7367986/)), but the safety claims in its introduction cite other studies and amount to second-hand evidence **[verify]**. ACSM 2026 found no increase in serious adverse events from resistance training across more than 38,000 participants, including more than 11,000 older adults ([Currier et al. 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)). A solo app user has no spotter, and older adults with osteoporosis or spinal conditions may be highly susceptible to injury under maximal loads ([PMC11806937](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11806937)). The app should offer an optional 3 to 5RM test to trained users who want precise percentages and never prompt a true 1RM attempt for untrained users, older users, or anyone reporting bone or spine conditions.

**Beginners can use RIR** (moderate). People underpredict reps to failure by **about 0.95 reps** on average, with accuracy better near failure, on lower-rep sets and on later sets, and training status had essentially no influence (beta -0.006) ([Halperin et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/)). Two later trials also found no effect of sex or experience on accuracy ([Remmert et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37036795/); [Refalo et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37967832/)). The generator can treat a reported "2 RIR" as roughly 2 to 3 RIR, should avoid relying on RIR for sets above about 12 reps, and can recalibrate with an occasional as-many-reps-as-possible last set on a safe exercise (practice). Users who decline RIR can tap easy, right, grinder or missed instead.

## Logged reps and RIR drive progression, and progress slows as training age rises

**Autoregulated loading matches or slightly beats fixed percentages** (moderate). A meta-analysis of 15 studies in trained people found a non-significant 1RM difference of 2.07 kg (SMD 0.21) favoring autoregulation ([Hickmott et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35038063/)). In a 12-week squat trial, RIR-based loading produced **back squat gains of 10.8% versus 7.1%**, largely because the fixed group's loads came from a pre-test 1RM that went stale as they got stronger ([Graham & Cleather 2021](https://research.stmarys.ac.uk/3067/3/Graham-Cleather-Autoregulation-Repetitions-in-Reserve.pdf)). Prescriptions should therefore read like "3 x 5 at 2 RIR" with the next load computed from the log. Velocity-based training needs bar-speed hardware that a phone and watch cannot supply reliably, and its strength advantage was not significant in the most recent pooling (SMD 0.21, p = 0.064) ([Wang et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41491263/)), so it is out of scope. A network meta-analysis ranked the APRE protocol first for squat and bench 1RM, but rankings from small networks are fragile ([Huang et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40791980/)) and the APRE adjustment table was not verified against the original **[verify before copying any rep-to-load mapping]**.

**Double progression has little direct evidence and no evidence against it** (weak for rep versus load progression, practice for mechanics). The one trial found adding reps and adding load gave similar results in trained lifters over eight weeks ([Plotkin et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36199287/)). ACSM 2009's consensus rule adds **2 to 10% when the lifter exceeds the target by one or two reps** ([ACSM 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)). The NSCA-style "2-for-2" rule and its suggested increments (2.5 to 5 lb upper body and 5 to 10 lb lower body for less-trained lifters) were found only in exam-prep summaries ([CSCS prep](https://app.achievable.me/study/cscs/learn/program-design-for-resistance-training-training-frequency-exercise-order-and-training-load-and-repetitions)) **[verify]**. The sensible default is a fixed rep target on compound strength lifts, raising load when all sets hit the target at or above target RIR, and an 8 to 12 style range on accessories, raising load once every working set reaches the top of the range at target RIR. Where equipment steps are too coarse (fixed dumbbells, 10 to 15 lb machine stacks, bodyweight), reps progress first, then sets. Equipment increments, more than physiology, decide which method a lift uses.

**Fixed-pound increments over-progress light lifts and under-progress heavy ones** (moderate for the asymmetry, practice for the rule). Competitive powerlifters' maximum yearly gains were **20.2 to 25.4 kg on the squat and 18.1 to 21.1 kg on the deadlift versus 10.5 to 12.8 kg on the bench**, with no sex difference ([Latella et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35019902/)). A percentage rule, about 2.5% of the working load for upper-body and small-muscle lifts and about 5% for lower-body lifts, rounded to the smallest loadable step and bounded by ACSM's 2 to 10%, handles this automatically. It is a synthesis, not a tested rule, and no sex-specific modifier is warranted.

**Expected rates of progress fall with training age** (moderate). In 14,690 adults on a minimal-dose program, strength followed a linear-log curve, with roughly 30 to 50% gains in the first year and a practical plateau by one to two years ([Steele et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35591809/)). Elite football-code athletes gained **0.42% per session** and 7.1% over 12 months ([McMaster et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23529287/)), and the strongest powerlifters gained about half as fast as the weakest ([Latella et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32865942/)). The generator should move a lift's progression cadence from per session to per week to per block when the lift stalls, rather than by the calendar. ACSM 2009's often-quoted average gains by training level (about 40% for untrained down to 2% for elite) were recalled from memory **[verify against the ACSM 2009 full text]**.

**Missed-rep rules are practice, so they should be labeled that way** (practice; moderate that an occasional miss is harmless, since strength is insensitive to proximity to failure). After one session with missed reps (reps below target, or RIR worse than target by two or more), hold the load. After a second consecutive miss at the same load, reduce that lift alone by about 5% or drop 1 to 2 reps for a session. After a third stall, or two resets of the same lift within about six weeks, change the cadence, change the rep target, or trigger a reactive deload. The larger 10% resets common in novice linear programs are convention. A miss the user attributes to poor sleep, illness or a short session should not count toward the stall counter, and the app should never add load after a set the user rated as a grinder or 0 RIR. No controlled trial tests any reset size, so the app should keep its own records of how these rules play out.

## Endurance plans share one easy-heavy backbone and differ by event

**Most cardio time should be easy, and polarized versus pyramidal distributions are close to a wash** (moderate-strong for "mostly easy," weak-contested for polarized specifically). An individual-participant network meta-analysis of 348 endurance athletes found no difference between polarized and pyramidal training for VO2max or time trials, with a subgroup hint that recreational athletes do slightly better on pyramidal and competitive ones on polarized ([Rosenblat et al. 2025](https://www.stir.ac.uk/research/hub/publication/2095779)). Polarized training's small VO2peak edge (SMD 0.24) appeared only in interventions shorter than 12 weeks and in highly trained athletes, with no time-trial difference ([Oliveira et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11329428/)). Programs with large low-intensity volume beat threshold-heavy ones in middle- and long-distance runners ([Campos et al. 2022](https://www.pubmed.gov/34749417/)). The default is **about 75 to 80% of weekly cardio time easy**, expressed for recreational users as a pyramidal week: three sessions become two easy and one quality, and four or five sessions become three or four easy and one quality, with a second quality session only for experienced users. True beginners can keep essentially everything easy for the first 8 to 12 weeks. Users who say they race regularly can choose a polarized option.

**Intervals and steady cardio both work, and intervals add a little** (strong that both work, moderate for the edge). Against no exercise, endurance training raised VO2max by about 4.9 mL/kg/min and interval training by about 5.5, an edge of about 1.2 mL/kg/min ([Milanović et al. 2015](https://research.tees.ac.uk/en/publications/effectiveness-of-high-intensity-interval-training-hit-and-continu-3/)); secondary sources report minor inconsistencies in that paper's moderator values **[verify]**. Only long-interval (2 minutes or more), higher-volume (15 or more minutes of work), 4-to-12-week protocols beat continuous training ([Wen et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30733142/)). The default interval session, weekly for intermediate users and optional for beginners after 4 to 8 consistent weeks, is **4 to 6 x 3 to 4 minutes hard** with equal or slightly shorter easy recovery. Short sprint intervals are a valid time saver, presented as an alternative to steady cardio rather than an upgrade. A 1 mL/kg/min edge never justifies trading consistency for intensity.

**Effort should be anchored to the talk test and RPE, with heart rate and pace as secondary layers** (moderate for talk test and RPE; strong that age-predicted maximum heart rate is imprecise). The talk test tracks the first ventilatory threshold, although most validation is in cardiac patients ([talk test review 2022](https://www.imrpress.com/journal/RCM/23/7/10.31083/j.rcm2307225/htm)). Three effort bands map onto the three-zone model in the training-distribution literature: easy (full sentences, RPE 3 to 4 out of 10), steady or tempo (short phrases, RPE 5 to 6) and hard (a few words, RPE 7 to 9). If the user has a heart-rate sensor, the app should estimate maximum heart rate with Tanaka's 208 - 0.7 x age ([Tanaka et al. 2001](https://webs.um.es/jlyuste/documts/tanaka.pdf)), show zones as ranges labeled as estimates, and let a measured or observed maximum override it. In sedentary adults the standard error was **12.4 bpm for 220 minus age and 11.4 bpm for Tanaka** ([HERITAGE](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/measured-maximal-heart-rates-compared-to-commonly-used-age-based-/)). For pace goals, Riegel's formula (T2 = T1 x (D2/D1)^1.06) is well calibrated up to the half marathon but too fast for the marathon, where at least half of runners finished 10 or more minutes slower than predicted; adding weekly mileage cut the error substantially ([Vickers & Vertosick 2016](https://synapse.mskcc.org/synapse/works/101585)). Retesting pace every 6 to 8 weeks, or inferring it from logged hard efforts, is a preference.

**The best-supported running guardrail caps single runs relative to recent history** (moderate for the single-run cap, which comes from one large exploratory cohort; folklore for the 10% rule; weak-contested for ACWR). In a 5,200-runner device cohort, overuse injury rose when one run exceeded **the longest run of the previous 30 days by more than 10%**, with large spikes over 100% carrying a hazard ratio of 2.28, while week-to-week ratios showed no relationship and the acute-to-chronic workload ratio showed an inverse one ([Frandsen et al. 2025, BJSM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/?p=11751)). The figures come from the BJSM blog and research-portal summaries, and the DOI and confidence intervals were not captured **[verify]**. In the only randomized test of the 10% rule, 532 novice runners had injury rates of **20.8% on a graded 10%-rule program and 20.3% on a standard one** ([Buist et al. 2008](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/no-effect-of-a-graded-training-program-on-the-number-of-running-r/)). Novices who increased distance by more than 30% over two weeks appeared more vulnerable than those under 10% ([Nielsen et al. 2014](https://vbn.aau.dk/da/publications/excessive-progression-in-weekly-running-distance-and-risk-of-runn)). ACWR is methodologically disputed ([Impellizzeri et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938)). The generator should cap any run at 110% of the 30-day longest, which the app can compute from its own logs and which tightens automatically after a layoff, keep novice two-week distance growth under about 30%, and may keep 10% a week as a conservative display default without claiming it prevents injury.

**Beginners get hurt and quit at high rates, so beginner plans should repeat weeks without penalty** (moderate). Novices had **17.8 injuries per 1,000 hours of running versus 7.7 for recreational runners** ([Videbæk et al. 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4473093/)). In a modified nine-week couch-to-5K cohort, only **27.3% completed**, 19% reported an injury, and a previous injury raised the odds of a new one (OR 7.56) ([Relph et al. 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10487403/)). The midpoint and endpoint quit rates of 48.2% and 64.5% circulate from a blog summary ([Kintec](https://www.kintec.net/blog/why-most-couch-to-5k-programs-fail/)) **[verify]**. Run/walk in non-elite marathoners gave similar finish times with less muscle pain and fatigue, but injury was not measured ([Hottenrott et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25467199/)). The first-5K default is three sessions a week of easy run/walk (for example about 1 minute running and 1.5 to 2 minutes walking for 20 to 30 minutes) over roughly 8 to 12 weeks, advancing only when the current step felt manageable with no pain the next day. A user with a past running injury starts one level easier. Run/walk is a fatigue tool and an on-ramp, and copy should not present it as injury prevention.

**Cycling, rowing and the elliptical can stand in for running for general fitness, and less fully for running goals** (moderate for general fitness, weak-contested for performance transfer). Treadmill and elliptical tests gave similar VO2max in one randomized-order study ([JSSM](https://jssm.org/jssm-05-662.xml-Fulltext)), though another found the treadmill higher and the attribution of those figures was not fully verified **[verify]**. A 2026 meta-analysis reportedly found no difference between run-only and cycle-only training for running performance when 20 to 50% of running was replaced ([Marathon Handbook summary of Menges et al.](https://marathonhandbook.com/cycling-for-runners/)) **[verify; original not read]**. For general fitness, all modalities count equally toward weekly minutes. For running goals, most aerobic work stays running, with other modes covering some easy volume or injured weeks. No modality-specific progression trials exist, so bike, rower and elliptical sessions progress by duration first, then add an interval session, then raise watts or resistance at constant RPE (preference). Elliptical console calorie and VO2 readouts should not drive zones.

**Tapers cut volume and keep intensity** (moderate-strong). Across 27 studies, the best taper lasted about two weeks with volume reduced **41 to 60%** while intensity and frequency were held ([Bosquet et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17762369/)); the often-quoted "8 to 14 days" and "about 3% improvement" are secondary paraphrases ([Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/tapering/)) **[verify]**. In more than 158,000 recreational marathoners, strict three-week tapers with steadily falling volume were associated with finishing a median **5 min 32 s (2.6%) faster** than minimal tapers ([Smyth & Lawlor 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.735220)), an observational result.

### Event groups change volume, long run, hard-work type and taper length

The backbone above applies to every event from 800 meters to ultra. What changes is shown below; event-specific evidence is mostly descriptive of elite practice or observational, and the grades reflect that.

The middle-distance structure follows a review of world-class 800 and 1,500 m training that puts the aerobic energy share at **about 60 to 75% at 800 m and 75 to 85% at 1,500 m**, with lactate intervals of 200 to 600 m at 800 to 1,500 m race pace as regular sessions, and with 800 m runners doing more strength and plyometric work ([Haugen et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8363530)). A 60/40 split of sessions below and above threshold is cited as distinguishing the two events, but which event gets which split could not be confirmed **[verify]**. Beginners chasing a first fast mile mostly need aerobic base. For 5K and 10K, world-class runners keep **at least 80% of volume at low intensity** year-round and begin tapering 7 to 10 days out ([Haugen et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35362850/)), and in a randomized trial of subelite runners the group given more easy training improved a simulated 10.4 km race by 157 s versus 121.5 s ([Esteve-Lanao et al. 2007](https://abacus.universidadeuropea.com/entities/publication/7fbfa682-6302-499f-9a81-4e468521d082)).

The half and marathon thresholds come from 997 Dutch recreational runners: half marathoners running **more than 32 km a week with a longest run over 21 km** finished faster, those under 25 km a week finished slower, and marathoners under 40 km a week or with a longest run under 25 km finished slower while those over 65 km finished faster, with **no association between these training levels and injury** ([Fokkema et al. 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7496388/)). A meta-regression of 137 marathon cohorts linked more weekly distance and more weekly runs to faster times ([Doherty et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31704026/)). These are observational, so the app should present them as what faster finishers did. Building long runs under the 110% single-run cap naturally sets a 14-to-20-week plan length for most novices. The marathon goal-time check should add a margin to a Riegel prediction or use weekly mileage, and warn when goal pace is faster than predicted.

Ultra evidence is the weakest. In 100 km finishers, weekly volume mattered more than training pace, the reverse of the marathon ([Tanda & Knechtle 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4425319)), and in a 24-hour race the longest training run and marathon best were the strongest predictors ([LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4023113?lng=en)). The widely quoted finding that **77% of 1,212 ultrarunners** had a run-stopping injury in the prior year comes from a secondary summary of the ULTRA study ([Runners Connect](https://runnersconnect.net/are-ultramarathoners-different/)) **[verify]**. Less training in the 12 weeks before Comrades was associated with more injury ([SAJSM](https://journals.assaf.org.za/index.php/sajsm/article/view/8559)), and abrupt weekly changes in either direction may raise risk ([Two Oceans](https://open.uct.ac.za/items/6c038b37-6df9-451a-84df-55627b3f7ac7)), so ultra plans should rise steadily and avoid both spikes and under-preparation. Back-to-back long runs are coaching opinion.

For fitness tests, the 12-minute run correlates with VO2max at about 0.78, the best of the time-based field tests ([Mayorga-Vega et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4795745)). Interval training improves shuttle-run performance in youth ([PMC6539300](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6539300)). Army training that cut running mileage about 50% and added intervals and agility saw a 1.5 relative risk of overuse injury in the traditional-training control group, with test scores higher or similar ([DTIC ADA523652](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA523652.pdf)); which DTIC report holds which figure was not verified **[verify]**, and the Marine and Navy figures (40% less mileage and 54% fewer stress fractures) come from a newspaper ([Kaiserslautern American](https://www.kaiserslauternamerican.com/constant-running-causes-overuse-injuries/)) **[verify]**. Converting a target score into a required pace is arithmetic, and the app can do it without any evidence claim.

## Sprinting from 60 to 400 meters needs maximal reps, long rest and hamstring care

**Running fast is the best way to get faster, and the work should match the distance** (moderate-strong that sprint training improves sprint times; weak for session layouts, which are coaching consensus). Specific sprint training had a moderate effect across 48 studies (ES -1.00, about -3.2%) ([Rumpf et al. 2016](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2016/06000/effect_of_different_sprint_training_methods_on.35.aspx)), a figure read at abstract and snippet level **[verify]**. Nearly all of this research studies young male team-sport athletes over 0 to 30 meters; trials of recreational adults training for 200 or 400 meters were not found. The session categories come from Haugen's integration of science and best practice ([Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)), read in full: accelerations of 10 to 50 m at more than 98% effort with 2 to 7 minutes rest and 100 to 300 m per session; flying max-velocity reps of 10 to 30 m after a 20 to 60 m build-up with 4 to 15 minutes rest and 50 to 150 m per session; sprint-specific endurance of 80 to 150 m at more than 95% with 8 to 30 minutes rest; speed endurance of 60 to 80 m at 90 to 95%; and tempo of 100 to 300 m at 60 to 70%. Haugen describes best practice as sprinting either at 95% or more or below 70% of maximum velocity, avoiding the band between, ending the session when performance drops or technique breaks, allowing **1 to 2 minutes of rest per second of maximal sprinting** for sprint-endurance reps, and leaving at least 48 hours between intensive sprint sessions.

The 40-yard-to-100-meter default is two sprint sessions a week at least 48 hours apart. One is acceleration (4 to 8 x 10 to 30 m from a two- or three-point start, about 1 minute rest per 10 m, 100 to 200 m total for a novice) and one is max velocity (3 to 5 x 10 to 20 m flying after a 20 to 30 m build-up, 4 to 6 minutes rest), with an optional tempo day. The 200-to-400-meter default keeps one acceleration or max-velocity session, because top speed sets the ceiling, replaces the second with sprint-specific endurance (for example 3 to 4 x 100 to 150 m at more than 95% with 8 to 12 minutes rest) or 60 to 80 m speed-endurance series, and adds a tempo session; the 400 m coaching tradition runs a long-to-short progression. Progression adds reps, then distance within those ranges, while intensity stays maximal and rest stays long. Effective resisted-sprint programs used **about 160 m or more per session, 2 to 3 sessions a week, for at least 6 weeks** ([Alcaraz et al. 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-018-0947-8)) **[verify; abstract and snippet level]**, so blocks should run at least six weeks before progress is judged. Elite sprinters improve only 0.1 to 0.2% a year and national-level ones 1.3 to 1.4% from age 18 ([Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)), so the app should not promise large time drops after the beginner phase. The rule that sessions end at about 3 to 5% slower than the session's best rep is a coaching heuristic, and RPE or "form broke" should also end a session.

**Sleds are optional and light loads suffice** (moderate that resisted and unresisted sprinting give similar overall gains, weak-contested for a resisted edge at 0 to 10 m, weak for assisted sprints). Load did not determine improvement in the Alcaraz meta-analysis. A 2025 meta-analysis found resisted sprinting better over 0 to 10 m (SMD 0.81) but a trim-and-fill estimate that crossed zero and a significant Egger's test ([Li et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12689304/)). Users with a sled can get light-to-moderate loads (roughly a 10 to 15% speed drop) over 10 to 30 m as one acceleration variant in preparation blocks. Assisted or towed sprints should not be programmed by default, since practitioners avoid them over injury risk.

**Lower-body strength and plyometrics transfer to sprinting, and heavy lifting does not make sprinters slow** (moderate-strong for strength transfer, moderate for plyometrics). Squat strength gains and sprint gains correlated at **r = -0.77** across 15 studies ([Seitz et al. 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-014-0227-1)) **[verify; abstract level, and the "2 days a week" detail is secondary]**. Plyometrics improved sprint performance in adult female athletes (SMD -0.61) ([PMC12477254](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12477254/)); the older pooled 20 m effect of 0.24 and the "more than 80 jumps a session, at least 15 sessions" moderators are snippet-level ([de Villarreal et al. 2012](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2012/02000/the_effects_of_plyometric_training_on_sprint.35.aspx)) **[verify]**. The default is two lower-body strength sessions a week (squat or trap-bar deadlift, a hinge, and a single-leg lift at about 3 to 5 sets of 3 to 6 reps), each opening with 40 to 80 plyometric contacts that build from pogos and skips toward bounds over 6 to 10 weeks. The strength part should run through the app's existing lifting engine so there is one progression model.

**The Nordic hamstring exercise is cheap and likely helpful, but its injury benefit is contested** (moderate-contested for injury reduction, moderate for eccentric strength). A meta-analysis of 8,459 athletes reported that programs including the exercise **halved hamstring injury rates (RR 0.49)** ([van Dyk et al. 2019](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/early/2019/02/26/bjsports-2018-100045.long)) **[verify; abstract and snippet level]**, while a reappraisal limited to true randomized trials found RR 0.59 with a confidence interval of 0.27 to 1.29 and called the evidence inconclusive ([Impellizzeri et al. 2021](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/151354)) **[verify; abstract and snippet level]**. Low-volume and high-volume programs both built eccentric strength ([Cuthbert et al. 2020](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/1366029/the-effect-of-nordic-hamstring-exercise-intervention-volume-on-eccentric-strength-and-muscle-architecture-adaptations-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analyses)), and as few as 48 reps over six weeks raised strength ([Behan et al. 2022](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8yq99/the-dose-response-of-the-nordic-hamstring-exercise-on-biceps-femoris-architecture-and-eccentric-knee-flexor-strength-a-randomized-interventional-trial)) **[verify both; snippet level]**. Every sprint program should include the Nordic exercise or a regression 1 to 2 times a week, at about 2 to 3 sets of 4 to 6 reps (practice), described in copy as likely helpful and low risk. Haugen reports that two-thirds of hamstring injuries in competitive sprinters occurred in the transition from preparation to competition season. Exposure management is the bigger lever for an app: weekly max-speed volume should rise gradually (a cap of about 10 to 20% a week is a heuristic), some near-maximal running should stay in every week, deload weeks included, and a break of two weeks or more should reset the user to an introductory sprint volume. A progressive warm-up with build-ups before the first maximal rep should be built into every sprint session (best-practice consensus). A hamstring strain in the past 12 months lengthens the introductory phase.

**Times are only comparable when the timing method matches** (moderate for hand-timing bias, strong for "same method every time" as measurement logic). Stopwatch times ran **about 0.22 to 0.26 s faster** than electronic timing over 40 yards, mostly from the timer's reaction at the start ([Hetzler et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/23446051_Reliability_and_Accuracy_of_Handheld_Stopwatches_Compared_With_Electronic_Timing_in_Measuring_Sprint_Performance); [Mayhew et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/41013336_Comparison_Between_Hand_and_Electronic_Timing_of_40-yd_Dash_Performance_in_College_Football_Players)) **[verify; snippet level]**. A smartphone video app showed a mean bias of 0.13 s against timing lights ([Stanton et al. 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4972912/)). The app should store the timing method with every result, compare only like with like, schedule a time trial every 4 to 6 weeks at the end of a lighter week, record best of two or three attempts with start type, surface and conditions, and treat hand-timed changes under about 0.1 s as noise (an inference from reported standard deviations, not a published threshold).

## Cardio rarely costs strength or size at the doses app users do

**Concurrent training does not measurably reduce hypertrophy or maximal strength, but it trims explosive power** (strong-to-moderate for no cost to size and maximal strength, moderate for the power cost). The most rigorous meta-analysis, 43 studies with strength-only controls, found maximal strength SMD -0.06, hypertrophy SMD -0.01, and **explosive strength SMD -0.28**, with no moderation by cycling versus running, weekly frequency, training status or age. The explosive cost appeared mainly within the same session and disappeared when the modes were **at least 3 hours apart** ([Schumann et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7)). An older meta-analysis reported larger costs, but it mixed fiber-level outcomes and studies without strength-only controls ([Wilson et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22002517/)). The contested signals are a lower-body 1RM penalty in already-trained people (ES -0.35) that appeared only in same-session designs ([Petré et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8053170)) and a lower-body penalty in men but not women ([Huiberts et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933151/)) (weak-contested). No study supports a minutes-per-week threshold beyond which gains stop, so the generator should not encode a cardio cap.

**When both share a session, the primary goal goes first** (moderate). Lifting before endurance work improved lower-body dynamic strength by **6.91%** ([Eddens et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28917030/)) or about 3.96 kg of lower-body 1RM ([Murlasits et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28783467/)), while VO2max and hypertrophy were unaffected by order. Modality is a preference because the signals conflict: running looked worse in Wilson and for type I fiber growth ([Lundberg et al. 2022](https://www.aminer.org/pub/63389ee190e50fcafd8a0b77/the-effects-of-concurrent-aerobic-and-strength-training-on-muscle-fiber-hypertrophy)), Schumann found no modality effect, and Sabag's cycling-HIIT penalty trended larger than running's ([Sabag et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29658408/)) **[verify; subgroup figures differ between versions]**. A strength-focused user who wants more than about five conditioning sessions a week can be offered low-impact modes for the extra volume and runs placed away from leg days (weak).

**Strength training helps runners** (moderate). In middle- and long-distance runners, adding strength training improved running economy by **about 2 to 8%** and tended to improve 1.5 to 10 km time trials, with VO2max and body composition typically unchanged ([Blagrove et al. 2018](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Effects_of_strength_training_on_the_physiological_determinants_of_middle-_and_long-distance_running_performance_a_systematic_review/9619553)). Heavy loads and plyometrics beat light-load and isometric work for economy ([Sports Medicine 2023](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/37272/1/s40279-023-01978-y.pdf)). Trained endurance athletes did not lose VO2max gains when lifting was added, although untrained people did slightly ([Huiberts et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933151/)). Sprint-interval work added to resistance training did not change strength, jump or sprint gains (lower-body strength SMD 0.01) ([Sponet record](https://www.sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097987?lng=en)) **[verify; snippet level]**.

## Two short strength sessions and 150 easy minutes clear the health floor

**The public-health targets are the floor every plan should meet or show** (strong). WHO 2020 recommends **150 to 300 minutes a week of moderate aerobic activity, or 75 to 150 of vigorous, or an equivalent mix**, plus muscle-strengthening of all major muscle groups on **two or more days** (strong recommendations, moderate-certainty evidence). Bouts of any length now count, some activity is better than none, and amounts beyond the range add little for mortality ([WHO 2020, Bull et al.](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955)). The US guidelines match on strength days and add multicomponent balance work for older adults ([Piercy et al. 2018](https://www.qxmd.com/r/30418471)). For adults 65 and older, WHO strongly recommends varied multicomponent activity emphasizing balance and strength on **three or more days a week**, backed by high-certainty evidence that balance and functional exercise reduce falls. The app can satisfy this by putting a short balance block (single-leg stance, step-ups, carries) on lifting days. Every generated plan, whatever the goal, should include at least two all-major-muscle strength days. A weekly aerobic-minutes display can count every logged bout, with vigorous minutes counted double, and can be switched off by users who do not care.

**Weekend-warrior schedules are fine for health** (moderate, observational). In a large US cohort, people active on one or two days a week had all-cause mortality similar to regularly active people at the same total volume (HR 0.92 versus 0.85 against inactive) ([Dos Santos et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35788615/)); the cohort's size of 350,978 and 10.4-year follow-up come from a secondary source ([Healio](https://cm.healio.com/news/primary-care/20220711/physical-activity-confers-similar-benefits-when-spread-out-or-concentrated-in-fewer-days)) **[verify]**. A user who picks one or two days should get a plan without a warning that it is inferior for health, with strength sessions on non-consecutive days where possible. Muscle-strengthening's mortality association is J-shaped, with maximum risk reduction at **about 30 to 60 minutes a week** ([Momma et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9209691)); the pooled RR of 0.85 and the roughly 40% lower mortality for combined strength and aerobic activity come from a press summary ([BMJ press release](https://bmjgroup.com/90-120-weekly-minutes-of-strength-training-may-be-optimal-for-lowering-death-risk/)) **[verify]**. That curve is observational and should never cap a goal-driven user. Any running at all was associated with **27% lower all-cause mortality** across about 232,000 people, with no clear dose-response ([Pedisic et al. 2020](https://vu-9.eprints-hosting.org/40291/1/Pedisic_etal%282020%29-Is_running_associated_with_alower_risk.pdf)); the "once a week or under 50 minutes" minimum-dose detail came partly from press coverage **[verify]**.

**Minimum effective doses are small** (moderate for strength and VO2max, weak for the hypertrophy floor). In resistance-trained men, **one hard set of 6 to 12 reps at about 70 to 85% of 1RM, two to three times a week**, still raised 1RM, though less than higher doses ([Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2020](https://read.qxmd.com/read/31797219/the-minimum-effective-training-dose-required-to-increase-1rm-strength-in-resistance-trained-men-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis)). For powerlifters, about 3 to 6 heavy sets of 1 to 5 reps per lift per week is the suggested minimum (weak, partly expert opinion) ([Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/text)). No validated per-muscle set floor for hypertrophy exists. For aerobic fitness, sedentary men doing **three 10-minute sessions a week containing 3 x 20 s all-out cycle sprints** improved VO2peak about 19%, the same as men doing 50-minute moderate sessions, in a small trial ([Gillen et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4846072)). Strength and size can be maintained for up to 32 weeks with **one session and one set per exercise a week** if relative load stays high, with older adults likely needing two sessions of 2 to 3 sets ([Spiering et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/)). A time-crunched default is therefore two full-body sessions of about 20 to 30 minutes with 1 to 3 hard sets per main lift, a third session when time allows, and either 2 to 3 short interval sessions or steady cardio for the aerobic side. Older and deconditioned users should default to moderate continuous or submaximal intervals, since no source here established the safety of all-out efforts in older adults, and pre-participation screening was not researched.

## A rotation with forgiving gaps beats a calendar, and deloads should be light weeks

**Fixed weekdays have no evidence behind them** (folklore that they are required; moderate that consistency builds habit; weak that flexible periodization works at least as well). In 111 new gym members, habit strength rose with consistency, low complexity, a stable environment and enjoyment ([Kaushal & Rhodes 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25851609/)). Lally's habit study found a median of 66 days to automaticity with wide variation ([Lally et al. 2010](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit)); the 18-to-254-day range and the finding that a single missed day did not derail habit formation come from secondary sources **[verify]**. Beginners who chose each day's training zone by readiness improved leg press more than those on a fixed plan in a 16-person trial ([McNamara & Stearne 2010](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4019534?lng=en)). The default is a rotation: the user sets a weekly frequency target, the app shows the next session whenever it is opened, and calendar days exist only as optional reminders. A missed session is never marked failed and never doubled up; the next session is simply the next one. Kaushal's "four sessions a week for six weeks" should not become a default frequency, because it comes from one small cohort and the dropout data favor an achievable commitment. Exercising at a consistent time of day was associated with more weekly exercise ([Schumacher et al. 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/oby.22535)), which justifies an optional reminder-time question. An in-session "lighter today" swap fits the readiness evidence.

**Deloads are near-universal in practice and nearly untested** (preference for frequency and structure; weak for outcomes). A Delphi panel of strength and physique coaches defined a deload as a period of reduced training stress and agreed it could be planned or taken when an athlete feels fatigued ([Bell et al. 2023](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC10511399)). Surveyed competitive athletes deloaded for **6.4 days every 5.6 weeks** on average, by cutting sets and reps, cutting load and raising RIR while keeping frequency and exercises ([Rogerson et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38499934/)). The only trial in trained lifters, which deloaded by stopping training entirely for a week, found **slightly smaller lower-body strength gains** and no hypertrophy benefit ([Coleman et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/)), while reduced-volume deload weeks did not hinder gains in untrained men ([Pancar et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41730991/)). Novices should get no planned deloads. Intermediate and advanced users should get an optional planned light week every 5 to 8 weeks plus reactive triggers: stalls on two or more lifts, a fatigue or joint-ache flag on two or more sessions, or RIR running consistently worse than target at unchanged loads. The default deload keeps frequency and exercises, cuts sets by 30 to 50%, cuts load about 10% or adds 2 to 3 RIR, and lasts about a week. Running and sprint plans conventionally insert a lighter week every third or fourth week (sprint coaches use 3:1 or 2:1 loading patterns), but no trial tests this for either; it should be an editable preference with the light week still containing some near-maximal running for sprinters.

**Short breaks cost little, and lost strength returns quickly** (moderate-strong for cessation effects and the age moderator, moderate for rapid regain). Trained men showed no leg-press strength loss after two weeks off ([Hwang et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28328712/)), elite football-code athletes maintained strength for up to three weeks ([McMaster et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23529287/)), and losses scaled with layoff length and were larger in people over 65 ([Bosquet et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347054/)). The often-quoted claim that maximal strength is essentially unchanged for up to 28 days, and the age-specific effect sizes, come from a secondary summary of Bosquet ([Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/detraining/)) **[verify]**, as does the heuristic that regaining takes about half the layoff length, which is the author's opinion. Untrained adults who took 10 weeks off regained strength and size rapidly and ended level with continuous trainers ([Halonen et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39364857/)), and three-week breaks every six weeks matched continuous training over 24 weeks ([Ogasawara et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23053130/)). The restart rules below are the researchers' synthesis and no trial tests them, so the app should present them as starting points and let the first session's RIR correct them.

| Days since the lift was last trained | Restart default (practice) |
|---|---|
| 10 or fewer | Resume as planned; hold load for one session if the gap exceeded 7 days |
| 11 to 21 | About 90 to 95% of the last working load, or the same load at 1 to 2 more RIR; return to it within 1 to 2 sessions if RIR comes in on target |
| 3 to 8 weeks | About 80 to 90% of the last load with about two-thirds of the sets; re-ramp using logged RIR |
| More than 8 weeks, or any length for users over 65 or with under 6 months of prior training | Re-seed from a fresh RIR-calibrated set, start around 70 to 80% of the previous load, and progress quickly as a returning novice |

Running needs no separate restart rule, because the 110% cap re-anchors to whatever the user actually ran in the past 30 days. Sprinting resets to introductory volume after two or more weeks off. Gaps under about six weeks should leave the user's program state intact; Conti's six-week window is an operational definition, so the exact threshold is a design choice. A reason-for-gap prompt (illness or injury versus schedule) can make a restart more conservative, though no evidence distinguishes the two. Busy periods can use a maintenance mode of one session a week with 1 to 2 hard sets per lift at normal relative load, or two sessions for older users.

## Conclusion

The evidence makes the wizard smaller than gym culture would suggest. The firmest numbers in this literature are floors: two strength days, 150 moderate minutes, one hard set, a run no longer than 110% of the recent longest. Optimal prescriptions are flat across a wide range, so the wizard's real job is to capture constraints (days, minutes, equipment steps, exclusions, event and date) and leave the tuning to the log. The best-supported adaptive mechanisms (RIR-driven loads, the 30-day single-run cap, stall counters, rotation scheduling, gap-aware restarts) all run on data the app already stores on the device, which suits a local-first app with no network.

The practice-grade defaults, including deload cadence, reset size, restart percentages, long-run fractions and sprint session layouts, are where the app most needs honest labeling and where its own logs are the only feedback available. They should ship as editable starting points behind one short disclosure, and the owner should revisit them with playtest data, since no trial will settle them soon. The verification table matters most for copy: a dozen of the most quotable numbers in this field, from "halves hamstring injuries" to "77% of ultrarunners injured," rest on snippets or secondary summaries and should not appear on screen until someone has read the primary paper.
