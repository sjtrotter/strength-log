# Resistance-training dose for strength vs hypertrophy, and safe starting loads

Scope note: compiled 2026-11-08. Evidence grades used throughout: **strong** (consistent across multiple meta-analyses/umbrella review), **moderate** (one or two good meta-analyses, some heterogeneity), **weak/contested** (exploratory meta-regression, preprint, or conflicting syntheses), **preference** (no meaningful outcome difference; pick for adherence/time). A marker "[not re-fetched]" means the citation and DOI come from the researcher's prior knowledge because PubMed blocked automated access this session; the direction of the finding is well established but the quoted numbers should be checked before they are put in app copy.

The single most important new source is the **2026 ACSM Position Stand** (Currier, ..., Schoenfeld, Phillips; Med Sci Sports Exerc 58(4):851-872; DOI 10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897), an umbrella review of 137 systematic reviews (>30,000 participants) that replaces the 2009 "Progression models" stand. Most of the evidence it synthesizes comes from inexperienced trainees. Its findings are used as the anchor in every section below.

---

## 1. Weekly sets per muscle: dose-response for hypertrophy and strength. Where does the curve flatten?

### Takeaway
More weekly sets produce more growth and more strength, but with diminishing returns. Strength flattens much earlier than hypertrophy. ACSM 2026 reads the meta-regression evidence as plateauing beyond about 2-3 sets per exercise for strength and about 18-20 weekly sets per muscle for hypertrophy, with ≥10 sets/muscle/week as the "enhanced hypertrophy" threshold. One set is far better than zero. Two sets beat one. **Grade: strong** that volume has a positive, diminishing dose-response. **Moderate/weak** on exactly where it flattens.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2026: "Muscle hypertrophy was enhanced by higher volumes (≥10 sets/wk)". Strength was enhanced by "2–3 sets" per exercise. Summary table: Hypertrophy "Volume: ≥10 sets/wk (dose-response)"; Strength "Volume: 2–3 sets/session" — [Currier et al. 2026, ACSM Position Stand, MSSE, DOI 10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf) (author-hosted PDF of the open-access paper; DOI record via [McMaster experts](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/3873753))
- ACSM 2026, discussion: "Clearly, one set is superior to zero sets (CTRL), and two sets are superior to one set ... but the exact number of sets required to optimize adaptations cannot be ascertained." A meta-regression showed "a dose-response that plateaued and showed diminishing returns beyond ~2–3 sets/exercise for strength and ~18–20 weekly sets for hypertrophy"; "healthy adults are advised to complete at least two sets per exercise" — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- Pelland, Remmert, Robinson, Hinson, Zourdos: meta-regressions over 67 studies and 2,058 participants, adjusted for duration and training status. Posterior probability of a positive volume slope was 100% for both hypertrophy and strength. Both show diminishing returns, "considerably more pronounced" for strength. Published as Sports Medicine 2026;56(2):481-505. Preprint DOI 10.51224/SRXIV.460 — [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460); [sponet record of the journal version](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)
- Pelland et al., set counting: sets were split into direct and indirect relative to the measured muscle or lift. Indirect sets counted as 1 ("total"), 0.5 ("fractional") or 0 ("direct"). The **fractional (0.5) method had the strongest relative evidence** and was used for the primary models — [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460)
- Pelland et al., curve shape (secondary summary): best-fit hypertrophy model was a square-root curve (gradual taper). Best-fit strength model was a reciprocal curve (steeper diminishing returns). The abstract gives no sets/week inflection number — [train.fitness summary](https://train.fitness/personal-trainer-blogs/not-all-sets-are-created-equal)
- Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017: a graded dose-response between weekly sets and hypertrophy across 34 treatment groups. A secondary summary gives about **+0.37% muscle growth per added weekly set**. The categorical comparison (<5, 5-9, 10+ sets/week) was only a trend (P = 0.074). J Sports Sci 35(11):1073-1082, DOI 10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197 — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Dose-response-relationship-between-weekly-training-Schoenfeld-Ogborn/0d34206f962394983054451cddd8a3b91818f732); [author PDF](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Dose-response%20relationship%20between%20weekly%20resistance%20training%20volume%20and%20increases%20in%20muscle%20mass%20-%20A%20systematic%20review%20and%20metaanalysis.pdf); per-set figure from [Nutrient Metrics summary](https://www.nutrientmetrics.com/en/evidence/schoenfeld-2017-volume-dose-response) (secondary)
- Ralston et al. 2017, "The Effect of Weekly Set Volume on Strength Gain: A Meta-Analysis" (Sports Med) is the strength-side counterpart cited by ACSM 2026 for volume → strength — [PMC5684266](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5684266/)
- Swinton et al. 2024 (Sports Med), a dose-response meta-analysis across outcome domains: with higher set volumes, the marginal benefit of each additional set is smaller. ACSM 2026 says its volume conclusion "aligns with" Swinton — [Springer, DOI 10.1007/s40279-024-02006-3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02006-3); [Stronger by Science commentary](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/research-spotlight-volume-returns/)
- ACSM 2026 notes that weekly volume load (load × reps × sets × exercises × sessions) "was rarely considered in included reviews" — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Generator default, hypertrophy goal:** about 10-12 fractional sets per muscle per week for untrained users, rising toward about 12-20 for trained users who ask for more. Do not go above about 20 by default: beyond ~18-20 the returns are small and fatigue and time cost rise. Count a compound lift's indirect muscles at 0.5 (Pelland's best-supported method). This also gives the app one SSOT rule for set counting.
- **Generator default, strength goal:** 2-3 working sets per main lift per session, repeated across ≥2 sessions/week (see §2). Extra volume pays off less for strength than for size.
- **Generator default, general fitness / older / time-poor:** 1-2 sets per exercise on all major muscle groups, ≥2 days/week. ACSM 2026 explicitly says that "minimal doses" produce substantial gains and that adherence and individualization matter more than hitting specific prescription numbers.
- **Ask the user:** available sessions per week and minutes per session. The generator should then fit the volume target into that budget, rather than asking for a set count directly. Optionally ask whether size or strength is the priority, which sets volume toward the upper or lower band.
- **Folklore check:** "one set is useless" is **false**. One set beats nothing by a wide margin. It is just less than optimal for hypertrophy.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the full Pelland text, so no per-set marginal gain or exact flattening point from that model is available here. The ~18-20 sets/2-3 sets plateau figures are ACSM 2026's reading of "a meta-regression (ref 187)", most likely Pelland.
- The evidence base for trained lifters above 20 sets/week is thin, and several high-volume studies (e.g., Schoenfeld 2019 5 vs 10 sets/exercise) are single trials.
- Remmert, Pelland et al. have a SportRxiv preprint on per-session volume (a possible per-session ceiling) — [SportRxiv 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537). I did not read the results, so I cannot state a per-session cap.

## 2. Frequency: when weekly volume is equated, does frequency matter?

### Takeaway
For **hypertrophy**, frequency barely matters once weekly volume is equated (1, 2 or 3 sessions/muscle/week give similar growth). **Grade: strong/moderate.** For **strength**, higher frequency has a small, consistently identifiable benefit with diminishing returns, partly from practicing the lift. **Grade: moderate.** Twice a week per muscle is the practical floor in every position stand. It is mainly a way to fit enough volume and good-quality sets, not a magic number.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2026: hypertrophy was "not affected by frequency". RT "did not influence hypertrophy performed with low (1 d/wk) versus high (>5 d/wk) frequency when total volume was equated". Strength "was enhanced by RT performed with higher frequency (≥2 d/wk, though the upper limit cannot ... be undetermined)", but "there was insufficient evidence to conclude a dose-response relationship, and the impact is diminished when volume is equated" — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- ACSM 2026 critique of 2009: the old recommendation of 2-3 full-body sessions/week for novice hypertrophy was graded top-quality evidence but "was informed by only three studies (n = 59 total participants), and two of the studies did not investigate frequency" — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- Pelland et al. 2026: frequency's effect on hypertrophy was "compatible with negligible" (posterior probability of a positive slope <100%). Frequency's effect on strength had a 100% posterior probability of a positive slope, with diminishing returns. "Frequency shows consistently identifiable effects only for strength" — [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460)
- Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2016 (Sports Med 46:1689-1697): training a muscle twice a week gave better hypertrophy than once a week. The between-condition ES difference was about 0.19 (95% CI 0.11-0.28). Most included studies did not equate volume — [Springer, DOI 10.1007/s40279-016-0543-8](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-016-0543-8)
- Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019 (J Sports Sci 37(11):1286-1295, DOI 10.1080/02640414.2018.1555906): the later, stricter analysis. On a volume-equated basis there was no significant frequency effect on hypertrophy — [JSAMS review of available evidence](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1440244018308624); secondary description in [Kettlebell Kings summary](https://www.kettlebellkings.com/blogs/default-blog/optimal-frequency-for-muscle-growth) [journal abstract not re-fetched]
- Boutagy & McKean 2026 (medRxiv preprint, not peer reviewed, not preregistered; 16 volume-equated trials, n = 380): pooled g = -0.06 (95% CI -0.24 to 0.13), I² = 0%. One vs two g = +0.07, one vs three g = -0.23 (ns), two vs three g = -0.01. Trained g = -0.04, untrained g = -0.08, no difference. GRADE certainty low to very low. "The data lack the precision to distinguish one, two or three sessions at a fixed weekly volume" — [medRxiv 10.64898/2026.10.07.26365015v3](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.07.26365015v3.full)
- Grgic et al. 2018, "Effect of resistance training frequency on gains in muscular strength" (Sports Med 48(5):1207-1220, DOI 10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x): higher frequency was associated with greater strength gains, but when volume was equated the frequency effect was not significant [not re-fetched; listed in ACSM 2026 reference list]

### Inferences
- **Default:** each muscle trained **2×/week**. Each main barbell lift is practiced **2×/week** for strength goals, optionally 3× for trained strength-focused users who have the days. Splits (full-body, upper/lower, push/pull/legs) are **preference** for hypertrophy, so the generator should choose a split based on available days, not on goal.
- **1×/week is acceptable** if that is all the user can do. Volume-equated data show little hypertrophy penalty, and ACSM's "any RT helps" framing applies. The real cost of 1×/week is that a whole week's volume goes into one session, which pushes per-session fatigue up.
- **Ask:** "How many days a week can you train?" (and which days). Derive frequency per muscle from the answer. Do not ask about frequency per muscle directly.

### Gaps
- The Grgic 2018 effect sizes could not be re-verified this session (PubMed blocked).
- No good data separate "frequency per lift" from "frequency per muscle" for strength. The skill-practice explanation is plausible but inferred.

## 3. Load and rep ranges: low vs high load for hypertrophy; strength specificity of heavy loads

### Takeaway
When sets are taken close to failure, **hypertrophy is similar across a wide load range (~30% to ~85%+ 1RM; roughly 5-30+ reps)**. **Grade: strong.** **Maximal strength (1RM) gains are larger with heavier loads (≥80% 1RM; ≤8RM)**, a specificity effect. **Grade: strong.** Moderate loads sit in between for strength. The choice of rep range for hypertrophy is mostly **preference** (time, discomfort, joint tolerance, equipment).

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2026: strength "was enhanced by lifting heavier loads (≥80% one-repetition maximum)" with a "dose-response" for load. Hypertrophy was "not affected by ... load", and RT did not differ for hypertrophy with "low (30% 1RM) to high (100% 1RM) loads". "Load ... has repeatedly been shown to impact strength more than other RT adaptations." Power: 30%-70% 1RM, fast concentric, ≤24 reps·sets — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- Lopez et al. 2021, network meta-analysis (MSSE 53(6):1206-1216; sets to failure only; low >15RM, moderate 9-15RM, high ≤8RM): no clear hypertrophy differences between load categories. For strength, high load (SMD 0.60) and moderate load (SMD 0.34) beat low load — [PMC8126497](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8126497/)
- Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn & Krieger 2017, "Strength and hypertrophy adaptations between low- vs. high-load resistance training" (JSCR 31(12):3508-3523, DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200): 1RM gains favored high load. Hypertrophy was similar between low- and high-load conditions when sets went to failure [not re-fetched; this is ref 83/84-cluster in the ACSM 2026 load evidence]
- 2009 ACSM general guidance, restated in 2026 for contrast: 2-3 sessions/week, 8-10 exercises, 1-4 sets, **8-20 reps**, 2-3 min rest, **40%-70% 1RM** for general health. ACSM 2026 notes others have estimated that the old full guidelines could require "20 h or more per week" and "may be less relevant for most adults, particularly older adults" — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- Borde et al. 2015, healthy older adults (Sports Med 45(12):1693-1720): training period (p = 0.04), intensity (p < 0.01) and total time under tension (p < 0.01) significantly moderated strength gains. The largest effects came with the longest programs and intensities of **70-79% 1RM** — [Springer, DOI 10.1007/s40279-015-0385-9](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-015-0385-9). From recall [not re-fetched]: the same paper reported 2-3 sets, 7-9 reps and 2 sessions/week as the most effective combination, and ~4-6 s between reps.
- Currier et al. 2023 (BJSM 57(18):1211), Bayesian network meta-analysis of RT prescription in healthy adults, is the earlier companion to ACSM 2026's load conclusions — [BJSM PDF](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/57/18/1211.full.pdf) [details not re-fetched]

### Inferences
- **Strength goal default:** main lifts at roughly **3-6 reps at ~80-90% 1RM** (or RPE 7-9), 2-3+ working sets. Accessories at moderate loads (6-12 reps).
- **Hypertrophy goal default:** **6-15 reps** as the main band (efficient and well tolerated). Allow 15-30 reps for isolation work, joint-friendly variants, or home/band setups. Above ~30 reps, sessions get long and RIR estimation gets poor (see §6), so keep that as an opt-in.
- **General fitness / older adults:** 8-15 reps at moderate load is a sensible default. ACSM 2026 says heavier loads improve strength, and Borde 2015 found the largest strength effects at 70-79% 1RM in older adults, so "light only" is not required for seniors. Progress load as tolerated.
- **Ask:** goal (strength / size / general) and any joint pain or limiting conditions. Equipment (heavy barbells vs dumbbells/bands) affects how load ranges are expressed.
- **Folklore check:** "3×10 for size, 5×5 for strength only" is **half true**. 5×5-style heavy work is better for 1RM, but size grows with either scheme if effort is high. "High reps don't build muscle" is **false** (strong evidence, sets near failure).

### Gaps
- Most low-vs-high-load hypertrophy trials take sets to or near failure. Whether low loads still match high loads when stopped at 3+ RIR is less clear, and probably they do not (inferred from the RIR literature in §4).
- Exact numbers from Schoenfeld 2017 load meta-analysis not re-verified this session.

## 4. Proximity to failure (RIR/RPE)

### Takeaway
Training to momentary failure is **not necessary** for strength or hypertrophy. **Grade: strong** (ACSM 2026 umbrella plus multiple meta-analyses). Hypertrophy likely improves as sets end **closer** to failure, roughly 0-3 RIR, possibly in a non-linear way. **Grade: moderate/weak**, because exploratory meta-regressions rely on estimated RIR. Strength gains look **flat across a wide RIR range**. **Grade: moderate.** A target of **~1-3 RIR** is the defensible default, with failure reserved for the last set of safe isolation/machine exercises if the user wants it.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2026: "Completing sets to fatigue (momentary muscular failure) does not enhance gains in strength, hypertrophy, and power, and so is not necessary for benefits to occur. It may also be that lifting to fatigue is inadvisable for certain populations (e.g., older individuals) due to risks to vascular health and an increased risk of injury resulting from poor form." Sufficient effort "can be accomplished by completing sets ... 'near-failure' or a target of 2–3 repetitions in reserve (RIR)". Primary recommendation: RT "with high effort ... at least twice weekly, with all major muscle groups being engaged" — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- Refalo, Helms, Trexler, Hamilton & Fyfe 2023 (Sports Med 53(3):649-665, DOI 10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y; 15 studies): pooled across set-failure definitions there was a trivial advantage for failure vs non-failure for hypertrophy (ES = 0.19, 95% CI 0.00-0.37, p = 0.045). Subgroups showed no advantage for **momentary** failure vs non-failure. The authors suggest "a potential non-linear relationship between proximity-to-failure and muscle hypertrophy". A letter (Fonseca et al. 2024) disputed some sample sizes used — [AUT open repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/78679612-6ff4-4d00-95d1-7920d072a760/full); [bibbase record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/refalo-helms-trexler-hamilton-fyfe-influenceofresistancetrainingproximitytofailureonskeletalmusclehypertrophyasystematicreviewwithmetaanalysis-2023)
- Robinson, Pelland, Remmert, Refalo, Jukic, Steele & Zourdos 2024 (Sports Med 54(9):2209-2231, DOI 10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2), meta-regressions: hypertrophy improves as sets end closer to failure. Strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR. The authors call the analysis exploratory and say "the exact relationship ... remains unclear" given how RIR was estimated — [Abertay repository](https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-the-dose-response-relationship-between-estimated-resist/); [FAU press release](https://www.fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/muscle-growth-strength-study); [News-Medical summary](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20240801/New-study-reveals-sweet-spot-for-muscle-growth.aspx)
- Grgic, Schoenfeld, Orazem & Sabol (J Sport Health Sci 2022;11(2):202-211, DOI 10.1016/j.jshs.2021.01.007), failure vs non-failure: no significant difference for strength (ES = -0.09, 95% CI -0.22 to 0.05) or hypertrophy (ES = 0.22, 95% CI -0.11 to 0.55). When volume was not equated, **non-failure favored strength** (ES = -0.32). In resistance-trained subjects failure showed a small hypertrophy effect (ES = 0.15) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2095254621000077)
- Halperin et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52(2):377-390, DOI 10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x; 12 studies, n = 414): people underpredict reps to failure by ~0.95 reps on average (95% CI 0.17-1.73), with considerable heterogeneity. Accuracy improves closer to failure, with lower-rep sets and in later sets. Training status did not meaningfully change accuracy — [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/67); [x-mol journal record](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1440095273477795840)
- ACSM 2026 calls RIR/perceived-exertion load methods "appealing because they could translate to various RT forms (e.g., elastic band or body weight RT)" — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Default target:** hypertrophy work at **1-3 RIR**. Strength main lifts at **2-3 RIR** (RPE 7-8), since failure adds fatigue without strength benefit and is riskier on barbell compounds. Older/untrained users at **2-4 RIR** at first, tightening toward 1-3 RIR after a few weeks of technique practice and RIR calibration.
- **Calibration:** because people underpredict RIR by ~1 rep, and accuracy improves near failure and with low reps, the app can: (a) treat early-weeks RIR reports as conservative; (b) occasionally prompt an "AMRAP last set" on a safe exercise to recalibrate; (c) avoid relying on RIR for very high-rep sets.
- **Ask:** nothing up front beyond experience level. Teach RIR in one line ("how many more clean reps could you have done?") and collect it per set or per exercise.
- **Folklore check:** "you must train to failure" is **false** (strong). "Never train to failure" is also unsupported. It is optional and mainly costs fatigue.

### Gaps
- No good data on long-term injury rates of failure training. ACSM's caution for older adults is presented as plausible ("may"), not shown.
- The "Without fail" SportRxiv preprint (single-set to failure vs RIR) was found but its results were not read — [SportRxiv 484](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/484).

## 5. Rest intervals between sets

### Takeaway
For **strength**, rest length does not consistently change outcomes in umbrella-level evidence. Longer rests (~2-3+ min) are the usual advice for heavy compounds in trained lifters, so that set quality holds. **Grade: moderate.** For **hypertrophy**, there is a small benefit from resting **more than 60 s**, and no appreciable difference beyond ~90 s. **Grade: moderate/weak** (9 studies, mostly untrained participants). Very short rest (≤60 s) mainly costs volume load. Beyond ~90-120 s, rest is **preference**.

### Cited Findings
- Singer, Wolf, Generoso, ..., Schoenfeld 2024, "Give it a rest" (Front Sports Act Living, DOI 10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789), Bayesian meta-analysis of 19 measurements from 9 studies: "a small hypertrophic benefit is seen with inter-set rests above 60 seconds", possibly from greater volume load. No appreciable difference beyond 90 s. Whole-body estimates leaned slightly toward shorter rest but were imprecise (-0.08, 95% CrI -0.45 to 0.29). Failure vs non-failure did not change the rest interaction. Mostly untrained participants. Substantial heterogeneity — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/full); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/18363f4031e0487793db2084bee23f38); [Biolayne commentary on population](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-28/which-between-set-rest-interval-is-best-for-muscle-growth/)
- ACSM 2026: strength was not affected by "short (<1 min) versus long (>1 min) between-set rest intervals". There were insufficient data to judge inter-set rest's effect on hypertrophy — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- Grgic, Schoenfeld, Skrepnik, Davies & Mikulic 2018, "Effects of rest interval duration in resistance training on measures of muscular strength: a systematic review" (Sports Med 48(1):137-151, DOI 10.1007/s40279-017-0788-x): longer rest (>2 min) tended to favor maximal strength in trained individuals. In untrained individuals, ~60-120 s appeared sufficient [not re-fetched; listed in ACSM 2026 reference list]
- A 2025 medRxiv preprint examines <60 s vs >60 s rest in males with >1 year of training — [medRxiv 2025.09.22.25336351](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.09.22.25336351.full.pdf) (results not read)

### Inferences
- **Defaults:** heavy compound strength work: **2-3 min** (3-5 min allowed for top sets). Hypertrophy compounds: **~2 min**. Isolation work: **60-90 s**. General fitness: **60-120 s**. Supersets of non-competing muscles are a valid time-saver.
- Expose rest as a timer the user can extend freely. "Rest until ready" is reasonable for heavy work. Do not force a strict 60 s rest for a hypertrophy goal.
- **Ask:** session time budget. If time is tight, prefer supersets or slightly shorter rests on isolation work over cutting sets.
- **Folklore check:** "1-minute rest for hypertrophy" is **not supported**. Rest under 60 s is, if anything, slightly worse, and >60-90 s is at least as good.

### Gaps
- No good rest-interval data specific to older adults.
- Trained-lifter hypertrophy data on rest are sparse.

## 6. Starting loads: estimation without testing, e1RM formulas and accuracy, RIR-based load selection, safety of 1RM testing

### Takeaway
A generator does not need a 1RM test. The best-supported approach is: (1) pick a conservative load the user can lift for the target reps with clean form; (2) have the user report reps and RIR; (3) compute an estimated 1RM from a **≤10-rep** set (Epley/Brzycki are adequate, about ±10% individual error); (4) set working loads from e1RM or from RIR. True 1RM testing is **reliable** (good-excellent test-retest) and appears safe in healthy people when supervised, but it adds little for novices, whose strength changes quickly. The evidence on how to estimate a novice's very first load without any lift is **essentially absent** (preference/heuristic).

### Cited Findings
- Grgic et al. 2020, test-retest reliability of 1RM (Sports Med Open 6:31, DOI 10.1186/s40798-020-00260-z; 32 studies, pooled n = 1,595): 1RM has "good to excellent test–retest reliability, regardless of resistance training experience, number of familiarization sessions, exercise selection", though reliability varies across studies. Its introduction states, citing three other studies rather than its own data, that 1RM testing has been shown to be safe across populations including older adults and clinical groups — [PMC7367986](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7367986/)
- Caution: older adults with spinal conditions such as osteoporosis "may be highly susceptible to injury" under maximal loads, which motivates submaximal alternatives — [PMC11806937](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11806937) (as summarized in search result; full text not read)
- ACSM 2026 safety: in >38,000 participants (>6,700 RT, >11,000 older adults), exercise did not increase serious adverse events, and non-serious adverse event rates were similar to aerobic exercise. RT "can be safe and effective for healthy adults of all ages". Many older adults avoid RT "due to misperceptions about safety and injury risks" — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- Nuzzo, Pinto, Nosaka & Steele 2024, "Maximal number of repetitions at percentages of the one repetition maximum" (Sports Med 54:303-321, DOI 10.1007/s40279-023-01937-7; 952 reps-to-failure tests, 7,289 individuals, 269 studies): sex, age and training status had little influence on the reps-%1RM relationship, so the main table can be applied to most people. **Exercise matters**: the leg press allows more reps than the bench press at the same %1RM, so separate tables were built — [PMC10933212](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10933212/)
- ACSM 2026 cites conversion methods between %1RM and RM, "including a recent analysis that considered exercise-specific conversions and between-subject variability" — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- e1RM accuracy: in sedentary older adults, accuracy of seven 1RM equations improved when reps to failure were ≤10 — [ResearchGate: Accuracy of Seven Equations ... Older Adults](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/243666838_Accuracy_of_Seven_Equations_for_Predicting_1RM_Performance_of_Apparently_Healthy_Sedentary_Older_Adults). Back squat: Epley better from 3RM loads, Brzycki better from 5RM loads (SEE 9.94 kg vs 10.29 kg) — [SIU thesis, Validation of the Brzycki and Epley Equations](https://opensiuc.lib.siu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1744&context=gs_rp) (graduate research paper, low weight)
- Epley (1RM = w × (1 + reps/30)) and Brzycki (1RM = w × 36/(37 − reps)) agree exactly at 10 reps. Below 10, Epley gives slightly higher estimates. Above 10, Brzycki rises faster and becomes unreliable. Individual estimates can be ≥10% off — [Wikipedia: One-repetition maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-repetition_maximum) (secondary)
- A 2026 arXiv preprint fit a weight-dependent 1RM equation on 303,494 near-failure sets across 388 exercises. Accuracy degrades above ~10 reps and varies by exercise (lower-body compounds and deadlift show larger errors than bench) — [arXiv 2603.17495](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.17495) (non-peer-reviewed; app-log data)
- RIR self-rating underpredicts reps to failure by ~1 rep on average (see §4) — [Halperin et al. 2022](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/67)

### Inferences
- **Default starting-load flow (no test):**
  1. Novice, first exposure: start with the empty bar, a light dumbbell, or the machine's lowest plates for 1-2 technique sets. Add load in small steps across warm-up sets until a set of the target reps feels like **~3-4 RIR**. That is the first working weight. Log reps and RIR.
  2. After the first session or two, derive e1RM from a working set using reps + RIR as "reps to failure" (e.g., 8 reps @ 2 RIR ≈ 10RM), computed with **Epley or Brzycki only when reps-to-failure ≤10**. Above 10, prefer RIR-based progression over e1RM.
  3. Set loads by RIR feedback: if reported RIR is above target, add load. If below target, hold or reduce. This method needs no test and works for bands and bodyweight (ACSM 2026's point).
- **Grade:** e1RM formulas are moderate (group-average accuracy within a few percent at ≤10 reps, ±10% individual). The RIR-based autoregulated start is a weak/expert-practice grade, with no RCT on "how to choose the first load".
- **1RM testing:** do not require it. Offer an **optional "rep-max test" (3-5RM)** for trained users who want precise percentages. Never prompt true 1RM attempts for untrained or older users, or for anyone reporting osteoporosis or spinal issues. Testing is reliable and appears safe in supervised healthy adults, but a solo app user has no spotter.
- **Ask the user:** training experience (never / some / regular for ≥1 year), any recent known lifts ("a weight you've done for about 5-10 reps"), age bracket, and any joint/bone conditions. For trained users with known lifts, seed e1RM from their stated reps × weight with Epley. Use a per-exercise reps-to-%1RM table (Nuzzo) if the app ever needs %1RM prescriptions, because leg press ≠ bench press.

### Gaps
- **No systematic evidence** found on predicting a novice's first load from body weight, sex or age without any lift. Bodyweight-ratio heuristics exist in coaching culture but I found no validation.
- I did not obtain the Nuzzo 2024 table values (reps at each %1RM). The report writer should not quote specific reps-at-%1RM numbers from these notes.
- I did not find a dedicated systematic review of injury rates during 1RM testing in novices or older adults. The safety claims are second-hand, from Grgic 2020's introduction and ACSM 2026's general adverse-event data.

## 7. Position stands: ACSM 2009 progression models, ACSM 2026, NSCA

### Takeaway
ACSM 2026 (overview of 137 reviews) replaced ACSM 2009. It is **simpler and less prescriptive**: high effort, all major muscle groups, ≥2×/week. Heavier loads (≥80% 1RM), 2-3 sets per exercise, full ROM and doing the target lift early in the session improve strength. ≥10 sets/muscle/week and eccentric overload improve hypertrophy. Failure, periodization, equipment type, time under tension and set structure did not consistently matter. It explicitly ranks **individualization and adherence above conforming to specific numbers**.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2026 abstract: strength "was enhanced by lifting heavier loads (≥80% one-repetition maximum), through a complete range of motion, for 2–3 sets, at the beginning of training sessions, and ≥2 sessions/wk. Muscle hypertrophy was enhanced by higher volumes (≥10 sets/wk) and eccentric overload ... Training to momentary muscle fatigue, equipment type, exercise complexity, set structure, time under tension, blood flow restriction, and periodization did not consistently impact training outcomes." Search current to October 2024. RCTs ≥6 weeks in healthy adults ≥18 y — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- ACSM 2026 evidence grading: quality of each review was scored from design (meta-analysis yes/no) and AMSTAR quality, adapted from GRADE. A summary percentage per variable was computed because "there remains no standardized method to evaluate the certainty of evidence in overviews of reviews" — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- ACSM 2026 on periodization: "periodization is not significantly superior to nonperiodized programs" given progressive overload, and "less important than previously hypothesized". On progression: it is "not necessary to achieve beneficial outcomes" vs no exercise, and is "likely a requirement only for those seeking continued longer term progress" — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- ACSM 2026 on individualization: "individualizing programs to increase RT participation is ... more important than conforming to specific RTx criteria outlined in previous Position Stands" and "'minimal doses' of RT are able to bring about substantial strength, hypertrophy, and physical functional gains" — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- Press coverage: lead author Stuart Phillips stresses training all major muscle groups at least twice a week over chasing a "perfect" program. The stand was released in March 2026 in the April issue — [Newswise / ACSM release](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1119943); [ACSM pronouncement deck](https://acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Pronouncement-ppt-deck_resistance-training-ps.pdf)
- ACSM 2009 "Progression models in resistance training for healthy adults" (MSSE 41(3):687-708, DOI 10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670) was criticized for lacking evidence-based rigor. As summarized by ACSM 2026, its general guidance was 2-3 sessions/week, 8-10 exercises, 1-4 sets, 8-20 reps, 2-3 min rest, 40-70% 1RM — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- NSCA: "Resistance Training for Older Adults: Position Statement From the National Strength and Conditioning Association" (Fragala et al., JSCR 2019;33(8):2019-2052, DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000003230) exists, but its content was not retrieved this session — [Borde 2015 related-listing search](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-015-0385-9) [not re-fetched]

### Inferences
- The 2026 stand supports a **simple generator**: few, evidence-backed knobs (sessions/week, sets per muscle, load band by goal, RIR target) and freedom on everything else (split, equipment, periodization style, tempo). That fits the project's "Simple" principle.
- **Older adults:** ACSM 2026 covers healthy adults of all ages and says its recommendations are evidence-based across ages, but it calls for future subpopulation guidelines. Defaults for older users should therefore follow the same structure: ≥2×/week, all major muscles, progressive. Starting effort is more conservative (2-4 RIR, no failure, no 1RM tests), with emphasis on progression and power/fast-concentric work for physical function (ACSM: "Power RT enhanced physical function").

### Gaps
- NSCA 2019 older-adult position statement details (e.g., recommended %1RM and sets) were not retrieved.
- ACSM 2026 explicitly does not cover clinical populations, frailty or sarcopenia.

## 8. Folklore audit (which common beliefs the evidence does not support)

### Takeaway
Most gym folklore overstates specificity or necessity. The evidence supports "effort, enough sets, ≥2×/week, heavy for 1RM", and little else as a hard rule.

### Cited Findings
- **"3×10 for size, 5×5 for strength only"**: partly true. Heavy loads better build 1RM (Lopez 2021: high load SMD 0.60 vs moderate 0.34 vs low for strength), but hypertrophy is similar across loads taken near failure (ACSM 2026: no hypertrophy difference from 30% to 100% 1RM) — [PMC8126497](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8126497/); [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- **"You must train to failure"**: false. ACSM 2026: failure "is not necessary for benefits to occur". Grgic 2022: no significant difference, and non-failure favored strength when volume was not equated (ES -0.32). Refalo 2023: no momentary-failure advantage — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2095254621000077); [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/78679612-6ff4-4d00-95d1-7920d072a760/full)
- **"One set is useless"**: false. "One set is superior to zero sets", with more sets better at diminishing returns — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- **"High reps don't build muscle"**: false when sets are near failure (ACSM 2026; Lopez 2021) — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf); [PMC8126497](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8126497/)
- **"1-minute rest for hypertrophy"**: not supported. A small benefit for >60 s, no difference beyond ~90 s (Singer 2024) — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/full)
- **"You must hit each muscle 2-3×/week to grow" / "bro splits don't work"**: not supported once volume is equated (ACSM 2026; Pelland 2026; Boutagy 2026 preprint g = -0.06) — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf); [SportRxiv 460](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460); [medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.07.26365015v3.full)
- **"You need periodization to progress"**: not supported for healthy adults; nonperiodized programs with progressive overload work about as well — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf)
- **"Lifting heavy is dangerous for older adults"**: not supported for healthy older adults. No increase in serious adverse events across >11,000 older adults in ACSM's cited analysis. Borde 2015 found the best strength effects at 70-79% 1RM. Caution remains for osteoporosis/spinal conditions and maximal testing — [Currier et al. 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf); [Borde 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-015-0385-9); [PMC11806937](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11806937)

### Inferences
- **Consolidated generator defaults** (grades in brackets):

| Item | Untrained | Trained | Older adult (healthy) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sessions/week | 2-3 full-body [strong for ≥2] | 3-5, split = preference | 2-3 full-body [strong for ≥2] |
| Frequency/muscle | 2× [moderate] | 2× (strength lifts 2-3×) [moderate] | 2× [moderate] |
| Weekly sets/muscle (fractional) | ~8-12 [strong direction, moderate number] | ~10-20 by goal [moderate/weak at top end] | ~6-10, build up [inferred] |
| Strength loads | 5-8 reps first weeks, then 3-6 @ ≥80% [strong] | 1-6 @ 80-90%+ [strong] | 6-10 @ 70-80% [moderate; Borde] |
| Hypertrophy loads | 8-15 reps [strong: any load works near failure] | 6-15, 15-30 on isolation [strong] | 8-15 [strong] |
| RIR target | 2-4, tighten to 1-3 [moderate] | 1-3 hypertrophy, 2-3 strength compounds [moderate] | 2-4, avoid failure [ACSM caution, weak] |
| Rest | 1.5-2 min compounds, 60-90 s isolation [moderate] | 2-3+ min heavy compounds [moderate] | 1.5-2 min [weak] |
| Starting load | Technique ramp to ~3-4 RIR [preference] | Seed e1RM from stated ≤10-rep lift [moderate] | Technique ramp, no 1RM test [weak/ACSM caution] |

- **Minimal onboarding questions:** goal (strength / size / general fitness); experience (none / <1 yr / ≥1 yr consistent); days per week and minutes per session; age bracket and any bone/joint conditions; equipment; optionally, recent lifts "weight × reps" for trained users. Everything else (split, rep scheme within band, rest) can be derived or left as an editable preference.

### Gaps
- Hypertrophy and strength data in older adults are mostly from untrained, healthy cohorts. No well-graded RIR or rest guidance specific to older adults was found.
- No sources examined here address women-specific dosing, but Nuzzo 2024 found sex did little to the reps-%1RM relationship — [PMC10933212](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10933212/).
