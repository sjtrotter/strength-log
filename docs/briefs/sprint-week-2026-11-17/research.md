# Sprinter week: lift frequency, same-day sequencing, 2/3/4-day structures, safety

Date 2026-11-16, revised after the council; precedent wording revised 2026-11-17 (design council round 1, Sol S8). This revision of `fix/research-sprinter-week.md` applies the Opus seat's source check (`research-sprinter-week-verify-opus.md`, 12 changes) and the Sol seat's reasoning check (`research-sprinter-verify-sol.md`, 8 findings). It builds on `docs/briefs/wizard-evidence-2026-11-08/notes/sprint-training.md` and the audit notes `sprint-plyo-nordic.md` and `concurrent-timing-warmup.md` (C03, C04, C07), and does not repeat them.

**Sources.** Abstracts were read through Europe PMC. Full text was read on PMC for Haugen 2019, Schumann 2022, Cuthbert 2021 and Kadlec 2023, and the Qu 2026 subgroup tables. Seitz 2014 is closed access, so only its abstract was read. Johnston 2016, Johnston 2017, Robineau 2016 and Bolger 2015 were added after the council; I re-read their abstracts on Europe PMC for this revision. Every number below is in a source the verify note confirmed or that I re-read.

**Grades, one rubric throughout.**
- **Strong:** position stands, or consistent meta-analyses of RCTs.
- **Moderate:** a meta-analysis with caveats, or several consistent RCTs.
- **Weak:** single small trials, acute or crossover lab studies, indirect populations.
- **Practice:** coaching texts, best-practice reviews, product choices.

An acute crossover study is weak even when it is well run. A rule built from studies at a different dose is practice, even if each study is graded higher.

**Population.** No trial tests sprinting plus lifting in recreational adult sprinters. The evidence comes from team-sport athletes (soccer, rugby; many youth or professional), combat-sport youth, a few elite masters sprinters, and trained young men in lab studies. The closest population evidence is Bolger 2015's systematic review of resistance training in competitive adult sprinters: five studies, three of them positive and one negative. Every transfer to the app's users is an inference.

## Q1. How many lower-body resistance sessions a week, to improve or maintain sprint speed?

### Answer
- **To improve speed: two lower-body sessions a week is a reasonable default. Weak evidence plus practice.**
  - The only direct 1-vs-2 comparison is one youth trial (Otero-Esquina). Its 2/wk group also did twice the weekly work.
  - Elite practice runs 2 to 3 a week (Haugen).
  - Frequency and weekly dose are confounded throughout this literature.
- **One session a week still builds strength in less-trained lifters.** Grgic: ES 0.74 at 1/wk, and with volume equated, frequency has no effect. One session a week is therefore not "maintenance only" for a novice. The honest label is "fewer lifting exposures, slower strength progress than two sessions" (practice).
- **To keep gains already made: one session a week. Weak to moderate:** Rønnestad (n = 7 per group, pros, after a 2/wk build), with Spiering for strength in general populations.
- **One session every other week lost the gains: weak.** One trial, groups of 7.
- **A maintenance result does not set a ceiling.** That one session a week maintained gains in pros does not show that one session cannot improve a novice.
- **WHO 2020 and ACSM call for two or more strength days a week for health (strong).** They set no threshold for sprint performance, so they are a health argument, not a speed one.
- **Strength transfers to sprinting: moderate.** Kadlec, Sainani & Nimphius 2023 re-analyzed Seitz 2014 and found errors: an outlier, effect sizes computed with SE in place of SD, and correlated effect sizes treated as independent. The corrected squat-to-sprint correlation is **r = −0.56 (95% CI −0.75 to −0.26)**, down from Seitz's −0.77.
- **Seitz's frequency moderator should not be used.** It rests on the same flawed effect sizes, and its direction cannot be read from the abstract.

### Findings
- **Seitz et al. 2014**, Sports Med 44(12):1693, doi:10.1007/s40279-014-0227-1, PMID 25059334. 15 studies, 510 subjects, 85 ES. Abstract: squat-sprint r = −0.77, sprint ES −0.87 (3.11%). Frequency (r = 0.50) and rest between sets (r = −0.47) were moderators; load, duration, sets, reps and age were not. The "2 days a week best" wording appears only in secondary blogs. **Superseded for the transfer estimate by Kadlec 2023; frequency moderator: do not use.**
- **Kadlec, Sainani & Nimphius 2023**, Sports Med, doi:10.1007/s40279-022-01766-0, PMC9877053 (full text, via the verify note).
  - Corrected correlation r = −0.56 (−0.75 to −0.26).
  - The SE-for-SD error includes the effect size taken from Rønnestad 2011.
  - The moderators were not re-run.
  - Kadlec also lists Grgic 2018 among 20 meta-analyses with a mixed error score; Grgic's point estimates still stand.
  - Grade **moderate** (transfer).
- **Otero-Esquina et al. 2017**, Eur J Sport Sci, doi:10.1080/17461391.2017.1378372, PMID 28944722. 36 elite U-17 to U-19 soccer players.
  - Design: 1 vs 2 sessions a week, against a control group. The program combined full back squat, YoYo leg curl, plyometrics and sled towing.
  - Both frequencies improved CMJ (ES 0.39-0.81) and change of direction.
  - Only the 2/wk group improved linear sprint (ES 0.43-0.52).
  - 2/wk beat 1/wk on 10 m (0.50) and V-cut (0.52), and beat 1/wk and control on 20 m (0.48-0.64).
  - Analysis by magnitude-based inference. Volume was not equated: the 2/wk group did twice the work. The program was multicomponent, not lifting alone.
  - Grade **weak**.
- **Rønnestad, Nymark & Raastad 2011**, JSCR 25(10):2653, doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e31822dcd96, PMID 21873897. Professional soccer, n = 14 (7 per group).
  - Design: 10 preseason weeks at 2/wk, then 12 in-season weeks at 1/wk vs 1 every 2 weeks.
  - 1/wk maintained strength and 40 m sprint; 1 every 2 weeks lost both.
  - Grade **weak-moderate** (maintenance).
- **Spiering et al. 2021**, JSCR 35(5):1449, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000003964, PMID 33629972. Narrative review.
  - Strength and size held for up to 32 weeks on 1 session a week and 1 set, if relative load is kept.
  - Older adults may need 2 sessions a week and 2-3 sets.
  - "Insufficient data ... for athletes."
  - Grade **moderate** for general populations, **weak** for sprint.
- **Qu et al. 2026**, BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil, doi:10.1186/s13102-025-01403-z, PMC12809985. In-season flywheel training, 7 RCTs, 199 male athletes, sprint SMD −0.38.
  - **Every comparison is flywheel against traditional RT, not RT against none.** Its frequency subgroups (<2/wk SMD −0.31; ≥2/wk −0.46; neither significant) describe flywheel's advantage over traditional lifting.
  - **Not frequency evidence. Removed from this question.**
- **Grgic et al. 2018**, Sports Med 48:1207, doi:10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x, PMID 29470825.
  - Strength ES 0.74 / 0.82 / 0.93 / 1.08 for 1 / 2 / 3 / 4+ sessions a week (p = 0.003).
  - With volume equated, frequency had no effect (p = 0.421); lower body p = 0.070.
  - Grade **moderate** (strength, not sprint).
- **Cuthbert et al. 2021**, Sports Med, doi:10.1007/s40279-021-01460-7, PMC8363540. Well-trained lifters: "no clear differences ... between training frequencies". The pooled lower-body difference is g = 0.061 (p = 0.651). Grade **moderate** (strength).
- **Kneffel et al. 2021**, J Sports Sci, doi:10.1080/02640414.2020.1822595. Adults over 60: +0.14 ES in maximal strength per extra weekly day; more than two sessions a week is unlikely to add benefit. Grade **moderate** (older-adult strength).
- **Fragala et al. 2019**, NSCA position statement on older adults, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000003230, PMID 31343601. The statement exists. Its 2-3 days a week figure is not in the abstract, and neither verifier re-read it. **Unverified; not used for a rule.**
- **Styles, Matthews & Comfort 2016**, JSCR, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000001243. 17 professional soccer players (mean age 18.3), 6 weeks in-season, 2/wk at 85-90% 1RM.
  - Squat 125 → 149 kg (d 0.62).
  - 5 m d 0.55, 10 m d 0.45, 20 m d 0.31.
  - No control group. Grade **weak**.
- **Haugen et al. 2019**, Sports Med Open 5:44, doi:10.1186/s40798-019-0221-0, PMC6872694 (full text).
  - Elite sprinters do strength "typically ... 2-3 times per week during the preparation period".
  - Caveats in the same review: "heavy strength training may induce negative short-term effects on sprint performance", and vertical heavy lifting "does not automatically translate" to sprinting.
  - Grade **practice**.
- **Bolger et al. 2015**, JSCR, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000000720, PMID 25268287. Systematic review of resistance training lasting more than 4 weeks in adult male competitive sprinters, with 10-100 m times as outcomes. Five studies; three improved sprinting and one worsened it. The evidence base is small and short. Grade **weak** (the population fits; the evidence is thin).
- **Cristea et al. 2008**, Acta Physiol, doi:10.1111/j.1748-1716.2008.01843.x, PMID 18284658. Elite masters sprinters aged 52-78, randomized (7 trained, 4 control), 20 weeks of sprint plus heavy and explosive strength training. Strength rose 21-40% and squat jump 10%. Frequency is not in the abstract. Grade **weak**.

### Inference for the app (practice)
- A speed plan defaults to two lower-body exposures a week. Copy must not claim that one session cannot build strength, nor that two is proven to make anyone faster.
- The two sessions need not be identical. Grgic suggests weekly volume matters more than how it is split.

## Q2. Same-day sequencing (sprint then lift) vs lifting on separate days; acute carry-over

### Answer
- **Within one session, sprint first. Weak evidence plus practice.**
  - Leg fatigue immediately before sprinting slows sprints (Roth, acute, weak).
  - One small RCT found sprint-first training gave larger sprint gains and lift-first larger strength gains (Chen, n = 8 per group).
  - A trial of near-identical design found no order effect (Xu & Wang).
  - Sprint-first remains the sensible default because it protects the session's main aim. It is practice-backed, not proven.
- **What the rule should say.** The evidence supports "no fatiguing leg work *immediately* before sprints" (weak). With 2 h or more between the sessions, order has not been shown to matter. In Johnston 2017, weights 2 h before speed did not change 24 h recovery, and 10 m was no slower. The old rule "no heavy lower work before sprints in the same session, ever" overreached and is withdrawn.
- **Sprinting and lifting on the same day, 2 h apart, cost no measured readiness the next day.** Johnston 2016: CMJ, CK and hormones at 24 h matched a speed-only day; only soreness was higher. This is the closest direct test of a high/low day. It shows the day is tolerable, not that it beats separate days (weak).
- **A same-session pairing may blunt maximal strength.** Robineau 2016: strength gains were smaller with aerobic work straight after strength (0 h) than with 6 h or 24 h between, or with strength alone. That was endurance-type work, not alactic sprinting. Chen points the same way (sprint-first gave a little less strength). **When the user can split the day, a gap of about 6 h or more is the preferred form** (practice informed by weak-moderate evidence).
- **Sprint-type conditioning plus RT showed no detected interference.** Ferraro-Farro 2026 is a meta-analysis. "No detected interference" is not equivalence, and the SIT protocols are not sprinter-style max-velocity sessions. The endurance-interference rules apply to tempo and longer intervals. They do not obviously apply to short maximal sprints with full rest.
- **Recovery windows depend on dose, so the 72 h figure is not a hard rule (practice).**
  - Thomas 2018's "about 72 h" came from 10 × 5 squats at 80% (50 heavy reps) and 15 × 30 m sprints (450 m maximal). It measured knee-extensor function, not sprint performance.
  - The app's lift (3 × 5 main plus 2-3 accessory sets) and its max-velocity volume (30-100 m) are a fraction of those doses.
  - Alonso-Fernandez's 72 h advice followed 40 Nordic reps (effects d 0.22) in men new to the exercise, and goes beyond its own 48 h data.
  - Coratella found a repeated bout four weeks later cut eccentric strength on day 1 only.
  - Haugen's elite late-preparation week puts explosive strength on Tuesday, 48 h before Thursday's max velocity.
  - **Working rule (practice):** about 72 h before the next max-velocity session after a new or high-volume heavy session and in a user's first weeks of Nordics; about 48 h once the user is adapted at the app's low volumes.
- **High/low** (Francis: sprints, heavy or explosive lifts and plyometrics on high days; tempo or easy work on low days) is **practice**. Johnston 2016 now gives it acute support. No trial compares it with separate-day lifting for sprint outcomes.
- **Haugen reports the other elite habit:** "the majority schedule strength training the day after sprint-specific training to avoid sore muscles when sprinting", and "sequencing of sessions differs among coaches". Both traditions exist; neither is tested against the other.
- **Reconciling this with the hybrid default (audit C03, decision row 14).** Trained Strength lifters on run hybrids default to separate days because running is endurance work. There, the same-session explosive-strength cost (Schumann, SMD −0.31 at ≤20 min apart) and Petré's trained-lifter 1RM penalty apply. Sprint sessions are short, maximal and fully rested. The SIT+RT evidence detects no interference, and Johnston 2016 shows a same-day 2 h split costs only soreness. Sprint plans therefore keep same-day high/low as the default. Separate sessions (≥ 6 h) stay the preferred variant, and Haugen's next-day lifting stays a valid alternative at four or more days. This is a reasoned choice, not a tested comparison.

### Findings: within-session order and same-day pairing
- **Chen et al. 2026**, J Sports Sci Med, doi:10.52082/jssm.2026.138, PMC12912664. RCT, 24 young male judo athletes (8 per group), 6 weeks at 3/wk, preparatory phase. Short sprint intervals (SSIT) plus power-based RT in either order, against a control group.
  - SSIT-first beat RT-first on 20 m (ES −0.46, p = 0.031), vertical jump (0.62), agility and peak power.
  - RT-first gave more strength (ES 0.14, p = 0.007).
  - Grade **weak**.
- **Xu & Wang 2025**, J Sports Sci Med, doi:10.52082/jssm.2025.205, PMC11877299. RCT, 40 national-level young karate athletes, 8 weeks at 3/wk. sSIT (4 × 5 × 5 s) with RT in either order.
  - No sequencing effect: 20 m ES −1.10 vs −1.16.
  - Within-group effect sizes were larger in the combined groups, but the abstract reports no significant difference from either mode alone.
  - Grade **weak**.
- **Johnston M et al. 2017**, J Sci Med Sport 20(5):502, doi:10.1016/j.jsams.2016.03.007, PMID 27036712. Randomized crossover, 15 academy rugby players: weights then speed vs speed then weights, 2 h apart.
  - CMJ, soreness, CK, testosterone and cortisol did not differ by order over 24 h.
  - 10 m was faster when speed came second (1.76 vs 1.80 s). The abstract calls this significant but prints p > 0.05, so read it as "no slower", not "faster".
  - Grade **weak**.
- **Johnston MJ et al. 2016**, JSCR 30(11):3098, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000001423, PMID 27028155. Randomized crossover, 15 academy rugby players: 6 × 50 m sprints alone vs followed 2 h later by weights (back squat and RDL, 4 × 5 at 85%).
  - At 24 h, no difference in CMJ, CK, testosterone or cortisol; soreness higher (p ≤ 0.05).
  - Grade **weak**.
- **Kotzamanidis et al. 2005**, JSCR 19(2):369, doi:10.1519/R-14944.1, PMID 15903377. 35 soccer players. Heavy RT plus speed training in the same session beat the same RT alone on 30 m, squat jump and CMJ; 1RM rose in both groups.
  - **What it shows:** adding speed work to heavy RT helps.
  - **What it does not test:** same-session against split-day training.
  - The order within the session is not in the abstract and is unchecked. If the full text puts RT first, the trial weighs against a strict sprint-first rule.
  - Grade **weak**.
- **Roth et al. 2021**, JSCR 35(8):2158, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000003112, PMID 30908374. 24 trained young adults (12 women, 12 men), randomized crossover, magnitude-based inference. A 20-min leg-exhaustion routine worsened 20 m sprint, agility, balance and strength (SMD 0.36-0.92).
  - It supports "don't sprint straight after fatiguing leg work".
  - It says nothing about lifting hours earlier.
  - Grade **weak** (acute).
- **Thatcher et al. 2012**, JSCR, doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e318245beea. One set of 5 deadlifts at 85%, 10 min before a 30 s cycle sprint, raised peak power to 109% of control. Low-dose heavy work can potentiate. Grade **weak**.

### Findings: effect of prior sprinting on the lifting that follows
- **Andrade et al. 2023**, Res Q Exerc Sport, doi:10.1080/02701367.2021.1948954. Treadmill HIIT right before squats to failure cut reps, more for half-squat than bench. Grade **weak** (metabolic HIIT, not alactic sprints).
- **Panissa et al. 2016**, Front Physiol, doi:10.3389/fphys.2016.00081. After 5 km of 1:1 HIIT, half-squat performance was −31% at 30 min and −19% at 1 h, with no significant loss from 4 h on. Grade **weak**.
- **Mathieu et al. 2022**, Sports (Basel), doi:10.3390/sports10050075. 25 rugby players: running or cycling SIT in the morning, RT 4 h later. No loss of bar velocity or CMJ; running SIT raised RPE and lowered wellbeing on the following days. Grade **weak** (acute).
- **Crewther et al. 2009**, Eur J Appl Physiol, doi:10.1007/s00421-008-0978-1. 18 semi-elite rugby players (groups of 6-8), 4 weeks. A 40-s cycle sprint before each leg workout did not change gains (box squat +20.5% vs +23.2%). It is not a running max-velocity sprint. Grade **weak**.
- **Johnston et al. 2015**, Eur J Sport Sci, doi:10.1080/17461391.2015.1010107, PMID 25674673. 18 rugby players, 6 × 50 m with 5 min rest.
  - Jumps dropped right after, recovered by 2 h, and dropped again at 24 h.
  - CK and soreness rose through 24 h; testosterone and cortisol fell at 2 h.
  - Grade **weak**. Lifting later the same day may meet better readiness than lifting the next day. That fits high/low, but it is one study.

### Findings: recovery windows
- **Thomas et al. 2018**, Med Sci Sports Exerc 50(12):2526, doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000001733, PMID 30067591. 10 male athletes, crossover: 10 × 5 squats at 80%, 10 × 5 jump squats, 15 × 30 m sprints.
  - Fatigue resolved by 48 h after jumps and by 72 h after squats and sprints.
  - Potentiated twitch was down at 48 h after all three protocols.
  - Voluntary activation was down at 24 h (jumps, sprints) and 48 h (squats).
  - The doses are far above the app's, and it measured knee extensors, not sprint performance. Grade **weak**; the 72 h rule built from it is **practice**.
- **Howatson & Milak 2009**, JSCR, doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181bac52e. 15 × 30 m with hard deceleration and 60 s rest. CK and DOMS were up for 72 h and MVC down for 48 h. A harsher session than sprinter-style work. Grade **weak**.
- **Alonso-Fernandez et al. 2024**, Sports Biomech, doi:10.1080/14763141.2021.1992493, PMID 34748450. 12 vs 12 men, 4 × 10 Nordics: high-speed horizontal force reduced up to 48 h (d 0.22). The authors advise 72 h, beyond their data. Grade **weak**.
- **Coratella et al. 2025**, Int J Sports Med, doi:10.1055/a-2595-3622, PMID 40280182. 24 Nordic reps. The first bout cut eccentric strength through day 3; a repeat bout 4 weeks later cut it on day 1 only. Grade **weak**.
- **Rhodes et al. 2019**, Res Sports Med, doi:10.1080/15438627.2018.1523168. After soccer-specific fatigue, eccentric hamstring strength bottomed at 40-48 h. Measurements stopped at 72 h, so the authors' "not fully recovered until 96 h" is **extrapolated** from a regression. Grade **weak**.
- **Harrison et al. 2019**, Sports Med, doi:10.1007/s40279-019-01136-3. Review: low-volume heavy or ballistic priming can improve performance 1-48 h later. **Tsoukos et al. 2018**, JSCR, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000001812: 5 × 4 jump squats at 40% raised CMJ 5.1% and RSI 10.7% at 24 h in 17 trained athletes. Grade **weak**. The dose decides whether a session costs recovery or primes.

### Findings: does the endurance-strength interference literature apply?
- **Ferraro-Farro et al. 2026**, Int J Sports Med, doi:10.1055/a-2820-4527, PMID 41734815. 9 RCTs, 177 healthy adults, SIT + RT vs RT.
  - Lower-body strength SMD 0.01, upper −0.06, jump 0.11, sprint −0.01 (p = 0.95), VO2max +0.78. Short sprints (≤10 s) improved jump (+0.41, p = 0.025).
  - Grade **moderate** for "no detected interference from SIT conditioning". It does not test a sprinter's track week.
- **Sabag et al. 2018**, J Sports Sci, doi:10.1080/02640414.2018.1464636. HIIT + RT vs RT: lower-body strength ES −0.248 (p = 0.049); cycling −0.377, running −0.176 (ns). Grade **moderate** (mostly longer intervals).
- **Schumann et al. 2022**, Sports Med, doi:10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7, PMC8891239 (full text). 43 studies, most small. Maximal strength and hypertrophy were unaffected by concurrent endurance training. Explosive strength fell within a session (≤20 min apart; SMD −0.31, 95% CI −0.62 to −0.01) and not at ≥3 h. Grade **moderate-strong** for endurance + strength, **weak** as a transfer to sprinting.
- **Robineau et al. 2016**, JSCR 30(3):672, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000000798, PMID 25546450. 58 amateur rugby players, 7 weeks: strength with aerobic work 0, 6 or 24 h later, against strength alone and a control group.
  - Bench and half-squat gains were lower with the 0 h gap than with 6 h, 24 h or strength alone.
  - The authors advise at least 6 h between "contradictory qualities".
  - Grade **weak-moderate**. A caution against one combined session when maximal strength matters.

### Practice sources
- **Francis high/low** (Charlie Francis Training System, 1992, via secondary sources such as https://longtermtraining.substack.com/p/highlow-model). Sprints, heavy or explosive lifts and plyometrics are grouped on high days; tempo or easy work goes on low days. "Sprinting is always #1, weights #2." Grade **practice**.
- **Haugen 2019 Table 3** (full text).
  - Early, mid and late preparation: sprint Mon/Thu, strength Tue/Fri (plyometrics added in late prep), tempo Wed/Sat.
  - Late prep has explosive strength on Tuesday, 48 h before Thursday's max velocity.
  - **Mid-season drops strength work and keeps plyometrics only.**
  - Table 2: 48-72 h after max velocity and speed endurance, 24 h after tempo.
  - These weeks assume about six training days. Grade **practice**.

## Q3. Week structures for 2, 3 and 4 days

**Read these as calendar examples.** The app's rotation entries are not days: a rest entry is a suggestion, and the user can train the next entry at once. A pattern like H/L/H run on consecutive days gives high-day gaps of 48 and 24 h. Every spacing below holds only if the user trains on the days shown. The design note handles what the plan can guarantee.

### Constraints (graded)
1. **Two sprint sessions a week, about 48-72 h apart:** practice (Haugen Table 2; Alcaraz 2-3/wk, audit notes).
2. **Two lower-body exposures a week as the speed-plan default:** weak + practice (Otero-Esquina; Haugen). One a week is the reduced-time option, with slower strength progress (practice).
3. **Heavy or high-volume legs and Nordics away from the day before a max-velocity session, by dose:** about 72 h in the first weeks or after a high-volume session, about 48 h once adapted at low volume. Practice, informed by Thomas, Alonso-Fernandez and Coratella (all weak) and Haugen's 48 h weeks.
4. **Within a day: sprints, then plyometrics, then lifts, then Nordic.**
   - Sprint first: weak + practice.
   - The rest of the order: practice (Haugen, Francis; audit S23).
5. **Plyometrics twice a week:** practice backed by weak-moderate meta-analytic moderators (audit S15: de Villarreal 2012 ≥15 sessions in <10 weeks; Haugen Table 3).

### 3 days
- **(b) "Sprint A + plyos, Lower, rest, Sprint B + Nordic, rest"** (merged after audit row 6).
  - One lower-body exposure and one plyometric exposure a week: slower strength progress than two sessions (practice). It is not "cannot build".
  - Calendar: Mon A, Tue Lower, Thu B gives Lower 48 h before B. That is inside the conservative 72 h tier, though acceptable once the user is adapted.
  - The Nordic on B is a full rotation from the next B.
  - Within the rotation, the wrap B → rest → A is one rest entry. In calendar terms that is 48-96 h, depending on the user.
- **(a) High/low: Sprint + Lower on the same day twice; the third day is upper body and, for 200/400 m, tempo.** *(Design note: tempo at three or four days was not adopted; the design keeps revision 31's tempo policy, because this tempo support is practice only.)*
  - Two lower exposures, two plyometric exposures, two sprint days.
  - Every lift follows that day's sprints. On a Mon/Thu or Mon/Fri calendar, every lift and Nordic is ≥72 h before the next sprint day.
  - Johnston 2016 supports the same-day pairing acutely (weak). Robineau warns that a combined session may blunt maximal strength, so a ≥ 6 h split is better when possible.
  - Costs: longer high days (about 40-60 min at the app's doses), and the lift is done after sprinting.
  - **Recommended; the structure is practice.**
- **Other options.**
  - Three sprint days in three training days leaves one 48 h gap. For 200/400 m only, with the third session tempo (Haugen 24 h).
  - The Haugen-style split (two sprint days, one lift day the day after Sprint A) is option (b) and remains the minimal-lifting choice.

### 2 days
- Both days are high days: sprint (A, then B), plyometrics, a lower lift, Nordic on the day followed by the longer gap. Days about 72 h apart (Mon/Thu, Tue/Fri).
- It is the only arrangement with two sprint and two lift exposures. Practice, with Johnston 2016 as acute support for the pairing.

### 4 days
- **Default high/low:** two high days about 72 h apart, two low days (upper body, tempo; no heavy legs, no Nordic). Practice.
- **Not recommended as a default:** the earlier Saturday speed-endurance variant for 200/400 m. It creates a third high day with 48 h gaps, which contradicts constraint 3's first-weeks tier.
- **Alternative (Haugen split, experienced lifters):** Mon Sprint A + plyos; Tue Lower A, light or ballistic; Thu Sprint B + plyos; Fri Lower B + Nordic. Practice. Lower A sits 48 h before Sprint B, as in Haugen's late-prep week, so it fits the adapted tier only.

## Q4. Safety of max sprinting and heavy lower-body lifting in the same session

### Answer
- **No study compares injury rates for same-session sprint + heavy lift with separate days.** The design cannot claim that either schedule is safer.
- What exists:
  - (i) Fatigue before sprinting alters sprint mechanics and hamstring loading (weak, acute mechanism studies).
  - (ii) Older age and a prior hamstring strain are the strongest hamstring-injury risk factors (Green, strong-moderate, mostly team sports).
  - (iii) Eccentric hamstring strength stays down for 48-72 h or so after fatiguing work, by dose (weak).
  - (iv) Masters sprinters have completed combined sprint and heavy strength training (weak; no injury data).
- **Inference (practice):** sprint while fresh, and keep heavy legs and Nordics off the day before a max-velocity session. These are precautions consistent with (i)-(iii). They are not proven to prevent injury, and risk cannot be attributed to fatigued sprinting alone.

### Findings
- **Green et al. 2020**, BJSM 54:1081, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2019-100983, PMID 32299793. 78 studies, 71,324 athletes, 8,319 hamstring strains.
  - Older age: SMD 1.6.
  - Any previous hamstring strain: RR 2.7. Recent strain: RR 4.8. Prior ACL: RR 1.7. Prior calf strain: RR 1.5.
  - "Older" means older within athlete cohorts. **An age-40 trigger is practice, not evidence.**
  - Grade **strong-moderate**.
- **Small et al. 2009**, Int J Sports Med, doi:10.1055/s-0029-1202822. 9 semi-pro soccer players. A soccer-fatigue simulation slowed sprints and shortened stride. Grade **weak**.
- **Liu et al. 2026**, BMC Musculoskelet Disord, doi:10.1186/s12891-026-09699-9. 19 track athletes. After fatigue, hamstring optimal length fell (d 0.95-1.07) and peak strain rose (d 0.46-0.52). Grade **weak**.
- **Rhodes et al. 2019** (above): eccentric strength lowest at 40-48 h; the 96 h figure is extrapolated. Grade **weak**.
- **Lovell et al. 2018**, Scand J Med Sci Sports, doi:10.1111/sms.12925. Amateur soccer, 12 weeks.
  - Nordics before or after training built strength equally (+11.9% / +11.6%).
  - Fascicle length rose more when done before, thickness more when done after.
  - Grade **weak-moderate**. For sprinters, Nordic after sprints stays the default: the "before" arm preceded soccer practice, not maximal sprinting (practice).
- **Ganse et al. 2014**, PMID 24879018. 3,154 masters athletes at a championship.
  - Injury rate 2.2-2.8%, higher in sprints and jumps.
  - No association with age.
  - Grade **weak** (competition only).
- **Cristea et al. 2008** (above). Grade **weak**.
- **Pickering, Hicks & Kiely 2021**, J Aging Phys Act, doi:10.1123/japa.2020-0302. Masters sprint narrative review. Grade **practice**.
- **Edouard et al. 2018**, Front Physiol, doi:10.3389/fphys.2018.01706. Under sprint fatigue, the glutes compensate for horizontal force. Grade **weak**.
- **Novices:** no study isolates novice adults doing maximal sprints plus heavy lifts. Haugen: "gradual familiarization, both in terms of intensity and duration/repetitions" (practice).

### Inference for older, previously injured and novice users (all practice)
- No evidence supports a particular age cutoff, a 12-month strain window, an effort cap or a particular intro length. Each such choice is a product precaution.
- Sensible precautions, each cheap:
  - The existing intro (three counted sessions per sprint day at range low ends).
  - The 72 h tier between high days for longer.
  - One heavy hinge per rotation, on the max-velocity day so the next max-velocity session is a rotation away. That is "one a week" only when the user runs one rotation a week.
  - Nordics at session end only.
  - Copy that asks for build-up reps short of all-out in the first sessions.
- The app has no intensity cap and no recovery check that sees lifts or Nordics (SprintRules counts only sprint sessions). These precautions are layout and copy, not runtime safeguards.

## Recommended default weeks

**Legend:** [weak] / [moderate] give the evidence grade of the component; [P] is practice or inference. No rule here rests on better than weak evidence for its *schedule*; the strength-transfer component is moderate (Kadlec).

**High-day order:** warm-up and build-up, sprints, plyometrics, lower lift, upper (if any), Nordic last [weak: Roth, Chen; P: Haugen, Francis, S23].

**Three different precedents sit behind "sprint and lift on the same day".** Keep them apart:
- **Francis** puts sprints and weights in the same high-day session (practice).
- **Johnston 2016 and 2017** tested the same day with the weights 2 h after (or before) the sprints (weak). That is the closest tested pairing, and it is not one continuous session.
- **Haugen's example weeks** put strength on separate days, the day after sprinting (practice).

A one-visit high day therefore rests on Francis's practice, with Johnston's 2 h same-day data as its nearest test.
- If lifting must come first, leave ≥ 2 h [weak: Johnston 2017].
- If the user splits the day, sprint first and lift ≥ 6 h later where possible [weak-moderate: Robineau; P].

**Calendar examples (the plan guarantees order only):**
- **2 days (Mon/Thu):**
  - High A: acceleration (or max velocity at 200/400 m), plyos, squat-pattern lift + single-leg.
  - High B: the other sprint session, plyos, hinge lift, Nordic last on the max-velocity day.
  - [P structure; moderate transfer; weak plyometric frequency]
- **3 days (Mon High A / Tue Low / Thu High B):** low day = upper body, plus tempo for 200/400 m [P: Haugen tempo, Francis low day]. *(Tempo here: not adopted in the design; see the note above.)*
- **4 days (Mon H / Tue L / Thu H / Fri or Sat L):** the low days hold upper body and tempo. No heavy legs and no Nordic on a low day [P]. *(Tempo at four days: not adopted in the design.)*

**Rules to encode regardless of day count:**
- **Two lower-body exposures** for a speed goal by default [weak + P]. One a week is the reduced-time option with slower strength progress [P].
- **Spacing before max velocity:**
  - No heavy or high-volume lower lift and no Nordic on the day before a max-velocity session.
  - Aim for about 72 h in the first weeks and after high-volume sessions, about 48 h once adapted at low volume [P; weak sources at higher doses].
- **No fatiguing leg work immediately before sprints** [weak]. With ≥ 2 h between, order is not shown to matter [weak].
- **A low-volume primer** (≤ 4-5 low-rep heavy or ballistic sets) the day before sprinting is permitted [weak]; the app does not schedule one.
- **Endurance-interference spacing rules** apply to tempo and longer intervals, not to short maximal sprints with full rest [moderate for no detected SIT interference; inference for the transfer].

## Gaps
- No RCT compares high/low (same-day) with separate-day lifting for sprint outcomes in any population. Johnston 2016 and 2017 are acute crossovers.
- No study measures recovery from a sprinter-style max-velocity session (≤ 150 m, full rest) plus the app's lift dose.
- No injury data exist on same-session sprint + lift in novices or adults over 40.
- Kotzamanidis 2005's within-session order is unknown (abstract only).
- Fragala 2019's frequency figure is unverified.
- An aggregator lists a 2026 JSCR youth-soccer pilot comparing same-session with next-day power and sprint work. Its primary source was not reached, so it is not cited.
