# Sprint, plyometric and Nordic rules (inventory S01 to S33, including PR 5c-i)

Scope: every row in the inventory's sprint section, including the "Not built" rows that PR 5c-i's lead answers settle (two-session steps, a three-session intro at the low end, a 15% weekly cap on max-speed metres that compounds per held session, a gap reset at "days since the day's last session − 7 ≥ 14", a one-rep time trial every fifth rotation, tempo outside the cap). Abstracts were read in full through Europe PMC unless a line says "snippet". Haugen 2019 and Haugen & Buchheit 2016 were read in full text. No source here studied recreational adults training for 40 yd to 400 m. The evidence comes from youth and team-sport athletes (soccer, Australian and Gaelic football, college football), recreationally active men in Nordic trials, and elite-sprinter coaching practice. Every transfer to the app's users is an inference.

## Plyometric dose: contacts per session, frequency and progression (S14, S15, S16, S17)

### Takeaway
A 40 to 80 contact range is defensible, but the evidence supports it only as practice. The meta-analytic moderators are >50 jumps per session for vertical jump, >40 for strength and >80 for sprint, mostly in young team-sport athletes. The app's real problem is elsewhere. It gives one exposure a week, on Sprint A only, of mostly low-intensity vertical work (pogos and A-skips), and it grows by adding reps. The literature favors two sessions a week, high-intensity and horizontal jumps, and progression by exercise rather than by reps.

### Cited Findings
- Vertical jump meta-analysis (56 studies, 225 effect sizes): programs of more than 10 weeks and more than 20 sessions, "using high-intensity programs (with more than 50 jumps per session)", maximized gains. Combining jump types (squat, countermovement and drop jumps) beat a single form, and added weight gave no extra benefit. Athletes with more sport experience gained more. — [Sáez de Villarreal et al. 2009, J Strength Cond Res 23(2):495-506, PMID 19197203, doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e318196b7c6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19197203/)
- Sprint meta-analysis (26 studies, 56 effect sizes): the strategies that maximized sprint gains were "training volume for <10 weeks; a minimum of 15 sessions; and high-intensity programs with >80 combined jumps per session". It recommends combining jump types and favoring "greater horizontal acceleration (i.e., sprint-specific plyometric exercises, jumps with horizontal displacement)". — [Sáez de Villarreal et al. 2012, J Strength Cond Res 26(2), PMID 22240550, doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e318220fd03](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22240550/). (The first review marked ">80" as [verify]. This abstract confirms it.)
- Strength meta-analysis by the same lead author: fewer than 10 weeks, more than 15 sessions, high intensity and more than 40 jumps per session were favored (snippet level). — [Sáez de Villarreal et al. 2010, "Does plyometric training improve strength performance? A meta-analysis"](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworks/6329)
- RCT, 29 subjects, 7 weeks: high volume (1,560 jumps) improved fast stretch-shortening actions (drop jump, 20 m sprint) more than moderate volume (780 jumps). On a hard surface, though, moderate volume was enough and more efficient. — [Ramírez-Campillo et al. 2013, J Strength Cond Res, PMID 23254550, doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e318280c9e9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23254550/)
- Volleyball meta-analysis: programs under 2,000 total jumps averaged about 42 jumps per session, those above 2,000 about 160, and both gave similar vertical-jump gains (snippet level). — [Ramírez-Campillo et al. 2020, J Sports Sci Med 19:489-499](https://www.jssm.org/volume19/iss3/cap/jssm-19-489.pdf)
- Youth maturity meta-analysis: a minimal effective dose of 4 weeks, 8 sessions and 92 weekly jumps; no dose effect for weeks, sessions or total jumps (snippet level). — [Ramírez-Campillo et al., plyometric-jump training by maturity](https://researchonline.nd.edu.au/med_article/1648/)
- Scoping reviews of 242 and then 420 plyometric studies: most studies lasted no longer than 7 weeks, and about 55% did not fully describe the prescription. Females (~22 to 24%), individual sports (~7 to 12%) and high-level athletes (~14%) are under-studied, and dose-response relationships "still need to be identified". — [Ramírez-Campillo et al. 2018, Sports Med, PMID 29470823](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29470823/); [Ramírez-Campillo et al. 2020, Scand J Med Sci Sports, PMID 32034819](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32034819/)
- Umbrella review of 29 meta-analyses: plyometrics improve most fitness measures, but only 5 of the 29 meta-analyses used control-group comparisons; the other 24 pooled pre-to-post effects. — [Kons et al. 2023, Sports Med Open, PMID 36625965, PMC9832201](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9832201/)
- Adult female athletes: plyometrics improved sprinting (SMD −0.61), including 10 m (−0.55), 20 m (−0.55) and 30 m (−0.72). — [Zhao et al. 2025, Front Physiol, PMID 41030316, PMC12477254](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12477254/)
- Small RCT (17 taekwondo athletes, 2 sessions a week for 6 weeks): high-intensity low-volume jumping beat low-intensity high-volume jumping for CMJ and drop jump. — [Park et al. 2025, J Sports Med Phys Fitness, PMID 40910931](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40910931/)
- Sprint best practice: sprinters should use "different types of high-intensive bounding, jumping, and skipping exercises" with horizontal power and short ground contacts. Haugen rates science and practice in "good agreement" here. The example weeks put plyometrics on two days a week (Tue and Fri) in the late preparation period and mid-season, each the day after a sprint day. — [Haugen et al. 2019, Sports Med Open 5:44, PMC6872694, doi:10.1186/s40798-019-0221-0](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)

### Inferences
- **S14 (40 to 80 contacts): LABEL AS PRACTICE, keep the range.** It sits inside the meta-analytic per-session moderators (>40 strength, >50 vertical jump), with 80 at the edge of the sprint moderator (>80). Those moderators come from cross-study comparisons, mostly in youth and team-sport athletes, and do not set a cap. The 80 ceiling is a practice choice.
- **S15 (Sprint A only, opening at 65): CHANGE TO two plyometric exposures a week wherever the plan has two or more lower or sprint days, about 40 contacts each for a novice** (sources: de Villarreal 2012, ≥15 sessions inside <10 weeks, which needs about 2 sessions a week; Haugen 2019 Table 3, two plyometric days a week). The opening of 65 is within range. At one exposure a week, the ≥15-session moderator takes 15 weeks.
- **S16 (RIR 4, 60 s rest): LABEL AS PRACTICE.** No source gives RIR for plyometrics. The sources judge plyometric quality by intensity and ground contact time (Haugen 2019), not reps in reserve. The 60 s rest has no source (see Gaps).
- **S17 (pogo +1 rep a session to 20, 65 → 80): CHANGE TO progression by exercise intensity and direction** (pogo and A-skip, then hops and bounds, then box or low drop jumps, mixing types) **rather than by reps** (sources: de Villarreal 2009 and 2012 on high intensity, combined types and horizontal jumps; Park 2025; Haugen 2019). The "6 to 10 weeks" for that progression is practice. A program of pogos and A-skips alone is low-intensity vertical work, which the moderators did not favor.
- A-skips count toward the 65 contacts (20 of them), but they are a drill rather than a jump. The real jump dose at opening is 45 low-intensity pogos.

### Gaps
- I found no primary source that sets plyometric rest periods (the NSCA work-to-rest guidance is a textbook figure I could not verify) or a contact cap for adult beginners. The textbook "beginner 80 to 100 contacts" figure was not checked against a primary source.
- I found no trial of plyometrics in recreational adults training for 40 yd to 400 m.

## Sprint volume: intro, openings, session content and progression cadence (S05, S06, S08, S09, S10, S11, S12, S28, S31)

### Takeaway
Alcaraz 2018's "> 160 m per session, about 2,680 m per program, 2 to 3 times a week, at least 6 weeks" is now confirmed from the full abstract. It comes from resisted-sled programs in recreationally active or trained team-sport men, and the sled groups did not beat their controls. Haugen 2019's best-practice tables are the only source for session shapes, and most of the app's sprint ranges sit inside them. Three numbers fall outside: the 40 m intro, which with two-session steps keeps acceleration under Haugen's 100 m session floor for months; the sprint-endurance rest of 480 s, below Haugen's untrained example and his per-second rule; and the tempo opening of 600 m, below the 1,000 m floor.

### Cited Findings
- Resisted sled meta-analysis (13 studies, 32 sled groups, 15 control groups). Within-group gains: acceleration ES 0.61 (SMD 0.57), full sprint ES 0.36; max-velocity phase not significant (ES 0.27). "Studies that included a control group found a non-significant improvement in participants in the RST group compared with the control group." The effect was "greatest in recreationally active or trained men who practice team sports". Load "is not a determinant", and "the recommended volume is > 160 m per session, and approximately 2680 m per total training program, with a training frequency of two to three times per week, for at least 6 weeks." — [Alcaraz et al. 2018, Sports Med, PMID 29926369, doi:10.1007/s40279-018-0947-8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29926369/). A published correction changed the duration wording to "at least 6 weeks" (search snippet of [doi:10.1007/s40279-018-0957-6](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-018-0957-6.pdf)).
- Haugen best-practice Table 2, sprint method by method (distance, intensity, rest, session volume, hours to the next high-intensity session):
  - Acceleration: 10–50 m, >98%, 2–7 min, 100–300 m, 48 h.
  - Max velocity: 10–30 m flying, >98%, 4–15 min, 50–150 m, 48–72 h.
  - Sprint-specific endurance: 80–150 m, >95%, 8–30 min, 300–900 m, 48–72 h.
  - Speed endurance: 60–80 m, 90–95%, 2–4 min (8–15 between series), 600–2,000 m, 48–72 h.
  - Tempo: 100–300 m, 60–70%, 1–3 min, 1,000–2,000 m, 24 h.

  — [Haugen et al. 2019, PMC6872694](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)
- Haugen's untrained examples:
  - Acceleration: "runs over 20 m from a crouched start with 2-min recovery between each repetition" (elite: 40 m from blocks, 7 min).
  - Max velocity: "a 20-m build-up for 10-m flying sprints with ~4-min recovery".
  - Sprint endurance: "2–3 × 100-m sprints with 10-min recovery may be an adequate sprint-specific endurance session for a relatively untrained junior" (elite: 4–6 × 150 m with 20–30 min).
  - Rule of thumb: "1–2-min recovery is required for every second spent on maximal sprinting."

  All are practice cited to UK Athletics and coaching texts. — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)
- Haugen on starting out: "during the initial weeks of a sprint training program, there should be a gradual familiarization, both in terms of intensity and duration/repetitions". In Vittori's speed-endurance scheme, intensity "began at 90% of maximal sprint velocity in the initial weeks and progressed to 95%", with volume rising from 600–800 m to 1,500–2,000 m. Tempo examples: "10 × 200 m tempo runs with 100-m walking" and "2 × 10 × 100 m tempo". — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)
- Junior soccer players repeated fifteen 20 m sprints with 60 s recovery without fatigue (0.2 ± 0.1% decrement). At 40 m, fatigue appeared after 3 to 4 reps even with 6 min recovery. — [Haugen & Buchheit 2016, Sports Med 46(5):641-656, doi:10.1007/s40279-015-0446-0](https://martin-buchheit.net/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/haugen-buchheit-sprint-monitoring-sports-med-review-in-press.pdf)
- Under-exposure is also a risk. Among 37 elite Gaelic footballers, those who reached >95% of max velocity at least once in training had lower injury risk than those whose best was 85% (OR 0.12), and "under- and over-exposure ... (represented by a U-shaped curve) increased the risk of injury". — [Malone et al. 2017, J Sci Med Sport 20:250-254, PMID 27554923, doi:10.1016/j.jsams.2016.08.005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27554923/)
- Adolescents (n = 28) sprinting two sessions a week for 4 weeks improved max speed (ES 0.83) and eccentric hamstring strength (ES 0.29). Session volume is not in the abstract. — [Freeman et al. 2019, J Sports Med Phys Fitness, PMID 31332988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332988/)

### Inferences
- **S05 (acceleration 4 → 8 × 10 → 30 m, 1 min per 10 m): KEEP, LABEL AS PRACTICE.** Volume runs 40 to 240 m, against Table 2's 100 to 300 m. One minute for 10 m is below Table 2's 2 min floor, but the no-fatigue data for 20 m reps on 60 s (Haugen & Buchheit) support short rest at short distances.
- **S06 (non-intro opening 8 × 20 m = 160 m): KEEP, LABEL AS PRACTICE.** It matches Haugen's 100 to 300 m and Alcaraz's ">160 m". The Alcaraz figure is now verified, but it describes sled programs in team-sport men that did not beat unresisted controls. It is not evidence about novice dose.
- **S08 (intro at 40 m acceleration, 30 m max velocity): CHANGE TO an acceleration opening of about 100 m (for example 5 × 20 m), with the intro's caution carried by effort** (for example build-ups at about 90%, then full effort) **rather than by volume** (sources: Haugen 2019 Table 2's 100 m floor, his untrained 20 m example, and his intensity-and-reps familiarization; Malone 2017 on under-exposure). The arithmetic is what misleads. Starting at 40 m and stepping 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, then 160, one step every two counted sessions, keeps acceleration under 100 m for about 13 counted sessions, roughly 3 months at one acceleration day a week. Alcaraz's "at least 6 weeks" judging window then passes on a dose below every sourced session volume. Max velocity at 3 × 10 m flying matches Haugen's untrained rep shape but sits under Table 2's 50 m floor. LABEL that one AS PRACTICE.
- **S09 (max velocity 3 → 5 × 10 → 20 m flying, 240 s): KEEP, LABEL AS PRACTICE.** It matches Haugen's untrained example (10 m flying after a 20 m build-up, about 4 min).
- **S10 (sprint endurance 3 → 4 × 100 → 150 m, 480 s rest): CHANGE rest TO at least 600 s, scaling with rep duration** (source: Haugen 2019's untrained example, 2–3 × 100 m with 10 min, and his 1–2 min per second of maximal sprinting). A recreational adult's 100 m takes about 13 to 16 s, which that rule turns into 13 to 32 min. 480 s is Table 2's elite floor, applied to the slowest users. For an intro user, open at 2 × 100 m (Haugen's untrained example) rather than 3.
- **S11 (tempo 6 × 100 → 300 m, 60 s walks): LABEL AS PRACTICE.** 60 s sits at Table 2's 1 min floor and near Haugen's "100-m walking" examples. The 600 m opening is below Table 2's 1,000 to 2,000 m. Haugen's examples use 10 reps (10 × 200 m, 2 × 10 × 100 m). A conservative opening is reasonable for a recreational adult, but no source gives 6.
- **S12 (600 s warm-up build-up): LABEL AS PRACTICE.** No primary source sets 10 min (see Gaps).
- **S28 (one step every two counted sessions): LABEL AS PRACTICE.** No source gives a cadence. Haugen supports "gradual" progression, and Alcaraz's at-least-6-week blocks are a minimum duration for judging effect, not a step rate.
- **S31 (intro = three counted sessions): LABEL AS PRACTICE.** Haugen says "initial weeks" without a number. The repeated-bout effect (see the Nordic section) suggests the first one or two exposures carry most of the extra soreness and damage, which fits a short intro.

### Gaps
- I found no trial of sprint dose (metres per session or per week) in recreational adults or novices, and no study of progression cadence.
- I found no primary source for warm-up duration before maximal sprinting.
- The Alcaraz correction text (doi:10.1007/s40279-018-0957-6) was not readable (paywall). Only its "at least 6 weeks" wording is known, from a snippet.

## Weekly sprint-volume caps and hamstring injury: the 15% compounding rule (S29) and tempo outside it

### Takeaway
No study tests a percentage cap on sprint volume. What the team-sport data link to hamstring strains is a transient spike in high-speed running above the athlete's own norm, strongest in the preceding 1 to 2 weeks, together with under-exposure. The compounding rule (`after ≤ before × 1.15^(1+held)`) holds the long-run average near 15% a week. It also lets one week jump by 60 to 100% after a few holds, and that is the kind of spike the data associate with injury.

### Cited Findings
- AFL, one team, two seasons, GPS. Higher-than-typical high-speed running (HSR) raised the odds of hamstring strain: in week −1, OR 6.44 (95% CI 2.99–14.41); weeks −1 and −2 summed, OR 3.06; summed over 4 weeks, OR 1.96 (1.54–2.51). "Exposing players to transient increases in HSR distances above their 2-yearly session average increased the odds". Absolute HSR distances were not associated with risk (snippet). — [Duhig et al. 2016, Br J Sports Med 50:1536-1540](https://eprints.qut.edu.au/221358/)
- AFL, 220 players, 5 teams, 30 hamstring strains. Weekly distance above 24 km/h of more than 653 m gave RR 3.4 (1.6–7.2). Distance above 24 km/h as more than 2.5% of distance above 10 km/h gave RR 6.3 (1.5–26.7). The association was strongest within 7–14 days, but AUC was 0.63, and the authors call the clinical utility at the individual level "limited". — [Ruddy et al. 2018, Br J Sports Med, PMID 27884865, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2016-096777](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27884865/)
- Gaelic football (37 players): a U-shaped relationship, with both under- and over-exposure to maximal velocity raising risk. Players with higher chronic loads (≥4,750 AU) tolerated 90–120 m and 10–15 exposures at max velocity a week, and those exposures were protective (OR 0.22). — [Malone et al. 2017, PMID 27554923](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27554923/)
- Novice distance runners (n = 874; 202 injured): no difference in overall injury rate across weekly progressions of <10%, 10–30% and >30%. For distance-related injuries, >30% vs <10% gave HR 1.59 (0.96–2.66, p = 0.07), and the authors flag the analysis as exploratory. — [Nielsen et al. 2014, J Orthop Sports Phys Ther, PMID 25155475, doi:10.2519/jospt.2014.5164](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25155475/)
- In competitive sprinters, "two-thirds of all hamstring injuries ... occurred in the transition period between specific preparation and competition season", a period of rising intensity. The phase "immediately following the off-season" is also vulnerable, and "excessive and rapid increases in training loads are likely responsible for a large proportion of soft-tissue injuries". The underlying data are from Haugen et al. 2018 (Scand J Med Sci Sports 28:1001-1008, doi:10.1111/sms.12953). — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)
- Tempo runs at 60–70% need only 24 h before the next high-intensity session (Table 2). — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)

### Inferences
- **S29 (15% weekly cap compounding per held session): LABEL the 15% AS PRACTICE. CHANGE the compounding so that no single week's max-speed metres rise more than about 30% over the previous week** (nearest sourced number: Nielsen 2014's >30% band, in novice distance runners, not significant overall). Fill distance in finer units, or alternate rep and distance steps, so the coarse steps cannot spike.
- The spec's own arithmetic shows the spike. Acceleration 80 → 160 m (+100%) passes at held 4, since 1.15^5 = 2.01. A 2-day 100 m plan's week of 130 → 210 m (+62%) passes at held 3, since 1.15^4 × 130 = 227. Duhig's strongest association was a transient rise above the athlete's norm in the week before injury (OR 6.44). Ruddy's was absolute weekly volume above 24 km/h in the following week. A rule that saves up allowance and spends it in one jump produces exactly that pattern.
- Malone's U-curve cuts the other way. Holding users at very low max-speed exposure for months (the S08 intro arithmetic) is not shown to be protective either.
- **Tempo outside the cap (5c Q4): KEEP, LABEL AS PRACTICE.** Haugen's Table 2 treats tempo (60–70%) as low-intensity work with a 24 h turnaround, so it does not count as max-speed metres. The fit rests on the user actually running tempo easy. The Good band, where a Hard word does not count, is the app's only enforcement.
- All of these injury data come from elite team-sport athletes with GPS, whose high-speed running happens inside games. Transferring them to a recreational sprinter's planned metres is an inference.

### Gaps
- No study tests 10%, 15% or 20% weekly caps for sprinting, in any population.
- Critiques of acute:chronic workload ratios (for example Impellizzeri and colleagues, 2020) were not read here.

## Hamstring care: Nordic dose, frequency and evidence strength (S18, S19)

### Takeaway
Both pooled estimates the first review flagged are now verified. Van Dyk 2019 gives RR 0.49 (0.32–0.74) across 15 studies and 8,459 athletes. Impellizzeri 2021, restricted to RCTs that randomized to the Nordic, gives RR 0.59 (0.27–1.29), inconclusive, with a prediction interval of 0.10–3.29. On dose: as few as 48 reps in 6 weeks raised eccentric strength, but low volumes did not lengthen biceps femoris fascicles. A 2025 meta-regression found no volume-strength relation and suggests about 29 to 64 reps a week after a progressive introduction. The app's 2 × 4–6 once a week (8 to 12 reps a week) matches the low-volume arm: strength, no architecture change.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis of 15 studies (8,459 athletes): RR 0.49 (95% CI 0.32–0.74). The eight randomized studies pooled to RR 0.52 (0.32–0.85), and without the high-risk-of-bias studies RR 0.55 (0.34–0.89). — [van Dyk et al. 2019, Br J Sports Med 53(21):1362-1370, PMID 30808663, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2018-100045](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30808663/)
- Reappraisal: only five of those 15 studies were RCTs that randomized to the Nordic. Pooled RR 0.56 (0.20–1.52), prediction interval 0.06–5.14. With one added RCT, RR 0.59 (0.27–1.29), prediction interval 0.10–3.29 (nonparametric 0.17–1.52). The evidence "remains inconclusive", is mostly from high-risk-of-bias RCTs, and supports at best a conditional recommendation, for soccer only. — [Impellizzeri, McCall & van Smeden 2021, J Clin Epidemiol 140:111-124](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/151354)
- Cluster RCT, 942 Danish male soccer players: acute hamstring injuries 3.8 vs 13.1 per 100 player-seasons (RR 0.293, 0.150–0.572); recurrent injuries 7.1 vs 45.8 (RR 0.137); NNT 13 overall, 3 for recurrence. The program was 10 weeks of progressive Nordics, then a weekly seasonal program. — [Petersen et al. 2011, Am J Sports Med, PMID 21825112, doi:10.1177/0363546511419277](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21825112/). The week-by-week sets and reps (2 × 5 in week 1, up to 3 × 12/10/8 three times a week) come from secondary sources only.
- Randomized trial, 40 recreationally active men, four 6-week Nordic volume schedules. Fascicle length rose only with initial high then low volumes (+0.83 cm, +7%, d 0.45) and with progressively increasing volumes (+1.48 cm, +14%, d 0.94). Strength rose with low volume (+14%, d 0.55), high-then-low (+17%, d 0.72) and progressive (+19%, d 0.83). "Low volumes of the NHE were insufficient to increase FL, although as few as 48 repetitions in 6 weeks did increase strength." Fascicle length returned to baseline after detraining. — [Behan et al. 2022, Int J Sports Physiol Perform 17(4):646, PMID 35176727, doi:10.1123/ijspp.2021-0276](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35176727/)
- Twenty recreationally active men did 2 weeks of standardized Nordics, then 4 weeks of high or low volume. Both groups gained fascicle length (+23% and +24%) and eccentric strength (+28% and +34%). Fascicle length reversed within 2 weeks of stopping (−15% to −17%), while strength held for at least 4 weeks. — [Presland et al. 2018, Scand J Med Sci Sports, PMID 29572976, doi:10.1111/sms.13085](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29572976/)
- Systematic review of 13 studies: after interventions of 6 weeks or more, eccentric strength effects were very large for both high volume (g 2.12) and low volume (g 2.28). — [Cuthbert et al. 2020, Sports Med, PMID 31502142, PMC6942028](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6942028/)
- Meta-analysis and meta-regression of 42 studies: prescriptions ranged 3–103 weekly reps (median 50), 1–3 sessions a week (median 2) and 3–39 weeks (median 6). Eccentric Nordic strength ES 0.98, eccentric isokinetic ES 0.52. There was "no association between strength gains and training volume-related variables", and the certainty was low to very low. "A weekly volume of approximately 29-64 NHE repetitions is likely to elicit meaningful strength gains. This target volume should be preceded by a progressive introduction and is typically distributed across 2-3 weekly sessions", and high volumes are "unnecessary during the first 6-8 weeks". — [Franke et al. 2025, J Strength Cond Res 39(10):1088-1104, PMID 40991853, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000005184](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40991853/)
- Adolescents: four weeks of sprint training raised eccentric hamstring strength (ES 0.29), comparable to the Nordic group (ES 0.39), and also improved max speed (ES 0.83). — [Freeman et al. 2019, PMID 31332988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31332988/)

### Inferences
- **S18 (Nordic or a regression on one lift day a week): KEEP. Relabel it from VERIFY to "supported for eccentric strength, contested for injury prevention".** One session a week matches Petersen's in-season maintenance and Behan's low-volume arm, which still gained strength. The injury evidence is almost entirely soccer, and on RCTs only the confidence interval crosses 1. App copy must not say "halves hamstring injuries". "Likely helpful, low risk" is accurate.
- **S19 (2 × 4–6, Range 4..6 at 4): KEEP as the opening dose. CHANGE the progression TO reach about 30 reps a week** (the low end of Franke 2025's 29–64 range), for example 3 × 6–10 once a week or 2 × 6–8 on two days, **over the first 6 to 8 weeks** (sources: Franke 2025; Behan 2022). 8 to 12 reps a week equals Behan's low-volume arm: strength yes, fascicle length no. The opening is right for a first exposure (see the recovery data in the next section). The run plans' "Nordic 2 × 6 (catalog reps)" fits the same verdict.

### Gaps
- I found no Nordic injury RCT in sprinters or recreational adults; all are soccer, with a few in other football codes.
- Behan 2022's exact per-group sets and reps were not readable (the IJSPP PDF returned 403). Only the 48-rep total for the lowest group is confirmed.
- Petersen 2011's week-by-week protocol table was not read in the primary source.

## Day order: recovery after Nordic and lower-body work before max velocity (S01, S02, S03, S23)

### Takeaway
Two recent studies measured recovery after a Nordic session. After 4 × 10 Nordics, high-speed horizontal force stayed reduced for 48 h, and the authors advise 72 h before demanding sprinting. After a first bout of 24 reps in trained men, eccentric strength stayed down through day 3, though a repeated bout 4 weeks later affected only day 1. Haugen's example weeks put strength the day after a sprint day and tempo or rest the day before. The 3-day row (A + plyos, R, Lower + Nordic, B, R) puts the hardest hamstring work directly before the max-velocity session, against both.

### Cited Findings
- Twenty-four men, 12 of them doing 4 × 10 Nordics, then a 30 m sprint at 24, 48 and 72 h. Maximum power was reduced at 24 h (d 0.22) and high-speed horizontal force up to 48 h (d 0.22). The authors suggest "separating weekly NHE sessions from competition or demanding training ... by at least 72 hours." — [Alonso-Fernandez et al. 2024, Sports Biomech, PMID 34748450, doi:10.1080/14763141.2021.1992493](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34748450/)
- Thirteen trained men did 24 Nordic reps. After the first bout, eccentric strength fell through day 3 (ES −2.28 to −1.95), range of motion through day 4, and soreness peaked on days 2 and 3. A second bout 4 weeks later reduced strength only on day 1 (ES −1.20). "While recovery is needed when a Nordic hamstring exercise session is performed for the first time, following Nordic hamstring exercise sessions may scarcely interfere with the training routine." — [Coratella et al. 2025, Int J Sports Med, PMID 40280182, doi:10.1055/a-2595-3622](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40280182/)
- Sixty-five women did 5 × 8 Nordics. Soreness in the control group was higher at 48 h than at baseline. — [Ozmen et al. 2017, J Bodyw Mov Ther, PMID 28167188](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28167188/)
- Haugen Table 3 (best practice) example weeks run: Mon sprint (acceleration), Tue strength (plus plyometrics in late prep), Wed tempo, Thu sprint (max velocity or speed endurance), Fri strength, Sat tempo, Sun off. Intensive sprint sessions "require at least 48 h of recovery", and "sprinting on consecutive days rarely occurs". Table 2 gives 48 h after acceleration and 48–72 h after max velocity. — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)
- In a five-week trial, eccentric and concentric hamstring training groups showed no difference in sprint decrement across two sprint sessions 48 h apart (snippet level). — [Duhig et al. 2019, ACU repository](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/download/fad5262b054886a815114610bd08e8cea442905036f7c5df19ac342f91f74288/146502/AM_Duhig_2019_Effect_of_concentric_and_eccentric_hamstring.pdf)

### Inferences
- **S02 (3-day row puts Lower + Nordic directly before Sprint B, max velocity): CHANGE TO "A + plyos, Lower, R, B + Nordic, R".** Lower lifts go the day after Sprint A, as in Haugen Table 3. The Nordic moves to the end of Sprint B (the inventory's own S23 order puts sprints, then lifts, then Nordic), leaving a rest slot before the next Sprint A. An acceptable alternative is "A + plyos, Lower + Nordic, R, B, R", but that gives only one rest slot (about 48 h) where Alonso-Fernandez advises 72 h. Sources: Alonso-Fernandez 2024; Coratella 2025; Haugen 2019 Table 3. This is the order most likely to hurt someone. Max-velocity running is when hamstring strains happen, and this row asks for it at the point of greatest eccentric-strength loss after the first Nordic bouts.
- **S01 (4-day layout): KEEP, LABEL AS PRACTICE.** It mirrors Haugen Table 3, with strength the day after each sprint day and rest before Sprint B. Lower + Nordic is the last slot, though, and the next rotation opens with Sprint A. If the user's calendar puts no rest day between them, the Nordic lands the day before acceleration. Flag the wraparound.
- **S03 (two sprint days at least one rest entry apart): KEEP, LABEL AS PRACTICE.** Haugen's ≥48 h is practice. The app's rest entries are rotation slots, not calendar days, so the 48 h is not guaranteed. Copy should say "leave a day between sprint days".
- **S23 (order: plyometrics, lifts, Nordic, with the sprint block first): KEEP.** It matches the review's order and Haugen's "sprints before strength".
- The repeated-bout data (Coratella) suggest the worst interference is in a user's first one or two Nordic sessions, which is another reason to open low (S19).

### Gaps
- I found no study of a heavy lower-body lifting session (squats or deadlifts) the day before max-velocity sprinting in recreational adults.
- The Alonso-Fernandez participants' training status is not stated in the abstract.

## Gaps in training and the reset threshold (S07, S30)

### Takeaway
Sprint speed itself decays slowly: about 1.5 to 3% slower over a 6-week off-season in professional soccer players. The hamstring adaptation that Nordic work builds decays fast: fascicle length is back to baseline within 2 weeks of stopping, while eccentric strength holds about 4 weeks. Haugen calls the weeks after a break a vulnerable period. Those data point to a reset after 14 days without sprinting. The rule as built waits until 21 days since that day's last session, measured per sprint day.

### Cited Findings
- Fifty-five professional soccer players over a 6-week off-season (2 weeks of no activity, then easy aerobic running 3 times a week). 10 m sprint slowed from 1.74 to 1.79 s and 1.73 to 1.78 s; 20 m from 3.02 to 3.06 s and 3.01 to 3.06 s (both p < 0.001). Squat and countermovement jumps fell about 2 to 3 cm. — [Koundourakis et al. 2014, PLoS One, PMID 24586293, PMC3929557](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3929557/)
- Meta-analysis of 103 studies: stopping training lowers maximal power (SMD −0.20) and maximal force (−0.46), with a dose-response by duration; the effects are larger in older and inactive people. — [Bosquet et al. 2013, Scand J Med Sci Sports, PMID 23347054](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347054/)
- Biceps femoris fascicle length reversed within 2 weeks of stopping Nordic training (−15% to −17%), while eccentric strength held for 4 weeks. — [Presland et al. 2018, PMID 29572976](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29572976/); fascicle length also returned to baseline in [Behan et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35176727/)
- "In sprinters, the training phase immediately following the off-season and the transition phase ... are particularly vulnerable periods for injury." — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)
- Previously injured soccer players in the control arm had recurrent hamstring strains at 45.8 per 100 player-seasons, against 7.1 with Nordic training. — [Petersen et al. 2011, PMID 21825112](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21825112/)

### Inferences
- **S30 (reset when days since the day's last session − 7 ≥ 14, so 21 days): CHANGE TO a reset when 14 or more days have passed since the user's last maximal sprint session of any kind.** Sources: Presland 2018 for the 2-week loss of fascicle length, Haugen 2019 for the vulnerable return period, and the review's "two weeks or more" practice rule. The threshold stays practice-grade. Measuring per sprint day also misfires both ways. A user who skips only Sprint A days keeps sprinting on B and gets a needless A reset. A user who stops entirely sprints again at full volume after 20 days.
- **S07 (intro if no all-out sprint in a month, or a hamstring strain in 12 months): KEEP, LABEL AS PRACTICE.** Petersen's recurrence numbers support treating a recent strain as high risk. The one-month window has no direct source, though it is consistent with measurable detraining over weeks (Koundourakis).

### Gaps
- I found no study of how sprint speed or hamstring injury risk changes after 2-, 3- or 4-week breaks in recreational sprinters. The detraining data are from soccer off-seasons and COVID lockdowns (the lockdown studies were seen only in search snippets).

## Light weeks for sprinters (S27)

### Takeaway
The 2:1 or 3:1 loading pattern is practice, cited by Haugen to coaching texts. No trial tests it, and no study read here shows harm from leaving it out.

### Cited Findings
- "Leading practitioners typically use a 2:1 or 3:1 periodization, that is, 2 or 3 weeks with relatively high training load are followed by an easier training week for recovery purposes" (cited to coaching books). Taper guidance for strength and power sports: 2–3 weeks with a 40–60% cut in volume, intensity and frequency kept. — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)
- Under-exposure to maximal velocity also raised injury risk in Gaelic football (U-curve). — [Malone et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27554923/)

### Inferences
- **S27 (sprint plans get only the lift light week, every 6 rotations, for a year or more of training; no sprint light week): LABEL AS PRACTICE.** If a sprint light week is added, it should cut volume and keep some near-maximal reps (Malone 2017; Haugen 2019), as the first review said. The 15% cap with holds already slows weekly growth, so the lack of a light week is not the main risk.

### Gaps
- I found no trial of deload frequency in sprinters.

## Timing, comparison and time trials (S24, S25, S26, S32, S33)

### Takeaway
The first review attributed the "0.22 to 0.26 s" hand-timing bias to Hetzler. It actually comes from Mann et al. 2015 (n = 81 NCAA Division I players). Mayhew 2010 found 0.31 s. Hetzler 2008 found a small mean bias (−0.04 to −0.05 s) but absolute errors of 0.15 to 0.16 s on 25 m splits, and no reliable correction. The bias depends on how the start is timed, which supports "never convert". The 0.10 s SAME band for hand times is tighter than the measured noise: Mann's smallest worthwhile difference is 0.14 s hand-timed and 0.12 s electronic. A single trial over short distances is the least reliable measurement, and the error falls with √n trials.

### Cited Findings
- NCAA Division II players (n = 59), 40 yd, timed by 7 experienced hand timers against electronic timing (hand-lift start). Hand timing was faster by 0.31 ± 0.07 s (6.0 ± 1.3%; 95% CI of the difference −0.44 to −0.18 s). The maximum difference among hand timers on any one trial was 0.19 ± 0.14 s. — [Mayhew et al. 2010, J Strength Cond Res, PMID 20072055, doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181c08860](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20072055/)
- NCAA Division I players (n = 81), two 40 yd trials. Hand timing was faster than electronic by 0.22 ± 0.07 s (experienced timers) and 0.26 ± 0.08 s (novices). The smallest worthwhile difference was "0.12 seconds with electronic timing and 0.14 seconds with hand timing". — [Mann et al. 2015, J Strength Cond Res, PMID 25785707, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000000941](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25785707/)
- 26 inexperienced timers timed trained runners (8 men, 10 women) at each 25 m split of a 200 m course. Mean error against electronic timing was −0.04 ± 0.24 and −0.05 ± 0.24 s, and absolute error 0.15 ± 0.20 and 0.16 ± 0.19 s. The stopwatch splits were faster in 67.3% of cases. The authors found no reliable way to correct hand times and recommend reporting them uncorrected. — [Hetzler et al. 2008, J Strength Cond Res 22(6):1969-1976, doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e318185f36c](https://www.sponet.de/sponet/Record/4015541?lng=en)
- A traditional 0.2 s correction exists for hand timing. Manual timing and single-beam photocells "should be avoided over short sprint distances (10–20 m)". The smallest worthwhile change is about 1% for 10–40 m sprints and max speed. A single trial's typical error can be about 2%, and "the TE decreases by a factor of √n repetitions". Fatigue appears after 3–4 reps at 40 m even with 6 min rest. A +2.0 m/s tailwind gives a 0.10–0.14 s faster 100 m, and a headwind of the same speed 0.12–0.17 s slower. — [Haugen & Buchheit 2016, Sports Med 46(5):641-656, doi:10.1007/s40279-015-0446-0](https://martin-buchheit.net/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/haugen-buchheit-sprint-monitoring-sports-med-review-in-press.pdf)
- Elite sprinters improve only 0.1 to 0.2% a year, and national-level ones 1.3 to 1.4% a year from age 18 (as read in the first review). — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)

### Inferences
- **S24 (compare only within distance, method and start): KEEP.** The measured hand-to-electronic bias ranges from 0.04 s (Hetzler, flying splits) to 0.31 s (Mayhew, first-movement starts), so no single conversion is valid. Correct the first review's attribution: 0.22 to 0.26 s is Mann 2015, not Hetzler.
- **S25 (hand times within 0.10 s of the best read SAME): CHANGE TO about 0.15 s for hand timing** (sources: Mann 2015's smallest worthwhile difference of 0.14 s at 40 yd; Hetzler 2008's absolute split error of 0.15–0.16 s; Mayhew 2010's 0.19 s spread between timers). The error is absolute (reaction at the start and finish), so it does not shrink at longer distances. Treating 0.10–0.14 s as a real change will report noise as personal bests. For electronic or video timing, "equal hundredths" is stricter than Mann's 0.12 s at 40 yd but harmless, since it only decides the SAME label.
- **S26 (40 yd stored as 37 m): KEEP.** 40 yd is 36.576 m. Since times are never compared across distances, the 0.42 m rounding does not affect any comparison.
- **S32 (a time trial every fifth rotation, never in the intro): KEEP, LABEL AS PRACTICE.** No study sets testing frequency. With a smallest worthwhile change of about 1% and a single-trial error of about 2% (Haugen & Buchheit), a 5-week trial in trained users will often show changes within the noise. Copy should say so.
- **S33 (time trial = one rep at the goal distance): CHANGE TO two reps with full recovery for 40 yd and 60 m, keeping one rep for 100 to 400 m** (sources: Haugen & Buchheit 2016 on error falling with √n and short distances needing repeats; Mann 2015's two-trial protocol; fatigue at 40 m only after 3–4 reps). The lead's reason, an intro sprinter's volume, applies to 100 m and up, where 2 × 100 m is a heavy maximal dose. At 37 m, 2 × 37 m = 74 m is about one acceleration session.

### Gaps
- I found no primary data on hand-timing error over 100, 200 or 400 m from a standing start, or on smartphone-video precision beyond Stanton 2016's mean bias of 0.13 s (cited in the first review, not re-read).

The 200–400 m tradition progresses from long distances to short. — [Haugen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/)

## Verdict per rule, and which rules would most mislead or injure a real user

### Takeaway
Two rules carry real injury risk. The 3-day row puts Lower + Nordic the day before max-velocity sprinting, and the 15% compounding cap can release a 60 to 100% one-week jump in max-speed metres after a few holds. The 21-day per-day gap reset and the 480 s sprint-endurance rest come next. The rules most likely to mislead are the 0.10 s hand-time SAME band, the one-rep 40 yd and 60 m time trial, the 40 m intro with its months-long climb, once-a-week low-intensity plyometrics, and Nordic copy that might say "halves injuries".

Ranked by harm to a real user:
1. **S02.** Hamstring strains occur at max velocity, and this row schedules max velocity the day after the Nordic and lower lifts, inside the 48 to 72 h window in which hamstring force and eccentric strength are measurably down. The impairment is worst in a user's first Nordic sessions, exactly when a new 3-day user meets this row.
2. **S29.** The compounding allowance turns a steady average into an acute one-week spike (acceleration 80 → 160 m, +100%, at held 4; a weekly 130 → 210 m, +62%, at held 3). That matches the "transient increase above the athlete's norm" Duhig linked to OR 6.44 the following week.
3. **S30.** A user who stops for 15 to 20 days returns at full volume after losing fascicle length (gone within 2 weeks), in the post-break period Haugen calls vulnerable. Measuring per day misfires in both directions.
4. **S10.** At 480 s between maximal 100 to 150 m reps, slower recreational users get about half the rest the sources give untrained athletes. Form breaks under fatigue in long reps (likely injury relevance, not directly measured).
5. **Misleading, low injury risk:**
   - S25's 0.10 s band reports noise as personal bests.
   - S33's one-rep 40 yd or 60 m trial is the least reliable test the review literature describes.
   - S08's 40 m intro plus two-session steps leaves acceleration under 100 m for about 13 sessions, so users judge the program on an under-dose.
   - S15 and S17 deliver once-a-week, low-intensity vertical plyometrics that the meta-analyses did not test as effective.
   - S18 and S19 copy must not promise "halves hamstring injuries". The RCT-only estimate's confidence interval crosses 1.

### Gaps
- Every injury association above comes from elite team-sport GPS data or soccer Nordic trials. No source studies the app's population, recreational adults training for 40 yd to 400 m, so every threshold above remains practice-grade, even where a number now has a source.
