# Progression models, autoregulation, deloads and missed sessions in resistance training

Evidence grades used throughout:
- **Strong**: consistent meta-analyses or an overview of reviews, effect direction stable.
- **Moderate**: one or two meta-analyses or several RCTs, some heterogeneity or small effects.
- **Weak/contested**: single small RCTs, exploratory meta-regressions, conflicting meta-analyses.
- **Preference/practice**: expert consensus, surveys, coaching convention, or no direct evidence.

Abstracts below were pulled from PubMed (E-utilities) and full texts from Europe PMC where noted. Effect sizes are SMD/Hedges g/Cohen d unless marked.

## 1. Linear vs undulating vs block periodization

### Takeaway
Periodized training beats non-periodized training for 1RM by a small margin (ES ~0.3-0.4), and undulating beats linear only in trained lifters (ES ~0.6 in one subgroup, wide CI). Hypertrophy does not differ by model. The 2026 ACSM position stand rates the periodization question "insufficient data" for strength and "no effect" for hypertrophy, and concludes that periodization matters less than progressive overload. A generator should not pick a model on evidence grounds. Use simple linear/double progression for novices, add weekly or daily variation for intermediate and advanced users, and present the choice as a preference. **Grade: moderate (small strength edge for periodization/UP in trained); strong (no hypertrophy difference).**

### Cited Findings
- Harries et al. 2015 (17 studies, n=510): no difference between linear and undulating periodization for upper- or lower-body strength. Studies were short-term, and training history was a confounder. JSCR 29(4):1113-25, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000000712. [PubMed 25268290](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25268290/)
- Williams et al. 2017 (18 studies, 81 effects, 1988-2015): periodized > non-periodized for 1RM, ES = 0.43 (95% CI 0.27-0.58). The undulating model was more favorable (β = 0.51). Gains were larger in untrained participants (β = -0.59 for training status), with higher frequency (β = 0.46) and in longer studies (β = 0.03). Sports Med 47(10):2083-2100, doi:10.1007/s40279-017-0734-y. [PubMed 28497285](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28497285/)
- Moesgaard et al. 2022 (35 studies, volume-equated only): periodized > non-periodized for 1RM, ES 0.31 (95% CI 0.04-0.57). No hypertrophy difference (ES 0.13, CI -0.10 to 0.36). UP > LP for 1RM, ES 0.31 (0.02-0.61), but **only in trained** participants: trained ES 0.61 (0.00-1.22) vs untrained ES 0.06 (-0.20 to 0.31). No LP vs UP hypertrophy difference (ES 0.05). Sports Med 52(7):1647-1666, doi:10.1007/s40279-021-01636-1. [PubMed 35044672](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/)
- Grgic et al. 2017 (13 studies): LP vs DUP hypertrophy difference d = -0.02 (95% CI -0.25 to 0.21), i.e. similar. PeerJ 5:e3695, doi:10.7717/peerj.3695. [PubMed 28848690](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28848690/)
- Zhang et al. 2026 (29 studies, search to July 2025): LP and UP comparable for athletic capacity, body composition and glucose regulation. UP was better for lean mass in obese individuals and for short-term weight loss. Front Public Health 14:1707627, doi:10.3389/fpubh.2026.1707627. [PubMed 41869632](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41869632/)
- ACSM 2026 position stand (overview of 137 systematic reviews, >30,000 participants, search to Oct 2024): "periodization did not consistently impact training outcomes." For strength there were "insufficient data to determine if strength was affected by … intrasession autoregulation, varied exercise selection, periodization." Hypertrophy was not affected by periodization (linear, undulating, nonperiodized). Conclusion: "With appropriate progressive overload, periodization is not significantly superior to nonperiodized programs; thus, periodization is less important than previously hypothesized." Strength was enhanced by heavier loads (≥80% 1RM), full ROM, 2-3 sets, placing the exercise early in the session, and ≥2 sessions/wk. Hypertrophy was enhanced by ≥10 sets/wk. Med Sci Sports Exerc 58(4):851-872, doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897. [PubMed 41843416](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/); full text via [Europe PMC PMC12965823](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)
- ACSM 2009 (superseded): recommended 8-12RM for novices; for intermediate (~6 months of consistent training) and advanced users, 1-12RM "in a periodized fashion" with emphasis on 1-6RM; frequency 2-3 d/wk novice, 3-4 intermediate, 4-5 advanced. doi:10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670. [PubMed 19204579](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)
- Block periodization: I found no meta-analysis of block periodization for resistance training strength. The only block-periodization meta-analysis surfaced was for endurance training (Open Access J Sports Med 2019, doi:10.2147/OAJSM.S180408). [PubMed 31802956](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31802956/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default:** novices (<~6 months of consistent training) get a single rep target per lift with load or double progression and no planned undulation. The evidence shows no benefit of UP over LP in untrained lifters (Moesgaard untrained ES 0.06), and simplicity aids adherence.
- Intermediate and advanced users: offer weekly or daily undulation, for example a heavy day (3-6 reps) and a moderate day (8-12 reps) per lift. UP's small edge appears only in trained lifters, and the CI's lower bound touches zero, so present it as "slightly better for strength, optional," not mandatory.
- Do not sell block periodization as evidence-based for general users. It is a coaching structure for peaking, with no RT meta-analytic support found.
- Moesgaard notes that the periodization effect may be neurophysiological (specificity and practice at heavy loads) rather than hypertrophic. Heavy exposure (≥80% 1RM) belongs in any strength-goal template regardless of model, and ACSM 2026 rates load as a positive strength modifier.
- **Ask the user:** training age (months of consistent lifting), goal (strength vs size vs general), and whether they prefer the same workout each week or variety. Variety preference can drive UP selection with no evidence cost.

### Gaps
- No RT-specific block-periodization meta-analysis found. Few studies longer than ~12-16 weeks, so long-term superiority of any model is untested.
- The ACSM 2026 per-item quality-of-evidence percentages for periodization were in tables I could only partially parse ("Progression: Periodization ? ND").

## 2. Autoregulation (RPE/RIR, APRE, velocity) vs percentage-based loading; RIR estimation accuracy

### Takeaway
Autoregulated load prescription produces strength gains equal to or slightly better than fixed %1RM. Pooled differences are small (SMD ~0.2) and often non-significant, but no study shows it is worse. The clearest mechanism is that fixed %1RM programs based on a pre-test 1RM under-load the lifter as they get stronger. Lifters underestimate reps to failure by ~1 rep on average. Accuracy improves near failure, with heavier loads and in later sets, and depends little on training status. **Grade: moderate (autoregulation ≥ percentage); moderate (RIR error ~1 rep, training status matters little); weak/contested (VBT superiority; APRE ranking).**

### Cited Findings
- Helms et al. 2018 (trained males 19-35 y, squat and bench 3x/wk DUP for 8 wk, RPE group n=10 vs %1RM group n=11, sets and reps matched): both improved. The difference was non-significant, with a small probable advantage for RPE: squat +17.05 ± 5.44 kg (RPE) vs +13.91 ± 5.89 kg (%1RM), ES 0.50 ± 0.63; bench +10.70 vs +9.64 kg. No hypertrophy difference. Front Physiol 9:247, doi:10.3389/fphys.2018.00247. [PubMed 29628895](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29628895/)
- Graham & Cleather 2019/2021 (31 trained males, 2x/wk squat, 12 wk, intensity and volume theoretically matched): RIR autoregulation > fixed loading. Front squat +11.7% vs +8.3% (p=0.004); back squat +10.8% vs +7.1% (p=0.006). The AR group trained heavier: back squat average 83.6% vs 80.4% 1RM. Fixed loading used the pre-test 1RM, so AR "accommodate[d] the increasing strength level" during the program. JSCR 35(9):2451-2456, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000003164. [Author PDF](https://research.stmarys.ac.uk/3067/3/Graham-Cleather-Autoregulation-Repetitions-in-Reserve.pdf); [PubMed 31009432](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31009432/)
- Larsen et al. 2021, systematic review (14 studies, 356 participants, no pooled meta-analysis): all autoregulation protocols (RIR-RPE, flexible DUP, velocity targets, velocity loss) increased 1RM with small-to-large ES. The authors call them "could be effective." PeerJ 9:e10663, doi:10.7717/peerj.10663. [PubMed 33520457](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33520457/)
- Hickmott et al. 2022 (15 studies, trained individuals): autoregulated (RPE/RIR or VBT) vs standardized load gave **no significant 1RM difference**, MD = 2.07 kg (95% CI -0.32 to 4.46), SMD = 0.21. Velocity-loss thresholds ≤25% gave more 1RM (MD 2.32 kg, SMD 0.23) but less hypertrophy than >25% (MD 0.61 cm², SMD 0.28). Sports Med Open 8:9, doi:10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9. [PubMed 35038063](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35038063/)
- Huang et al. 2025 network meta-analysis: for back squat 1RM, "no moderate/large effect sizes were observed between interventions." SUCRA ranked APRE (93%) > RPE (67%) > VBT (27%) > %1RM (13%). For bench, the text reports PBRT vs APRE SMD = -0.83. The authors conclude APRE, VBT and RPE > %1RM. SUCRA rankings from small networks are fragile, so treat the ranking as weak. J Exerc Sci Fit 23(4):360-369, doi:10.1016/j.jesf.2025.07.006. [PubMed 40791980](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40791980/)
- VBT vs %1RM: Zhang et al. 2022 (7 studies in meta-analysis, n=139): SMD = 0.26 (95% CI 0.03-0.49) favoring VBT for maximal strength; athletes subgroup 0.35. Int J Sports Med 43(12):981-995, doi:10.1055/a-1790-8546. [PubMed 35255509](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35255509/). Contradicted by Wang et al. 2026 (17 studies, n=348, trained): maximal strength SMD = 0.21 (95% CI -0.01 to 0.43, p=0.064), not significant; VBT was better for jumps (0.27) and change of direction (0.45). BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil 18:57, doi:10.1186/s13102-025-01504-9. [PubMed 41491263](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41491263/)
- ACSM 2026: "insufficient data to determine if strength was affected by … intrasession autoregulation." It also notes that the same relative load "can also be sustained when regular strength testing is performed or perceived exertion scales are used to increase absolute load commensurate with strength gains." [Europe PMC PMC12965823](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)
- RIR accuracy, Halperin et al. 2022 (scoping review plus exploratory meta-analysis, 12 studies, n=414): participants **underpredicted reps to failure by 0.95 reps** (95% CI 0.17-1.73), with very high heterogeneity (I² = 97.9%). Accuracy improved closer to failure and with lower rep counts (≤12 reps β = 0.06; >12 reps β = 0.47) and slightly in later sets. **Training status did not influence accuracy** (β = -0.006). Upper vs lower body showed no clear difference. Sports Med 52(2):377-390, doi:10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x. [PubMed 34542869](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/)
- Remmert et al. 2023 (27 men, 31 women; single-joint machine exercises at 72.5% 1RM to failure): sex, training experience and experience using RIR did not affect accuracy. Accuracy was better closer to failure and in later sets. Percept Mot Skills 130(3):1239-1254, doi:10.1177/00315125231169868. [PubMed 37036795](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37036795/)
- Refalo et al. 2024 (24 trained men and women, bench at 75% 1RM): raw RIR error -0.17 ± 1.00 and absolute error 0.65 ± 0.78 reps when calling 1 and 3 RIR, equivalent within ±1 rep. No relationship with sex, years of training or relative strength. JSCR 38(3):e78-e85, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000004653. [PubMed 37967832](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37967832/)
- RIR-based RPE scale origin: Zourdos et al. 2016, JSCR 30(1), doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000001049. [PubMed 26049792](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26049792/)
- Proximity to failure matters for hypertrophy, not strength. Robinson et al. 2024 meta-regressions: for strength, the slope of estimated RIR included zero ("strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR"); hypertrophy improved as sets ended closer to failure. Exploratory. Sports Med 54(9):2209-2231, doi:10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2. [PubMed 38970765](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/). ACSM 2026 likewise found strength "not affected in programs with lifting to fatigue/failure."

### Inferences
- **Generator default:** prescribe working sets as a rep target plus an RIR target (e.g. "3 x 5 @ 2 RIR"), not as a fixed %1RM of a stale test. Let the logged reps and RIR drive the next load. This captures the main Graham & Cleather benefit (load tracks rising strength) at no cost. A %1RM display can be derived from the e1RM for users who want it.
- Use **RIR 1-3** for compound strength work and **0-2** for isolation and hypertrophy work. Strength is insensitive to RIR in this range; hypertrophy benefits from being closer to failure. Avoid prescribing RIR >4. Accuracy is worse far from failure and with high-rep sets (>12), so RIR self-report on 15-20-rep sets is unreliable.
- Bias correction: users underpredict by ~1 rep on average, which means they are actually further from failure than they report. A generator can treat a reported "2 RIR" as roughly 2-3 RIR. Do not add a novice-specific large penalty, because evidence says training status barely matters (Halperin, Remmert, Refalo).
- Calibration aid (inference, practice-based): an occasional AMRAP last set at a known load lets the app check RIR calls against real reps. Accuracy improves near failure and in later sets, so the last set is the best place for it.
- VBT needs hardware. The app (no network, phone and watch) can't measure bar velocity reliably, so treat VBT as out of scope. Its strength advantage is not robust anyway (Wang 2026, p = 0.064).
- **Ask the user:** "Are you comfortable judging how many more reps you could have done?" If not, fall back to a fixed rep target with load or double progression and an optional per-set "how hard?" tap (easy / right / grinder / missed).

### Gaps
- No head-to-head trials in novices of RIR-based vs %1RM prescription were found, apart from mixed samples. Most autoregulation RCTs used trained young men.
- I did not retrieve the exact APRE adjustment table (Mann et al. 2010, JSCR, doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181def4a6, [PubMed 20543732](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20543732/)). It should be checked against the original before a generator copies its rep-to-load mapping.

## 3. Double progression and rep-range progression

### Takeaway
"Double progression" (work up through a rep range at a fixed load, then add load and drop back to the bottom of the range) has almost no direct trial evidence. It is effectively the ACSM 2009 rule ("+2-10% when you exceed the target by 1-2 reps"). The one RCT on rep vs load progression found similar outcomes in trained lifters. **Grade: weak (one 8-week RCT) for rep progression ≈ load progression; preference/practice for the exact double-progression mechanics.**

### Cited Findings
- Plotkin et al. 2022 (43 trained participants, ≥1 yr of lower-body training, 4 sets of 4 lower-body exercises 2x/wk for 8 wk). LOAD (add load, keep reps constant) vs REPS (add reps, keep load constant): rectus femoris growth modestly favored REPS (sum of sites +2.8 mm, CI90% -0.5 to 5.8). Smith squat 1RM slightly favored LOAD (+2.0 kg, CI -2.4 to 7.8), "of questionable practical significance." Other outcomes were near-identical with narrow CIs. "Both progressions … appear to be viable." PeerJ 10:e14142, doi:10.7717/peerj.14142. [PubMed 36199287](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36199287/)
- ACSM 2009: "When training at a specific RM load, it is recommended that 2-10% increase in load be applied when the individual can perform the current workload for one to two repetitions over the desired number." This is expert consensus, not a trial result. [PubMed 19204579](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)
- NSCA-style "2-for-2 rule": if the lifter can do 2 or more reps beyond the goal on the last set in 2 consecutive sessions, add load next session. Suggested increments for less-trained lifters are upper body 2.5-5 lb (1-2 kg) and lower body 5-10 lb (2-4 kg); for more-trained lifters, upper 5-10 lb and lower 10-15 lb. **Only secondary exam-prep sources were found, not the NSCA primary text.** [CSCS prep summary](https://app.achievable.me/study/cscs/learn/program-design-for-resistance-training-training-frequency-exercise-order-and-training-load-and-repetitions); [Open exam prep](https://open-exam-prep.com/exams/cscs/program-design/resistance-training-program-design)

### Inferences
- **Generator default (practice-based, consistent with ACSM 2009 and Plotkin):**
  - Compound strength lifts: fixed rep target (e.g. 5), add load when all sets hit the target at ≤ target RIR.
  - Accessory and hypertrophy lifts: double progression in a range (e.g. 8-12). When **all working sets reach the top of the range at the target RIR** (or the first set beats the top by ≥1 rep), raise the load by the smallest increment that drops reps to about the bottom of the range, roughly 2.5-10% depending on lift and equipment.
  - Exercises where load can't be raised in small enough steps (fixed dumbbells, machines with 10-15 lb stacks, bodyweight): progress reps (then sets or tempo) first. Plotkin supports rep progression as a valid substitute.
- The "2 consecutive sessions" confirmation step (2-for-2) is a reasonable anti-noise rule for users without RIR input. With RIR input, a single session at or above target with RIR ≥ target is sufficient (inference).
- **Ask the user:** the available equipment increments (smallest plate pair, dumbbell jumps, machine stack step, microplates yes/no) and lb vs kg. Increment feasibility, not physiology, determines whether load or rep progression is used.

### Gaps
- No trial compares double progression against fixed-rep load progression or against RIR autoregulation over long periods. Plotkin covered lower body only, in trained lifters, for 8 weeks.
- The NSCA increments could not be verified against the NSCA Essentials textbook itself.

## 4. Realistic progression rates by training status and lift; fixed vs percentage increments

### Takeaway
Strength gains are fastest early and decelerate roughly logarithmically, reaching a practical plateau within ~1-2 years on minimal-dose programs. Elite athletes gain ~0.4% per session and ~7% per year. Competitive lifters still add ~10-25 kg/yr per lift, with lower-body lifts gaining about twice the absolute kilos of bench press. The strongest lifters progress slowest. In absolute terms, increments should differ by lift; a percentage-of-load rule with plate rounding handles this automatically. **Grade: moderate (deceleration with training age; lower-body > upper-body absolute gains); preference (exact increment rule).**

### Cited Findings
- Steele et al. 2023 (n=14,690 adults, 60% female, age 48 ± 11, minimal dose of 1x/wk single sets to failure on 6 exercises, up to 6.8 yr of records): linear-log growth. "By ~1-2 years strength had practically reached a 'plateau'"; "~30-50% gains over the first year reaching ~50-60% of baseline 6 years later." Sex, bodyweight and age had minimal interaction effects. Res Q Exerc Sport 94(4):913-930, doi:10.1080/02701367.2022.2070592. [PubMed 35591809](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35591809/)
- McMaster et al. 2013 (27 studies, n=1,015 elite rugby/American football players): 3-6 sets of 4-10 reps at 74-88% 1RM gave strength increases of **0.42% per session**. Weekly strength change was 0.9%, 1.8% and 1.3% for 2, 3 and 4 sessions per muscle region per week. Long-term strength gains were 7.1% (12 mo), 8.5% (24 mo) and 12.5% (48 mo), showing diminishing returns past 12-24 months. Sports Med 43(5):367-84, doi:10.1007/s40279-013-0031-3. [PubMed 23529287](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23529287/)
- Latella et al. 2020 (1,897 powerlifters, 2003-2018): total strength gain/day was 0.12 ± 0.69 kg (F) and 0.15 ± 0.44 kg (M), similar between sexes. The strongest males gained 0.102 kg/day vs 0.211 kg/day for the least strong. JSCR 34(9):2412-2418, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000003657. [PubMed 32865942](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32865942/)
- Latella et al. 2022 (n=1,896 powerlifters): maximum yearly gains were squat 20.2-25.4 kg/yr and deadlift 18.1-21.1 kg/yr vs bench 10.5-12.8 kg/yr (P ≤ 0.001). No sex difference in absolute or relative change. Med Sci Sports Exerc 54(5):841-850, doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000002858. [PubMed 35019902](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35019902/)
- Rhea et al. 2003 (140 studies, 1,433 ES): optimal mean intensity was 60% 1RM for untrained vs 80% for trained; 3 d/wk per muscle for untrained vs 2 d/wk for trained; 4 sets per muscle group for both. MSSE 35(3):456-64, doi:10.1249/01.MSS.0000053727.63505.D4. [PubMed 12618576](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12618576/)
- Williams 2017: training status moderated 1RM gains (β = -0.59), with larger improvements among untrained participants. [PubMed 28497285](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28497285/)
- Pelland et al. 2026 (67 studies, n=2,058): strength rises with weekly volume and frequency but with pronounced diminishing returns for strength (more pronounced than for hypertrophy). Sports Med 56(2):481-505, doi:10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w. [PubMed 41343037](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/)
- ACSM 2009 increments: 2-10% load increase (see section 3). [PubMed 19204579](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)
- Commonly cited figures (from memory, **not verified this session**): the ACSM 2009 body text, citing Kraemer & Ratamess, gives average strength improvements of ~40% (untrained), 20% (moderately trained), 16% (trained), 10% (advanced) and 2% (elite) over periods of 4 weeks to 2 years. Verify against the [ACSM 2009 full text](https://journals.lww.com/acsm-msse/fulltext/2009/03000/progression_models_in_resistance_training_for.26.aspx) before using.

### Inferences
- **Fixed-lb increments will over-progress light lifts and under-progress heavy ones.** Powerlifters add about twice the kilos per year on squat and deadlift as on bench (Latella 2022), and lighter lifts (e.g. lateral raises) can't absorb 5 lb jumps. **Generator default:** increment = **~2.5% of current working load for upper body / small-muscle lifts and ~5% for lower body / large-muscle lifts**, rounded to the nearest loadable increment. Use the ACSM 2-10% band as the outer bound, with a floor of the smallest available plate step. Novices at light loads will in practice hit the floor (2.5 lb upper, 5 lb lower), which matches the NSCA-style figures. This rule is a defensible synthesis, not a tested rule.
- Expected rates to show users (inference from Steele, McMaster, Latella): novices gain fast for months, then flatten by year 1-2. Elite and trained lifters gain about 0.5-2%/week at best in a block, and about 7-12% per year in the long run. A generator should therefore expect progress to slow from **per-session** (novice) to **per-week** (intermediate, ~6-24 months) to **per-block/month** (advanced). Switch progression cadence when the user stalls (see section 5) rather than by calendar.
- Do not apply a sex-specific progression modifier. Absolute and relative change did not differ by sex in the powerlifting data or in Steele's minimal-dose data.
- **Ask the user:** months/years of consistent training, current working weights or recent best sets (to seed an e1RM), and age. Age matters more for detraining (section 7) than for progression rate in Steele's data.

### Gaps
- No RCT tests percentage-based vs fixed-mass increment rules. The upper/lower split is inferred from absolute-gain differences and practitioner convention.
- The Steele cohort was older (mean 48) and on a minimal dose, so its plateau timing may differ for higher-volume young lifters. Powerlifting data are survivorship-biased (people who keep competing).

## 5. Missed reps and failed sessions: hold, reduce, reset

### Takeaway
No RCT or meta-analysis tests specific rules for what to do after missed reps (hold vs drop 5-10%). Common coaching rules ("repeat the weight; after 2-3 stalls reset ~10%") are practice-based. The evidence does give boundary conditions. Strength is insensitive to proximity to failure, so an occasional missed rep is not harmful, but grinding to failure has no strength payoff. Autoregulated loading that tracks daily readiness performs at least as well as fixed plans. APRE, a rule-based load adjustment from reps achieved on a test set, ranks well in a network meta-analysis. **Grade: preference/practice (specific reset percentages); moderate (missing a target occasionally is not harmful; failure isn't needed for strength).**

### Cited Findings
- Training to failure did not enhance strength: ACSM 2026 lists failure among variables that "did not consistently impact training outcomes" ([Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)). Robinson 2024 found strength gains "similar across a wide range of RIR" ([PubMed 38970765](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/)).
- Velocity-loss thresholds ≤25% (stopping sets earlier) produced more 1RM gain than >25% (SMD 0.23), suggesting that excess within-set fatigue blunts strength ([Hickmott 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35038063/)).
- Fixed loading from a pre-test can't respond to daily state, while RIR autoregulation lets load go down on bad days and up on good ones. Graham & Cleather: AR group +10.8% vs +7.1% back squat ([Author PDF](https://research.stmarys.ac.uk/3067/3/Graham-Cleather-Autoregulation-Repetitions-in-Reserve.pdf)). Larsen 2021 frames autoregulation as handling "daily fluctuations in performance … fitness and fatigue, together with readiness from daily non-training stressors" ([PubMed 33520457](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33520457/)).
- APRE (load for later sets and next session set from reps achieved on a set to failure): ranked first for back squat and bench 1RM (SUCRA 93-97%), though squat differences were not moderate or large ([Huang 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40791980/)). Original: Mann et al. 2010, college athletes ([PubMed 20543732](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20543732/)).
- Deload consensus lists "when performance stalled" as a common reactive trigger. In the Rogerson/Bell 2024 survey, athletes deloaded "when performance stalled or during periods of increased muscle soreness or joint aches." ([PubMed 38499934](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38499934/))

### Inferences
**Generator defaults (all practice-based; label them as such in the UI or docs):**
- **One session with missed reps** (reps below target or RIR worse than target by ≥2): **hold the load** next session. One bad day is within normal day-to-day variation, and RIR calls are off by ~1 rep anyway.
- **Second consecutive miss at the same load:** **reduce ~5%** (rounded to the plate step) on that lift only, or keep the load and drop reps by 1-2 for one session. The reduction is lift-specific, not whole-program.
- **Third stall, or two resets of the same lift within ~6 weeks:** treat it as a stall, not bad luck. Change the progression cadence (session → weekly → block), switch the rep target (e.g. 5s → 3s or 8s), or schedule a reactive deload (section 6). Larger resets (~10%) are typical of novice linear programs; they are convention, not evidence.
- A miss caused by an external factor the user reports (poor sleep, illness, short session) should not count toward the stall counter. Autoregulation is meant to absorb these.
- Never progress load when the lifter beat the rep target but reported RIR 0 or a grinder on the last set. Hold until the target is met at ≥ target RIR.
- **Ask the user, post-set or post-session:** "Did you get all reps? How many left in the tank?" Optionally ask about the reason for a miss (sleep / stress / sick / just heavy) so the generator can decide whether to count it.

### Gaps
- No controlled evidence on reset size (5% vs 10%), on how many failed sessions should trigger a reset, or on whether resets beat simply holding. Recommend logging outcomes in-app so these defaults can be revisited.
- The APRE rep-to-load adjustment table was not verified (see section 2).

## 6. Deloads: definitions, Delphi consensus, surveys, planned vs reactive

### Takeaway
Deloads are near-universal in practice (every ~4-6 weeks for ~1 week), but there is almost no outcome evidence that they improve gains. The only RCT in trained lifters, which deloaded by fully stopping training for one week, found **slightly worse** lower-body strength and no hypertrophy benefit. A 2026 within-subject trial in untrained men found that reduced-volume deloads did not hinder gains. Expert consensus allows both planned and autoregulated (reactive) deloads, with neither shown superior. "Always deload every 4th week" is folklore-grade. **Grade: preference/practice (frequency, structure); weak (RCT effects: neutral to slightly negative for strength when the deload is full cessation).**

### Cited Findings
- Bell et al. 2023 Delphi (34 → 29 → 21 expert strength and physique coaches over 3 rounds, 138 statements). Consensus definition: "Deloading is a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue, promote recovery, and enhance preparedness for subsequent training." [Sports Med Open 9:87, doi:10.1186/s40798-023-00633-0](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37730925/)
  - Round 3 agreement of 100% that a deload could be a reduction in overall demand, intensity, volume, frequency or proximity to failure. "Deloading could be pre-planned" also reached 100%. "Deload frequency may depend on how athlete responds to training stimulus" reached 100%. Deloads "could be introduced between mesocycles" reached 100%, and timing at beginning, middle or end of a mesocycle reached 76.2%. Panelists agreed deloads could be pre-planned or "utilized when athletes felt physically or mentally fatigued, regardless of where they were within their current mesocycle." [Full text, Europe PMC PMC10511399](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC10511399)
  - Background in the paper: "Deloading is generally undertaken every 4–6 weeks for a period of ~7 days, although some deloads might range in duration from a singular training session to 2 weeks" (citing Bell 2022 coach interviews). The authors also state "there is limited evidence to suggest that pre-planned, periodized training is superior to non-periodized training," and that the resensitization hypothesis is unstudied. "Further research is required" before autoregulated deloads can be said to replace planned ones. [Europe PMC PMC10511399](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC10511399)
- Rogerson, Bell et al. 2024 survey (n=246 competitive strength and physique athletes, 8.2 ± 6.2 yr of training): all athletes deloaded. Typical deloads lasted **6.4 ± 1.7 days every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks**, planned proactively or combined with autoregulation, and taken when performance stalled or with soreness or joint aches. Athletes reduced volume (reps per set and sets per week), reduced load, and increased RIR. Frequency and exercise selection were kept. Sports Med Open 10:26, doi:10.1186/s40798-024-00691-y. [PubMed 38499934](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38499934/)
- De Marco et al. 2024 (n=204 S&C coaches, mostly elite) on planned deloading periods (taper, season, travel): typical weekly prescription was 1-2 sessions of 30-60 min, 1-3 sets of 1-6 reps at 60-84% 1RM. The most common volume reduction was **0-25%**, and about half also cut intensity by 0-25%. JSCR 38(12):2099-2106, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000004932. [PubMed 39446750](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39446750/)
- Coleman et al. 2024 RCT (39 trained men and women, ~3.5 yr of experience; 9-wk high-volume program, with the DELOAD group **abstaining entirely** in week 5): no difference in lower-body hypertrophy, endurance or power. **TRAD showed greater isometric and dynamic lower-body strength gains**, and slightly better readiness-to-train scores. Posterior probabilities favoring the deload were 0.273-0.835. PeerJ 12:e16777, doi:10.7717/peerj.16777. [PubMed 38274324](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/)
- Pancar et al. 2026 (19 untrained men, within-subject limb design, 8 wk; deload weeks 4 and 8 cut to 1x/wk and 2 sets): no time × condition interaction for muscle thickness or 10RM. Reduced-volume deloads "do[] not appear to hinder adaptations." Sci Rep 16:10299, doi:10.1038/s41598-026-40612-5. [PubMed 41730991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41730991/)
- Ogasawara 2013 (14 young men, bench 3x/wk): 3-week breaks after every 6 weeks of training over 24 weeks gave the same overall hypertrophy and strength as continuous training. Eur J Appl Physiol 113:975-985, doi:10.1007/s00421-012-2511-9. [PubMed 23053130](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23053130/)
- Taper analogue: Burke et al. 2023 (12 powerlifters): a 1-week step taper followed by 2-4 days of full rest maintained or slightly improved 1RMs (total g = 0.11 after 2 days off). JSCR 37(12):e625-e632, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000004564. [PubMed 37639652](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37639652/)

### Inferences
- **Generator default:** do **not** hard-schedule a deload every 4th week for everyone. Instead:
  - **Novices:** no planned deloads. Progress is limited more by technique and load progression than by accumulated fatigue, and no evidence supports deloading them. Deload reactively only.
  - **Intermediate and advanced:** offer an *optional* planned light week every ~5-8 weeks (matching the 5.6 ± 2.3 wk survey mean), plus **reactive triggers**: a stall on ≥2 lifts (section 5), a self-reported fatigue or joint-ache flag for ≥2 sessions, or a run of RIR consistently worse than target at unchanged loads.
- **Deload structure default:** keep frequency and exercises. Cut sets by ~30-50% (e.g. 4 → 2), cut load by ~10% or add 2-3 RIR, and run it for one week (~7 days). This matches the survey and Delphi, and the Pancar result supports it. Coleman's result suggests **a deload should be a light week, not a week off**, for strength-goal users.
- If the user wants a full week off, it's fine. Coleman's strength cost was modest and posterior probabilities overlapped, and Ogasawara and Halonen show rapid recovery. Restart per section 7.
- **Ask the user:** "Do you want planned light weeks or only when you need one?" (Delphi: both valid). Also offer a recurring quick check: "Joints/energy OK?"

### Gaps
- No RCT compares planned vs reactive deloads, or deload frequencies (every 4 vs 6 vs 8 weeks). No trial tests deloads in novices beyond the Pancar untrained study.
- The specific Delphi wording on volume-reduction magnitude was not given as a number. Panelists agreed only that volume "could be reduced" and that a "minimum effective dose could be used."

## 7. Detraining, retraining ("muscle memory") and restarting after a layoff

### Takeaway
Maximal strength is largely retained for 2-4 weeks off in younger trained people, and losses accelerate after ~4 weeks. Older adults (>65) and inactive or new trainees lose more and sooner. Muscle size declines non-significantly over the first ~3 weeks. Lost strength and size come back faster than they were first built, and periodic training with breaks ends up equal to continuous training. Strength and size can be maintained for months on 1 session/week of 1 set/exercise if intensity (relative load) stays high. **Grade: moderate-strong (cessation effects and dose-response with duration; age moderator); moderate (rapid regain); moderate (minimal maintenance dose, mostly young adults).**

### Cited Findings
- Bosquet et al. 2013 meta-analysis (103 studies): training cessation reduced submaximal strength (SMD -0.62), maximal force (-0.46) and maximal power (-0.20). Losses scaled with cessation duration (dose-response). They were larger in people >65 y, and larger in inactive people than in recreational athletes for maximal force and power. Scand J Med Sci Sports 23(3):e140-9, doi:10.1111/sms.12047. [PubMed 23347054](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347054/)
  - Secondary summary of Bosquet (Stronger by Science): maximal strength "mostly unaffected (pooled effect sizes were trivial; g < 0.2) following up to 28 days," with losses "accelerat[ing] after 28 days." By age, older adults g = 0.76 vs younger g = 0.31. Strength-endurance is lost sooner than max strength. [Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/detraining/) (secondary; the week breakdown was not verified against the primary paper)
- McMaster 2013 (elite football codes): "Strength levels can be maintained for up to 3 weeks of detraining, but decay rates will increase thereafter (i.e. 5-16 weeks)." Mean −14.5% strength (ES −0.64) over 7.2 ± 5.8 weeks of detraining. [PubMed 23529287](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23529287/)
- Hwang et al. 2017 (20 trained men): leg-press strength showed **no decrease after 2 weeks** of detraining. Lean mass was retained, though not statistically tested as different. JSCR 31(4):869-881, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000001807. [PubMed 28328712](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28328712/)
- Halonen et al. 2024 RCT (55 untrained adults age ~32, 45% female): 10 wk training → 10 wk off → 10 wk retraining vs 20 wk continuous. 1RM and CSA dropped during the break but were "regained rapidly." Early retraining gains outpaced the continuous group, and both groups ended with similar adaptations. "Trainees should not be too concerned about occasional short-term training breaks." Scand J Med Sci Sports 34(10):e14739, doi:10.1111/sms.14739. [PubMed 39364857](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39364857/)
- Hulmi et al. 2025 (same design, muscle proteomics): many RT-induced proteins reverted during 10 wk off, but some, including the epigenetic muscle-memory gene CAPN2, stayed elevated. This suggests a "proteomic memory." Mechanistic only. J Physiol 603(9):2655-2673, doi:10.1113/JP288104. [PubMed 40183698](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40183698/)
- Ogasawara 2013: 3-week breaks every 6 weeks matched continuous training over 24 weeks. [PubMed 23053130](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23053130/)
- Retraining-time examples (secondary, via Stronger by Science): Taaffe & Marcus 1997, 12 wk off then 6 wk retraining to regain strength; Henwood & Taaffe 2008, 24 wk off then 12 wk retraining. The author's heuristic is that regain takes about half the layoff length (1/3-2/3), which is an opinion. [Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/detraining/)
- Older adults, muscle size (Grgic 2022, 6 studies): size loss after cessation d = -0.83 overall. It was non-significant at 12-24 weeks off (d = -0.60, CI -1.21 to 0.01) and significant at 31-52 weeks (d = -1.11). Int J Environ Res Public Health 19(21):14048, doi:10.3390/ijerph192114048. [PubMed 36360927](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36360927/)
- Middle-aged and older adults, strength (Yang 2022, 15 RCTs, n=383): with ≥24 weeks of prior training and a detraining period no longer than the training period, gains were retained even at moderate or low intensity (SMD 1.16). When training lasted <24 weeks, only high-intensity groups retained gains. When detraining exceeded training duration, gains were not maintained. J Aging Phys Act 30(3):552-566, doi:10.1123/japa.2020-0493. [PubMed 34510028](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34510028/)
- Maintenance dose (Spiering et al. 2021 narrative review): "Strength and muscle size (at least in younger populations) can be maintained for up to 32 weeks with as little as 1 session of strength training per week and 1 set per exercise, as long as exercise intensity (relative load) is maintained." Older adults may need 2 sessions/wk with 2-3 sets. JSCR 35(5):1449-1458, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000003964. [PubMed 33629972](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/)

### Inferences
**Generator restart defaults by days since the last session for that lift (inference; the thresholds are a synthesis of the above):**
- **≤10 days:** resume as planned. Hold load (don't progress) for the first session back if the gap was >7 days.
- **11-21 days:** first session back at **~90-95%** of the last working load, or the same load with +1-2 RIR. Return to the previous load within ~1-2 sessions if RIR comes in on target.
- **3-8 weeks:** start at **~80-90%** of the last working load with reduced sets (about two thirds). Re-ramp over roughly half the layoff length, using the logged RIR to accelerate.
- **>8 weeks, or any length for users >65 or with <6 months of prior training:** re-seed from a fresh estimate (submaximal test set or RIR-calibrated set). Start around ~70-80% of the previous load and treat the user as a "returning novice" with faster session-to-session progression until previous bests are approached (rapid-regain evidence: Halonen, Ogasawara).
- Also adjust volume on return, not just load. The Delphi and the survey treat volume as the main fatigue lever, and soreness after layoffs is a practical issue (inference).
- **Missed single sessions** within an otherwise regular week: do not "double up" or skip ahead. Shift the schedule (do the next planned session). Short breaks don't matter for long-term outcomes (Halonen; Hwang).
- **Busy periods:** offer a "maintenance mode" of 1 session/week, 1-2 hard sets per lift at normal relative load. Older users get 2 sessions/week with 2-3 sets (Spiering).
- **Ask the user:** age band; reason for the layoff (illness or injury vs schedule; illness or injury warrants a more conservative restart, practice-based); whether they trained anything else during the break; and how the first session back felt (RIR).

### Gaps
- No controlled trial tests specific restart percentages. The 90/85/75% figures are synthesis, not evidence.
- The week-by-week Bosquet breakdown came from a secondary summary. The primary full text was not retrieved.
- Hypertrophy-loss time course in young trained lifters relies on a few small studies (Psilander 2019, Seaborne 2018, Bjørnsen 2019, per secondary source). Not verified here.
- No direct evidence on how a layoff caused by illness differs from a scheduled break.

## 8. Folklore check

### Takeaway
Several popular rules are not supported, or are contradicted, by the evidence and should not be hard-coded into a generator.

### Cited Findings
- **"Add 5 lb every session forever."** Contradicted. Strength follows a linear-log curve that plateaus practically within ~1-2 years on a fixed minimal-dose program ([Steele 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35591809/)). Elite athletes gain ~0.42%/session and 7-12% per year ([McMaster 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23529287/)). The strongest lifters progress at about half the rate of the weakest ([Latella 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32865942/)). Bench gains about half the absolute kilos of squat or deadlift ([Latella 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35019902/)), so a fixed 5 lb step is wrong for upper vs lower lifts.
- **"Always deload every 4th week."** Not evidence-based. The 4-6 week figure is descriptive of practice ([Bell 2023 Delphi](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC10511399); survey mean 5.6 ± 2.3 wk, [Rogerson 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38499934/)). Coaches agreed frequency "may depend on how athlete responds" (100%). The only trained-lifter RCT found a week off slightly *reduced* strength gains ([Coleman 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/)).
- **"Muscle confusion" (constantly varying exercises).** Contradicted as stated. Kassiano et al. 2022 systematic review (8 studies, n=241 young men): "some degree of systematic variation" can help regional hypertrophy and dynamic strength, but "excessive, random variation may compromise muscular gains." Frequent rotation "may actually hinder muscular adaptations." JSCR 36(6):1753-1762, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000004258. [PubMed 35438660](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/). ACSM 2026: insufficient data that varied exercise selection affects strength ([Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)). Undulating load and reps on the *same* exercise (UP) differs from rotating exercises and has modest support in trained lifters ([Moesgaard 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/)).
- **"You lose gains after a week off."** Contradicted. There was no strength loss after 2 weeks in trained men ([Hwang 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28328712/)); strength was maintained up to ~3 weeks ([McMaster 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23529287/)); losses were trivial up to ~28 days in the Bosquet meta-analysis per the secondary summary ([SBS](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/detraining/)). Periodic training with 3- to 10-week breaks reached the same end-point as continuous training ([Ogasawara 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23053130/); [Halonen 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39364857/)). Caveat: Coleman 2024 found a small strength penalty from a mid-program week off in trained lifters ([PubMed 38274324](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/)), so "no effect" is also too strong. It is small and recoverable.
- **"Periodization is essential" / "you must use block or DUP."** Overstated. ACSM 2026: "periodization is less important than previously hypothesized." [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)
- **"You must train to failure to get stronger."** Contradicted for strength. [Robinson 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/); ACSM 2026.
- **"Beginners can't use RIR."** Not supported. Accuracy did not depend on training status ([Halperin 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/); [Remmert 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37036795/)). Everyone underestimates by ~1 rep, and accuracy is poor on high-rep sets.

### Inferences
- The generator's copy and logic should avoid promising linear progress, avoid default 4-week deload cycles, avoid random exercise rotation, and avoid alarm about short breaks. These are also good spots for brief, restrained "why" text in the app.

### Gaps
- No direct trial of "add 5 lb every session" novice linear progression vs alternatives was found. The rebuttal rests on long-term growth data, not head-to-head RCTs.
