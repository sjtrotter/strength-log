Sources were read in full text where marked "(full text)"; otherwise the abstract was read through Europe PMC. Verdict key: **KEEP** (number holds against the source named), **CHANGE TO** (a different value has a source), **LABEL AS PRACTICE** (no study tests it; it is a coaching or product choice and should be called that).

## 1. Weekly sets per muscle: dose-response, trained vs untrained, and the 0.5 credit for secondary muscles

### Takeaway
Pelland 2026 (full preprint read) supports counting indirect sets as 0.5. It does **not** report a plateau at 18–20 weekly sets. For hypertrophy it finds diminishing returns with no clear plateau, and very few studies above about 25 fractional sets. For strength it finds a reciprocal curve: the smallest detectable gain at 1 fractional set a week, detectable steps up to about 4, and nothing consistently detectable past 5. The "plateau near 18–20" is ACSM 2026's paraphrase of Pelland, not Pelland's own result.

### Cited Findings
**Pelland et al. 2026, Sports Med 56(2):481–505, doi 10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w (PMID 41343037); preprint full text, SportRxiv 460**
- 67 studies, 2,058 participants (79.1% male, mean age 25.16 ± 5.22 y); 28 studies untrained, 39 trained; interventions 10.42 ± 4.48 weeks. Every model was adjusted for training status and intervention length. — [Pelland 2026 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/); [preprint PDF](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460/967)
- Set classification: for hypertrophy, a set is "direct" when the measured muscle is "likely to be the primary force generator" and "indirect" when it is "meaningfully trained but not the primary force generator (i.e., synergist)". Example: 5 sets of curls plus 5 sets of rows count as 10 'total', 7.5 'fractional' and 5 'direct' biceps sets. For strength, "direct" means only the exact test exercise, and every other set training the involved muscles is indirect (5 squat + 5 squat + 5 leg press = 12.5 fractional squat sets). The authors call the classification "not wholly objective". — [preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460/967)
- Model comparison (2 × log Bayes factor), fractional vs total and vs direct: strength volume 9.96 and 10.82; hypertrophy volume 9.48 and 10.29; strength frequency 31.27 and 54.84 (hypertrophy frequency: fractional beat total by 18.21 and direct by 45.96). "Strong" to "very strong" evidence for fractional counting. — [preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460/967)
- Hypertrophy (35 studies, 220 effects, 1,032 participants): best fit was a square-root model, marginal slope +0.24% per set (95% CrI 0.15–0.33%) at the mean fractional volume of 12.25 sets. Schoenfeld 2017's linear estimate was 0.38% per set. — [preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460/967)
- Hypertrophy efficiency tiers (smallest detectable effect 2.05%), in fractional weekly sets: minimum effective dose **4**; higher efficiency **5–10** (about 6 more sets per detectable step); intermediate **11–18** (about 8.5); lower **19–29** (about 10.75); lowest **30–42** (about 12.5); 43+ unclear. The authors: the tiers "indicate no clear plateau", and "caution is warranted as few studies have explored ~25+ 'fractional' weekly sets". — [preprint Table 2A and §5](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460/967)
- Strength (66 studies, 490 effects, 2,020 participants): best fit was a reciprocal model with "strong diminishing returns and a functional plateau", slope +0.21% per set (95% CrI 0.16–0.26%). Tiers (smallest detectable effect 3.96%): minimum effective dose **1** fractional weekly set; **2** (about 0.75 more set per step); **3–4** (about 2.25); **5+**: "additional weekly sets do not consistently enhance strength gains > SDES". The authors note a 3.96% threshold may exceed what some call relevant, so sets past 4 may still add gains below detection. — [preprint Table 2B and §5.5](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460/967)
- Pelland suspects learning effects on unfamiliar strength tests inflate the low-dose strength effect. It also cites well-trained powerlifters, for whom 1–3 direct weekly sets were insufficient but 3–9 were. — [preprint §5.5](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460/967)
- Median doses in the pooled training groups: 10.5 fractional sets a week for hypertrophy and 6 for strength, at 2 sessions a week. About 78% of effects used some definition of failure. — [preprint §4.3](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460/967)
- Frequency: the effect on hypertrophy is "compatible with negligible effects". Strength rises with frequency with diminishing returns, from fractional frequency 1 (12.72%) to 2 (17.32%), with "accelerating diminishing returns" beyond. — [Pelland 2026 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/); [preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460/967)

**ACSM 2026 position stand (Currier et al., MSSE, doi 10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897, PMC12965823), full text**
- "A meta-regression (187) [= Pelland] ... showed ... a dose-response that plateaued and showed diminishing returns beyond ~2–3 sets/exercise for strength and ~18–20 weekly sets for hypertrophy ... thus, healthy adults are advised to complete at least two sets per exercise." Its summary table puts strength volume at "2–3 sets/session" with "≥2 sessions/wk", and hypertrophy volume at "≥10 sets/wk (dose-response)", or "≥10 sets/muscle group/wk" in the text. — [Currier 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)
- So the 18–20 figure is ACSM's reading. Pelland's own text and tables do not say it (above).

**Schoenfeld et al. 2017, J Sports Sci 35(11):1073–1082, doi 10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197 (PMID 27433992)**
- 15 studies, 34 treatment groups. Each added weekly set raised ES by 0.023, or 0.37% muscle gain. Higher vs lower volume within study: ES difference 0.241 (3.9% more gain). The three categories (<5, 5–9, 10+ sets per muscle) showed only a trend (p = 0.074). — [Schoenfeld 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/)
- Pelland reports Schoenfeld's split as ≥9 vs <9 sets per muscle per week: ES 0.46 (0.21–0.71) vs 0.32 (0.19–0.46), p = 0.076. — [Pelland preprint intro](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460/967)

**Baz-Valle et al. 2022, J Hum Kinet 81:199–210, doi 10.2478/hukin-2022-0017 (PMC8884877)**
- 7 RCTs (6 meta-analyzed). Participants were trained (≥1 year), aged 18–35, mostly men. Volume groups: low <12, moderate 12–20, high >20 weekly sets. Moderate and high did not differ for quadriceps (p = 0.19) or biceps (p = 0.59); high was better for triceps (p = 0.01; SMD −0.50, 95% CI −0.88 to −0.11 per Pelland). Conclusion: "12–20 weekly sets per muscle group may be an optimum standard recommendation ... in young, trained men." — [Baz-Valle 2022](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC8884877)

**Ralston et al. 2017, Sports Med 47(12):2585–2601, doi 10.1007/s40279-017-0762-7 (PMC5684266), full text**
- 9 studies, 61 treatment groups. Sets are counted **per exercise** per week: low ≤5, medium 5–9, high ≥10. High beat low (ES 0.18, 95% CI 0.06–0.30); medium beat low by a margin (ES 0.15, 0.01–0.30). For novice and intermediate men, the authors say low weekly sets "do not lead to strength gains compared with MWS or HWS". — [Ralston 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC5684266)

**Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2020, Sports Med 50(4):751–765, doi 10.1007/s40279-019-01236-0 (PMID 31797219)**
- 6 studies of resistance-trained men. One set of 6–12 reps at about 70–85% 1RM, 2–3 times a week, taken to volitional or momentary failure for 8–12 weeks, raised 1RM by 12.09 kg overall (95% CI 8.16–16.03), squat by 17.48 kg (8.51–26.46) and bench by 8.25 kg (0.68–15.83). The authors call these gains "suboptimal, yet significant". Not shown for the deadlift, trained women or highly trained strength athletes. — [Androulakis-Korakakis 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31797219/)

### Inferences
- **L70, secondary muscle = 0.5 set: KEEP.** Pelland's fractional method is the best-fitting of the three it tested, by Bayes factors in the "strong" range. Two caveats belong in the doc. First, 0.5 was the only fraction tested (0, 0.5 or 1), not a value fitted from the data. Second, Pelland's "direct" for strength means the exact test lift, so the 0.5 rule fits the app's per-muscle hypertrophy counting better than its strength floors. Ramps not counting fits Pelland, whose sets are working sets (about 78% to failure).
- **L68, cap 20 weekly sets: KEEP 20, LABEL AS PRACTICE, and fix the citation.** No source shows a plateau at 20. Pelland shows returns falling past about 18 (19–29 is its "lower efficiency" tier) but no ceiling. Baz-Valle found no gain from >20 over 12–20 in quads and biceps of young trained men, but did for triceps. Cite it as "past about 20, each detectable gain needs about 11 more weekly sets (Pelland tier 19–29), and 12–20 matched >20 for most muscles (Baz-Valle 2022)". It is an efficiency and time ceiling, not a plateau. Copy should not say "plateau at 18–20".
- **L65, Muscle floor 10 (New, Under a year): KEEP.** It matches ACSM 2026's "≥10 sets/muscle group/wk". Pelland puts 10 at the top of its "higher efficiency" tier (5–10).
- **L66, Muscle floor 12 (A year or more): KEEP; cite Baz-Valle 2022** ("12–20 weekly sets ... young, trained men"). It sits inside Pelland's intermediate tier (11–18).
- **L63, Strength floor 6 per floor muscle: KEEP, cite as derived.** ACSM's "2–3 sets/session" at "≥2 sessions/wk" gives 4–6 sets of the lift a week. Ralston: more than 5 weekly sets per exercise beat ≤5 (ES 0.18). Pelland: detectable strength steps up to about 4 fractional sets, with Pelland's pooled strength median at 6. Status can move from INVENTED to SUPPORTED, "derived from ACSM 2026 + Ralston 2017". Caveat: the evidence counts sets of the lift, and the app counts sets per muscle.
- **L64, General fitness floor 4: KEEP, cite Pelland's minimum effective dose.** Pelland's minimum effective dose for detectable hypertrophy is 4 fractional weekly sets, and for strength 1. It also matches ACSM's "at least two sets per exercise" on 2 days. The population caveat: Pelland's sample is young (mean 25 y) and 79% male.
- **L75, dose floor of one working set per main lift: KEEP, with an effort caveat.** Androulakis-Korakakis tested a single set *to failure*, 2–3 times a week. The app's floor set runs at 2–3 RIR, which is easier than anything tested. Pelland (MED = 1 fractional weekly set) gives a second source that does not depend on failure (though 78% of its effects used failure).
- **L76, dose-ladder order: LABEL AS PRACTICE.** Two findings fit the direction without testing the order. ACSM's "exercise order: beginning of training session" favors keeping the main lift. Pelland's steep low end for strength means the first sets of a lift are worth most, so cutting accessories first costs least. No source covers the 80% × 3 ramp or the 120 s rest cap.
- **L69, fill and trim mechanics (5-set slot cap): LABEL AS PRACTICE.** No source.
- **L71, muscle credit table: LABEL AS PRACTICE.** Pelland itself says its direct/indirect calls were "not wholly objective". The table is an anatomical convention under a supported counting rule.

### Gaps
- I could not read the published 2026 version of Pelland in full (Springer). The tier tables above come from the preprint (version 2, September 2024). The published abstract matches the preprint's abstract, but the tier numbers could have shifted in peer review. Worth checking before copy quotes "4 sets" or "19–29".
- No meta-analysis gives a weekly-set dose for women, adults over 40, or trained lifters above about 25 sets.
- Schoenfeld 2017 full text was not read. The <5 / 5–9 / 10+ category effect sizes are not quoted here.

## 2. Which muscle groups guidelines require, and whether floors of 6 / 4 / 10–12 hold

### Takeaway
WHO 2020, ACSM 2026 and the US guidelines all say "all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week" and give no weekly set number. ACSM 2026 says four movement regions (upper and lower body, push and pull), or six with horizontal and vertical upper-body pulls and pushes, are "sufficient to target major muscle groups". The US guidelines list the groups as legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders and arms. The app's floor list matches these except for **abdomen** (no floor) and **arms** (floored only on Muscle).

### Cited Findings
- WHO 2020, adults and older adults: "muscle-strengthening activities at moderate or greater intensity that involve all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week" (strong recommendation). — [Bull et al. 2020, BJSM, doi 10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955, PMC7719906](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC7719906)
- ACSM 2026: "applying the recommendations herein to the body regions 'upper' and 'lower' for 'push' and 'pull' exercises (i.e., four body regions) is sufficient to target major muscle groups. The movement directions 'horizontal' and 'vertical' may also be considered for upper body exercises (i.e., six body regions)." It also says "the available evidence did not compare distinct body regions or muscle groups". Primary recommendation: high-effort RT "at least twice weekly, with all major muscle groups being engaged". — [Currier 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)
- US Physical Activity Guidelines, 2nd edition (HHS 2018): "It is important to work all the major muscle groups of the body—the legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders, and arms." — [PAG 2nd ed., p. 72 region](https://health.gov/sites/default/files/2019-09/Physical_Activity_Guidelines_2nd_edition.pdf)
- None of the guideline documents gives a weekly set count per muscle for health. ACSM's numbers (2–3 sets per exercise per session, ≥10 weekly sets for hypertrophy) are goal-specific. — [Currier 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)

### Inferences
- **L67, floor muscles: LABEL AS PRACTICE, with one CHANGE to consider.** The list (quads, hamstrings, glutes, chest, lats, upper back, front delts, plus biceps and triceps on Muscle) covers ACSM's four or six regions and the US list's legs, hips, back, chest and shoulders. No guideline specifies muscles below that level, so the exact set is a design choice. Two gaps against the US list:
  - **Abdomen.** Core has no floor, though L19 already gives a 2-set core slot every lift day.
  - **Arms.** On Strength and General, arms are covered only through secondary credit from presses and pulls. At 0.5 a set, that probably meets any health-level dose anyway.
  - Neither needs a floor to meet WHO, because the guidance names groups and gives no dose. If the doc wants to claim "all major muscle groups (US PAG list)", core should count as covered by L19.
- **Floors 6 / 4 / 10–12:** see section 1. All four numbers can be tied to a source (ACSM + Ralston, Pelland MED, ACSM, Baz-Valle). None is a guideline requirement. They are goal-specific defaults.

### Gaps
- No trial compares per-muscle floors with per-pattern coverage for health outcomes.

## 3. Detraining: how fast strength and size go, and what restart loads the evidence supports

### Takeaway
In young adults, both trained and untrained, maximal strength holds through about 2–3 weeks off. Losses grow with time off past about 3–4 weeks, averaging about 14.5% across 5–16-week layoffs in elite football players. In older adults, two trials found training gains held through 12 weeks off, while one meta-analysis says losses are larger after 65. Regain is fast in every population studied. No study tests restart loads, so every restart factor is practice. The app's adult bands are conservative but defensible. The 65+ rule (× 0.75 and recalibrate after 14 days) is far more cautious than any measured loss.

### Cited Findings
- **Bosquet et al. 2013**, Scand J Med Sci Sports 23(3):e140–e149, doi 10.1111/sms.12047. 103 studies. Cessation effects: submaximal strength SMD −0.62 (−0.80 to −0.45), maximal force −0.46 (−0.54 to −0.37), maximal power −0.20 (−0.28 to −0.13). There was "a dose-response relationship between the amplitude of SMD and the duration of training cessation". The effect "was found to be larger in older people (> 65 years old)" and larger in inactive people than in recreational athletes for maximal force and power. — [Bosquet 2013 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347054/)
- Bosquet, figures from the secondary summary only: maximal strength "mostly unaffected ... up to 28 days (g < 0.2)" and "losses accelerated after 28 days". By age, older (≥65) g = 0.76 (0.62–0.90) vs younger g = 0.31 (0.21–0.40) for maximal strength; 0.46 vs 0.18 for power; 0.85 vs 0.48 for strength endurance. — [Stronger by Science, secondary](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/detraining/). **Still [verify]: the full text is paywalled (Unpaywall: no open-access copy), and neither the 28-day claim nor the age effect sizes appear in the abstract.**
- **Hwang et al. 2017**, JSCR 31(4):869–881, doi 10.1519/JSC.0000000000001807. 20 resistance-trained men aged about 21: 4 weeks of 4-day-a-week training, 2 weeks off, then 4 weeks of retraining. "No decrease in LPS [leg press strength] after DT in both groups"; lean mass retained (not significant). — [Hwang 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28328712/)
- **McMaster et al. 2013**, Sports Med 43(5):367–384, doi 10.1007/s40279-013-0031-3. 27 studies, n = 1,015 elite rugby union, rugby league and American football players. Strength fell a mean **14.5% (ES −0.64)** over detraining periods of 7.2 ± 5.8 weeks, and power 0.4% (ES −0.10) over 7.6 ± 5.1 weeks. "Strength levels can be maintained for up to 3 weeks of detraining, but decay rates will increase thereafter (i.e. 5–16 weeks)." — [McMaster 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23529287/)
- **Halonen et al. 2024**, Scand J Med Sci Sports 34(10):e14739, doi 10.1111/sms.14739. 55 untrained adults (age 32 ± 5, 45% women) trained full body twice a week. Periodic group: 10 weeks training, 10 off, 10 training; continuous group: 20 weeks. Leg press and biceps curl 1RM, CMJ, and vastus lateralis and biceps CSA all fell during the 10 weeks off (p < 0.05). In the first 5 weeks of retraining, gains in leg press 1RM and in VL and biceps CSA outpaced the continuous group's (p < 0.01), and the groups ended with "similar postintervention adaptations". The abstract gives no percent loss. — [Halonen 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39364857/)
- **Ogasawara et al. 2013**, Eur J Appl Physiol, PMID 23053130. 14 young men, bench press at 75% 1RM, 3 × 10, 3 days a week. Three cycles of 6 weeks training with 3 weeks off produced the same CSA and 1RM gains as 24 weeks of continuous training. — [Ogasawara 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23053130/)
- **Lemmer et al. 2000**, MSSE 32(8):1505–1512, doi 10.1097/00005768-200008000-00021. 18 young (20–30) and 23 older (65–75) adults; 9 weeks of unilateral knee-extension training, 3 days a week, then 31 weeks off. Gains were "maintained equally well in young and older men and women during 12 wk of detraining". By 31 weeks, 1RM had fallen 8 ± 2% (young) vs 14 ± 2% (older), mostly between weeks 12 and 31. — [Lemmer 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10949019/)
- **Taaffe et al. 2009**, Gerontology, doi 10.1159/000182084 (PMC2756799). 13 adults aged 65–83 trained twice a week. Strength changed +48.8 ± 2.9% after 24 weeks of training, −17.6 ± 1.3% after 24 weeks off, and +19.8 ± 2.0% after 12 weeks of retraining. — [Taaffe 2009](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC2756799)
- **Yang et al. 2022**, J Aging Phys Act 30(3):552–566, doi 10.1123/japa.2020-0493. 15 RCTs, n = 383 middle-aged and older adults. When training lasted under 24 weeks and the layoff was no longer than the training, only high-intensity groups kept their gains: SMD 0.71 (0.34–1.08) after 4–6 weeks off, 1.35 (0.66–2.04) after 8–16 weeks. Gains were not kept when the layoff was longer than the training. — [Yang 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34510028/)
- Muscle size in older adults: 6 studies with layoffs of 12–52 weeks. After 12–24 weeks off, the size loss was not significant (d −0.60, 95% CI −1.21 to 0.01); after 31–52 weeks it was (d −1.11, −1.75 to −0.47). — [IJERPH 2022, PMC9657634](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC9657634)
- Restart loads: I found no trial comparing restart loads after a layoff. Every study above retrained on its original protocol. The first review's restart table is labeled "synthesis, not evidence". — [first review report §restart](../../wizard-evidence-2026-11-08/report.md)

### Inferences
- **L58, recurrence offset: LABEL AS PRACTICE.** Every study measures time since the last session. The offset means a lift trained once a week enters "layoff 14" at 21 days since its last session, which is still inside the no-loss window (Hwang 2 weeks, McMaster 3 weeks, Ogasawara 3 weeks). So the offset does no harm. The doc should say bands count from the expected next session, not from the last one.
- **L59, adult day bands (≤3 normal; 4–7 hold; 8–14 × 0.95; 15–42 × 0.85 with fewer sets; > 42 recalibrate): LABEL AS PRACTICE.**
  - Up to about 3 weeks since the last session, the evidence predicts no strength loss. So "hold" and × 0.95 are cheap insurance, not a response to measured loss.
  - × 0.85 for 15–42 days past recurrence (about 3–7 weeks since the last session for a once-weekly lift) lines up with McMaster's mean 14.5% loss over about 7 weeks, an upper bound for that window.
  - The 6-week vs 8-week recalibration threshold: the evidence cannot tell them apart (McMaster's decay window is 5–16 weeks). Either is defensible. Keep 42 days past recurrence (about 7 weeks since the last session for a once-weekly lift), and note that it is close to the review's 8 weeks once the offset is added.
- **L60, factors (× 0.95; × 0.85 with ⌈⅔⌉ sets; × 0.75 and calibrating): LABEL AS PRACTICE.** × 0.85 can cite McMaster 2013 as "about the average loss after 1–2 months off, elite young athletes". × 0.75 exceeds every measured loss, even after 24–31 weeks in older adults (−14% Lemmer, −17.6% Taaffe). That is fine because calibration corrects it upward.
- **L61, return to the pre-layoff load after one on-target session: KEEP (PRACTICE).** Fast regain is well supported: Halonen's retraining gains outpaced continuous training, Ogasawara's 3-week breaks cost nothing, and Taaffe regained everything in 12 weeks after 24 off. The "one session" is practice.
- **O10, 65+ → × 0.75 and recalibrate after 14 days: CHANGE TO a 65+ path that is one step more cautious than adults, not a full recalibration at 15 days.** For example, × 0.85 with fewer sets from 15 days past recurrence, and × 0.75 and recalibrate past 42 days. Sources: Lemmer 2000 (gains held equally in 65–75-year-olds through 12 weeks off), Yang 2022 (high-intensity gains held at 4–6 weeks off), Taaffe 2009 (−17.6% only after 24 weeks off). Bosquet 2013 supports some extra caution after 65, but its size is unverified. If the owner wants the stricter rule for safety or confidence, keep it and **LABEL AS PRACTICE**. The detraining data do not require it.

### Gaps
- Bosquet 2013's duration bands and age effect sizes remain unverified (paywalled).
- No restart-load trial exists in any population.
- Older adults with short layoffs (2–6 weeks) after *long-term* training are not studied. The trials above detrained people after 9–24 weeks of supervised training.
- Illness or injury layoffs (bed rest, immobilization) cause faster loss than voluntary cessation, but they fall outside these studies.

## 4. Older adults: balance dose and frequency, carry loads, intensity and RIR, sets

### Takeaway
WHO's 3+ days a week of balance-focused multicomponent activity is a strong recommendation. A balance block only on lifting days misses it on 2-day plans. The app's balance dose (2 × 30 s a side, about 2 minutes) is far below what effective trials used: about 3 sessions and 91–120 min a week (Lesinski 2015), or more than 3 hours a week of exercise that challenges balance (Sherrington 2017). Borde 2015 (full text read) confirms 2–3 sets per exercise, 7–9 reps, 70–79% 1RM and 2 sessions a week for strength in healthy adults 65+. I found no study of farmer's carry loads in older beginners. The 50 lb default has no basis.

### Cited Findings
- **WHO 2020:** older adults "should do varied multicomponent physical activity that emphasises functional balance and strength training at moderate or greater intensity on 3 or more days a week, to enhance functional capacity and to prevent falls" (strong recommendation). The supporting evidence: "High-certainty evidence demonstrates that balance and functional exercises reduce the rate of falls." Example given: "standing on one foot while doing an upper body muscle-strengthening activity, such as bicep curls." — [Bull et al. 2020](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC7719906)
- **US PAG 2018:** "Studies of fall prevention programs generally include about three sessions a week"; examples are heel-to-toe walking, sit-to-stand and wobble board. — [PAG 2nd ed.](https://health.gov/sites/default/files/2019-09/Physical_Activity_Guidelines_2nd_edition.pdf)
- **Sherrington et al. 2019, Cochrane** (CD012424.pub2, PMC6360922): 108 RCTs, 23,407 community-dwelling adults aged 60+ (mean 76, 77% women). All exercise cut the rate of falls by 23% (RaR 0.77, 0.71–0.83; high certainty). Balance and functional exercise cut it by 24% (RaR 0.76, 0.70–0.81; 39 studies, high certainty). Multiple types, commonly balance plus resistance, cut it by 34% (RaR 0.66, 0.50–0.88; moderate certainty). The effects of mainly-resistance programs are uncertain. — [Sherrington 2019](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC6360922); [abridged BJSM 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31792067/)
- **Sherrington et al. 2017**, BJSM, doi 10.1136/bjsports-2016-096547: 99 comparisons, 88 trials, 19,478 participants. Exercise cut falls by 21% (RR 0.79). There were "greater effects ... from exercise programmes that challenged balance and involved more than 3 hours/week". Together these explained 76% of between-trial heterogeneity, and combined they gave a 39% reduction (IRR 0.61, 0.53–0.72). — [Sherrington 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27707740/)
- **Lesinski et al. 2015**, Sports Med 45(12):1721–1738, doi 10.1007/s40279-015-0375-y (PMC4656699): 23 RCTs of healthy community-dwelling adults ≥65. The most effective doses were 11–12 weeks (SMD 1.26), **3 sessions a week** (SMD 1.20), 36–40 total sessions (1.39), 31–45 min a session (1.19) and **91–120 min a week** (1.93). Each was computed independently, the studies were of low quality (mean PEDro 5) and heterogeneity was high (I² 76–92%). Too few studies reported sets, reps or exercise duration to model those. — [Lesinski 2015](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC4656699)
- **Borde et al. 2015**, Sports Med 45(12):1693–1720, doi 10.1007/s40279-015-0385-9 (PMC4656698), full text: 25 RCTs, healthy adults with mean age ≥65. Training raised strength (SMD 1.57) much more than muscle size (0.42). Meta-regression: intensity 70–79% 1RM gave the largest strength effect (SMD 1.89), and training period 50–53 weeks gave 2.34. Independently computed strength doses: **2 sessions a week** (2.13), **2–3 sets per exercise** (2.99), **7–9 reps** (1.98), **4.0 s between reps** (3.72). Between-set rest of 60 s was best (4.68, two studies, p = 0.06). For muscle size, the best values were 3 sessions a week, 2–3 sets, 7–9 reps, 51–69% 1RM, 120 s rest between sets and 2.5 s between reps. Mean PEDro 4.6; I² = 80%. — [Borde 2015](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC4656698)
- **ACSM 2026:** there is "insufficient evidence to quantify exact RIR and perceived exertion targets". "Near-failure or a target of 2–3 RIR" gives sufficient effort. Its guidance covers healthy adults; it names older adults as a future subpopulation. — [Currier 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12965823)
- **Carries:** no trial prescribes farmer's carry loads for untrained older adults. One biomechanics study (31 community-dwelling older adults, 15 with recent falls) had them walk carrying a one-hand dumbbell of 2–10% of body mass. Dynamic balance (mediolateral margin of stability) was preserved at every load, with or without a fall history. — [Human Movement Science 2026, PMID 42413170](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42413170)
- The app's catalog entry is `farmers_carry ... time(40, 50.0)`, which is 40 s at 50 lb. The 65+ block runs it at 2 × 20 s. — `domain/.../library/ExerciseLibrary.kt:322`

### Inferences
- **O02, 6–10 reps at 3 RIR (about 70–77% 1RM): KEEP.** Borde: 70–79% is best for strength. The 3 RIR is practice (ACSM: no evidence for an exact RIR). Note Borde's 7–9 reps sits inside 6–10.
- **O03, 2 sets every slot: KEEP. The [verify] resolves:** Borde 2015 does say 2–3 sets per exercise, 7–9 reps and 2 sessions a week. The earlier recalled "4 to 6 s between reps" is wrong: it is 4.0 s (strength) or 2.5 s (size) between reps, and Borde found 60 s between sets best for strength (two studies only) and 120 s for size.
- **O05, 120 s rest: KEEP.** It matches Borde's best rest for muscle size. Borde's 60 s for strength rests on two studies.
- **O06, 65+ floor of 6: KEEP, now sourced.** Borde's 2–3 sets × 2 sessions = 4–6 sets of each exercise a week.
- **O04, no SECONDARY exposures: LABEL AS PRACTICE.** Borde's best frequency is 2 sessions a week, which full-body days on a 2-day plan already give each muscle. No source says to drop a second exposure of the same lift.
- **O07, balance only on lifting days: CHANGE TO balance on ≥3 days a week, whatever the lifting day count (WHO 2020, strong; PAG "about three sessions a week"; Lesinski 2015: 3 a week most effective).** The simplest fix is a short home balance item on non-lifting days when the plan has fewer than 3 lifting days. WHO counts multicomponent activity, so a run day with a balance block also counts.
- **O08, balance dose 2 × 30 s a side: LABEL AS PRACTICE, and record that it is a token dose.** Effective programs ran 31–45 min a session and 91–120 min a week (Lesinski), and the largest fall reductions came from more than 3 hours a week of balance-challenging exercise (Sherrington 2017). Static single-leg stance with no progression is unlikely to count as "challenging". Progression should come first: eyes closed, narrower stance, head turns, reaching, and tandem walk, matching the PAG and WHO examples. Matching trial minutes inside a lifting session may not be feasible. In that case, the app should not suggest the block meets the fall-prevention dose.
- **O09, carry at 50 lb for 2 × 20 s: CHANGE TO a light, self-calibrated opening (LABEL AS PRACTICE).** No source supports 50 lb. If it means 50 lb in each hand, that is about 32% of body mass per hand for a 70 kg person, more than three times the 10% top of the only older-adult loaded-walking data. Suggested default: open at about 10% of body mass per hand where the profile has body weight, otherwise at the lightest dumbbell pair that allows an easy walk. Then calibrate by effort like other lifts. 2 × 20 s is fine as practice. Also check whether the catalog's 50 lb means per hand or total; the code does not say.
